## 1. A significant result, and the paper's own analysis saying the data were never sufficient to find one

**[Preoperative cognitive training for preventing perioperative neurocognitive disorders: A systematic review
and meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis and meta-regression of randomised controlled
trials](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2026.112359)** — *Journal of Clinical Anesthesia*, volume 116,
article 112359, **first published 25 October 2026**. Paywalled at the publisher; abstract read through the
Europe PMC index.

**Thirteen randomised trials, 1,446 patients, comparing preoperative cognitive training with usual care.** The
abstract states its own premise plainly:

> **Although nonpharmacological interventions are recommended for preventing perioperative neurocognitive
> disorders (PND), cognitive training has not been explicitly advocated.**

**The headline results:**

- **Postoperative delirium: RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.36–0.77, P = 0.001, low certainty.**
- **Delayed neurocognitive recovery: RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.46–0.96, P = 0.03, very low certainty** — and the
  abstract adds that this finding **"was not supported by several sensitivity analyses"**.
- **Training format mattered: subgroup P for interaction = 0.01, meta-regression β1 1.072, 95% CI 0.006–2.138,
  P = 0.049, with "a potentially greater benefit for traditional training"** over digital.

**And then:**

> **TSA suggested a potential for false-positive findings, as the cumulative Z-curves did not cross the TSA
> boundaries, and the required information sizes were not reached for either outcome.**

**Read those two blocks together.** A 47% relative reduction in delirium at P = 0.001, and the paper's own
trial sequential analysis saying the accumulated information was never enough to exclude a false positive for
**either** primary outcome. **That is not a contradiction — it is what a well-conducted TSA is for — but it
inverts the usual direction of such a disclosure.** The conventional pattern is a null result with the
author noting insufficient power. **Here it is a positive result with the authors noting insufficient
information, which is the rarer and more useful admission.**

**The conclusion matches, which is to its credit:** POD **"may"** be reduced, and for DNR **"the current
evidence is insufficient to support a preventive effect."**

**This is the second consecutive day on which this archive has read a trial sequential analysis, and the two
point in opposite directions.** **Yesterday's transcranial electrical stimulation review stated that the
current sample size was sufficient to confirm benefit across three outcomes while its GRADE certainty ran very
low to moderate — ample data from biased studies.** **Today's review states that certainty is low and very
low, <i>and</i> that the information size was never reached — not enough data, and what there is is biased.**
**The pairing is instructive precisely because TSA and GRADE are answering different questions, which this
archive recorded as a rule yesterday: TSA asks whether there is enough data, GRADE asks how far it can be
trusted.** **Yesterday's paper scored high on one and low on the other. Today's scores low on both, and says
so.**

**The meta-regression finding deserves scepticism of a different kind.** **β1 1.072 with a confidence interval
running from 0.006 to 2.138 and P = 0.049 is a coefficient whose lower bound is 0.006** — that is, the
interval excludes zero by six thousandths. **A meta-regression across thirteen trials, partitioned into
traditional versus digital training, fitting a single moderator at the boundary of significance, is a
hypothesis and not a finding.** **The paper's own hedging — "potentially greater benefit", "a potential
moderator" — is proportionate, and the interval's lower bound makes the case better than any adjective
could.** Note also that **the DNR meta-regression found overall risk of bias to be a potential moderator at
P = 0.045**, which is the same boundary and the same caveat.

**My reading.** **The clinically interesting claim here is not the risk ratio; it is the format effect.** If
traditional cognitive training outperforms digital, that is a statement about how the intervention is
delivered rather than whether it works, and it is the kind of finding that determines whether a prehabilitation
programme is feasible in a real preoperative clinic. **But a boundary-significant meta-regression coefficient
across thirteen heterogeneous trials cannot carry that weight, and the paper does not ask it to.**

**Archive context: cognitive training and preoperative cognitive prehabilitation have never appeared in this
archive before — not in any brief and not in any guidelines entry.** **The abstract's own opening sentence
says why: non-pharmacological prevention is recommended, but this particular intervention is not explicitly
advocated in guidance.** **So the first appearance of the topic here is a paper reporting a positive result
and simultaneously explaining why that result cannot yet change a recommendation.** That is an unusually
self-aware entry point for a new topic.

## 2. The surrogate has denominators; the outcome does not

**[Perioperative Safety Considerations and Surgical Outcomes Associated With GLP-1-Based Therapies: A
Systematic Review](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.116901)** — *Cureus*, volume 18, article e116901, **first
published 25 September 2026**. Open-access journal; abstract read through the Europe PMC index.

**Not new — a month old — and included deliberately, because of what this archive already holds.** **The
*Anaesthesia* multidisciplinary consensus statement on GLP-1 receptor agonists, GIP agonists and SGLT2
inhibitors in the perioperative period is recorded in this archive at
[/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/), together with a
SPAQI consensus on SGLT2 inhibitors.** **Today's review is the evidence base those consensus panels had to
work from, and reading it after them rather than before is the more informative order.**

**Sixteen studies. The composition is the whole story:**

> **Sixteen studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising seven observational studies and nine case reports
> or case series.**

**Nine of sixteen are case reports or case series.** **Now read where each outcome comes from:**

> Observational evidence demonstrated an association between GLP-1 RA use, particularly semaglutide, and
> **increased residual gastric contents or delayed gastric emptying despite adherence to standard fasting
> protocols**. These findings were identified primarily through gastric ultrasound and endoscopic assessment.
> **In contrast, clinically apparent aspiration or regurgitation events were uncommon and were reported
> predominantly in case-based literature.**

**The surrogate outcome — residual gastric contents on ultrasound — comes from the seven observational studies,
which have denominators.** **The outcome that actually matters — aspiration — is reported "predominantly in
case-based literature", and a case series has no denominator at all.**

**This matters more than it may appear.** **The word "uncommon" is doing work that the cited design cannot
support.** A case report establishes that an event occurred. **It cannot establish a rate, because the
population from which the case was drawn is undefined.** **So "aspiration events were uncommon" is not a
measurement from the case-based literature; it is an inference from the absence of such reports, which is
publication-dependent in both directions.** The review is careful in its own conclusion —
**"current evidence does not establish a corresponding increase in clinically significant aspiration
events"** — which is a statement about what the evidence fails to show rather than a claim that the risk is
low. **The distinction is the entire clinical question, and the abstract keeps it.**

**The one place the observational studies do speak to the hard outcome is noted and is the most reassuring
sentence in the abstract:** **"Larger observational studies did not demonstrate a clear increase in aspiration
or broader perioperative complications."** **That is a denominator-bearing null, and it carries more weight
than the nine case reports combined.**

**One further finding is operationally important and easy to skim past:**

> **Identification of retained gastric contents also influenced clinical management in several reports,
> resulting in changes to anesthetic planning, additional gastric assessment, or postponement of elective
> procedures.**

**This is an instance of a pattern this archive has now logged repeatedly: the intervention alters the
instrument that measures its own effect.** **If finding residual contents on ultrasound causes the case to be
postponed, rapid-sequence-induced, or further assessed, then the aspiration rate observed in ultrasound-screened
patients is partly a product of the screening.** **The surrogate is not merely a proxy for the outcome — it
changes the outcome by changing management.** **Any attempt to estimate "the aspiration risk of GLP-1 agonists"
from a cohort in which gastric ultrasound was used has this built into it, and the review's call for
prospective studies to quantify aspiration risk will run straight into it.**

**My reading.** **The evidence structure here is upside-down relative to the clinical need.** We have
denominator-bearing data on a surrogate that is mechanistically obvious — a drug that slows gastric emptying
leaves more in the stomach — and anecdote-grade data on the outcome that determines whether anyone should
change practice. **That asymmetry explains why the September consensus statement exists at all, and why its
recommendations had to be structured around individualised assessment and selective ultrasound rather than a
fasting interval.** **A panel cannot specify a fasting time from case reports.** **It can tell you to look,
which is what both this review and that consensus end up saying.**

## 3. Three primary outcomes, and no result reported for one of them

**[Comparison of Classic Laryngeal Mask Airway and Endotracheal Intubation for Airway Management During Short
Urologic Procedures in the Prone Position: A Randomized Controlled
Trial](https://doi.org/10.1155/anrp/1390565)** — *Anesthesiology Research and Practice*, volume 2026, article
1390565, **first published 25 October 2026**. Abstract read through the Europe PMC index.

**Seventy patients undergoing prone-position urologic surgery of 90 minutes or less, randomised 35 to a
Classic LMA and 35 to an endotracheal tube.** Trial registration IRCT20250729066688N1.

**The primary outcomes, as stated:**

> **Primary outcomes included intraoperative ventilatory performance assessed by peak airway pressure (Ppeak),
> need for conversion of airway technique, and airway-related complications.**

**Three primary outcomes of three different types: a continuous measurement, a binary event, and a composite
event set.** **Two observations follow, and the second is the substantive one.**

**First, on the result that is reported.** **Ppeak was significantly higher in the ETT group at P < 0.001.**
**That is the one unambiguous between-group difference in the abstract, and it is the least informative of the
three.** **A tracheal tube is a narrower conduit than a supraglottic airway bowl; higher peak pressure through
it is a property of the apparatus, not a finding about the patient.** **Reported as a primary outcome in
favour of the LMA, it reads as evidence of benefit; read mechanically, it is a measurement of tube
resistance.** **Nothing in the abstract connects it to any patient-relevant consequence — not desaturation,
not gastric insufflation, not a ventilation failure — and SpO₂, ETCO₂ and minute ventilation were all
comparable, which is to say the pressure difference did not translate into a ventilation difference.**

**Second, and this is the gap: the abstract reports no result for "need for conversion of airway technique".**
**Not a number, not a statement that none occurred, not a comparison.** **It is named as a primary outcome and
then does not reappear.** **In a prone-position airway trial, conversion rate is the outcome a clinician would
read first — because a supraglottic airway that fails in the prone position is the specific hazard that makes
the technique controversial, and the manoeuvre required to rescue it is the reason for the controversy.**
**An abstract that designates it primary and omits its result has omitted the finding that determines whether
the paper changes anything.** The paper may well report it in full; **but on the abstract alone the central
safety question is unanswerable, and that is worth saying plainly rather than assuming the number is
reassuring.**

**Third, on what 70 patients can establish.** The conclusion is an equivalence claim:

> **Classic LMA provided airway maintenance and ventilation comparable to ETT in short prone-position urologic
> surgeries and may be considered as a feasible airway device in patients meeting appropriate airway
> assessment criteria.**

**"Comparable" from 35 per arm is an absence of detected difference, not demonstrated equivalence, and the
abstract states no equivalence margin and no power calculation.** **For the complications it names — sore
throat and laryngospasm, described as "infrequent and comparable" — 35 patients per arm can exclude almost
nothing.** **If laryngospasm in the prone position occurs in, say, 2% of cases, a 70-patient trial expects
between one and two events in total and will report "comparable" whatever the truth is.** **The appropriate
reading of this trial is that it found no signal of harm, which is useful and is not the same as finding
safety.**

**My reading.** **The trial is asking a real and current question, and its hedged conclusion — "in patients
meeting appropriate airway assessment criteria" — is the right shape.** **But the honest summary of what 70
patients in short cases established is narrower than the abstract's own framing: in selected patients without
anticipated difficult airway, for procedures under 90 minutes, a Classic LMA ventilated as well as a tube at
lower peak pressure, with no detected difference in anything else and with the conversion rate not stated.**
**The conversion rate is the paper's own primary outcome and the only one of the three that bears on the
hazard the technique is questioned for.**

**Archive context: prone-position airway management has appeared once in this archive, in the
[4 October guidelines entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-04/).** **This is the first randomised
trial on the question here.**

## 4. A mortality risk ratio of 0.12 from five retrospective cohorts, with a heterogeneity statistic of exactly zero four times over

**[Perioperative Antifibrotic Treatment and Early Postoperative Outcomes in Patients With Idiopathic Pulmonary
Fibrosis Undergoing Lung Cancer Surgery: A Systematic Review and
Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/1759-7714.70408)** — *Thoracic Cancer*, volume 17, **first published
1 October 2026**. Abstract read through the Europe PMC index.

**The clinical problem is genuine and well posed.** Patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis who undergo
lung cancer surgery risk acute exacerbation of their IPF, **"which is associated with high mortality"**.
Antifibrotic therapy has been proposed as prevention. **Five retrospective cohort studies, predominantly
evaluating pirfenidone.**

**Every pooled result:**

| Outcome | Risk ratio | 95% CI | I² |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **AE-IPF within 30 days** | **0.24** | 0.07–0.76 | **0%** |
| **AE-IPF within 90 days** | **0.23** | 0.09–0.57 | **0%** |
| **Mortality at 30 days** | **0.12** | 0.02–0.91 | **0%** |
| **Mortality at 90 days** | **0.13** | 0.02–0.76 | **0%** |

**Four outcomes, four heterogeneity statistics of exactly zero.** **In five small retrospective studies this
is not evidence of consistency; it is evidence that the analysis has almost no power to detect
heterogeneity.** **I² is a ratio whose denominator depends on the number of studies and the precision within
them, and with five retrospective cohorts and events in single or low double digits the statistic is
essentially uninformative.** **Reading four zeros as "the studies agree" is the error; the correct reading is
that heterogeneity could not have been detected had it been present.**

**The effect sizes are the second problem, and they point the same way.** **A mortality risk ratio of 0.12 is
an 88% relative reduction in death at 30 days from an oral antifibrotic given around the time of a lung
resection.** **The confidence interval runs to 0.91, so the data are compatible with a 9% reduction, and the
interval's width — spanning a factor of 45 — is itself the signal: these are very few events.** **For a drug
that does not alter the operation, the anaesthetic, or the tumour, an 88% point estimate for all-cause
postoperative mortality is larger than the mechanism plausibly supports.**

**And confounding by indication runs in the direction that would produce exactly this.** **In retrospective
cohorts, antifibrotic therapy is given to patients who are well enough to be on it and who are under
specialist respiratory follow-up — which is to say, to a group selected for better baseline function and
closer surveillance.** **The comparator is patients not on antifibrotics, a group that includes those too
unwell to tolerate them, those diagnosed late, and those outside specialist care.** **A mortality difference
of this size in a non-randomised comparison is at least as consistent with that selection as with a drug
effect.** **The authors' own sensitivity analysis says as much:** **"Sensitivity analyses, including a
nationwide database study, supported the association, although the effect was attenuated"** — **attenuation on
adding the largest and least selected data source is the signature of selection effects, and the paper reports
it.**

**One detail in the methods tells you where this literature lives:**

> **We systematically searched major databases, including PubMed and Ichushi-Web, through August 2025.**

**Ichushi-Web is the Japan Medical Abstracts Society database, and searching it alongside PubMed is a
deliberate choice made when a body of evidence is substantially Japanese-language.** **That is consistent with
pirfenidone's history and with where perioperative AE-IPF has been most studied, and it means the pooled
estimate reflects a particular practice context — surgical selection, perioperative management and antifibrotic
prescribing in Japan — rather than a global one.** **It is not a criticism of the search; it is a constraint on
generalisation that the abstract does not state.**

**The nintedanib gap is stated honestly:** **"Evidence for nintedanib was limited to a small case series."**
**So this is a pirfenidone result wearing the label "antifibrotic therapy", and the paper's conclusion says
"particularly with pirfenidone", which is the correct qualification.**

**My reading.** **The direction is almost certainly right and the magnitude almost certainly is not.**
Preventing acute exacerbation in a patient with IPF facing a lung resection is a real and urgent goal, the
biological rationale for antifibrotics is coherent, and four outcomes pointing the same way across five
cohorts is not nothing. **But the quantity a clinician would take from this table — an 88% reduction in 30-day
mortality — is a number produced by few events, selected comparators and a heterogeneity statistic that could
not have warned anyone.** **The paper's closing call for prospective studies is the operative sentence, and the
attenuation on adding the nationwide database is the best indication of what a prospective estimate would
look like: smaller.**

**Archive context: IPF and antifibrotic therapy appear in this archive twice, in the
[6 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-06/) and
[9 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-09/) guidelines entries.** **This is the first time the
perioperative question has appeared here as pooled evidence.**
