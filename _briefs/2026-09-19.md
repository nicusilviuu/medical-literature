## 1. AI-assisted nerve tracking — the fourth technology this month that works and changes nothing

**Journal · Published:** Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine, **18 September 2026** — **published
yesterday** · NCT05857787
Kim, Nam, Yoo J, Hong, Joo, Yoo Y, Moon

Prospective randomised trial in **80 ultrasound novices — 60 medical students and 20 anaesthesiology
residents** — assigned to B-mode ultrasound training with or without **NerveTrack**, a real-time AI
nerve tracking system. Standardised lecture, **10-minute hands-on session**, then assessment **with
the AI function disabled**. Primary outcome: **first-attempt independent identification** of the
median nerve and supraclavicular brachial plexus.

**78 completed.**

- **Median nerve: 57.5% control vs 65.8% AI — RR 1.14 (0.81–1.62), P = 0.452**
- **Brachial plexus: 35.0% vs 42.1% — RR 1.20 (0.68–2.11), P = 0.519**
- After additional AI-assisted training, the AI group reported **greater confidence** in identifying
  the brachial plexus — **4.7 ± 0.5 vs 4.4 ± 0.9, P = 0.046**
- **No significant difference in independent identification success after additional training either**
- The authors' own limits: these findings *"do not establish long-term retention, transfer to
  unfamiliar anatomy, or clinical procedural performance"*

**Interpretation.** **Confidence went up and competence did not.** That is the finding, and it is the
one that should worry anyone buying this class of device for training.

A 0.3-point difference on a 5-point confidence scale at P = 0.046 is fragile, but its direction
against two flat identification outcomes is exactly the pattern that matters: **the tool made
novices feel better at a task they were not better at.** In regional anaesthesia, where the failure
mode is injecting where you think the nerve is, misplaced confidence is not a neutral outcome.

**This is now the fourth device in a month to behave the same way in this archive.** The
[Hypotension Prediction Index](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-10/), whose benefit tracked
treatment intensity rather than prediction. [ETCO₂](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-11/), a real
prognostic marker whose authors were careful to say it was not a target. The
[Analgesia Nociception Index](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-16/), which genuinely reduced
intraoperative remifentanil by 9.5 µg/kg and changed no postoperative outcome. **And now an AI
overlay that identifies nerves correctly and does not teach anyone to identify nerves.** The common
structure: the technology does what it claims, and the claim turns out not to be the thing that
matters.

**The design deserves credit for asking the hard question.** Testing **with the AI disabled** is
what makes this informative — an evaluation with the tool switched on would have shown it works, and
told you nothing about whether it teaches. Most device evaluations do the easy version.

**The honest caveats are the authors' own.** Ten minutes of training, novices rather than
practitioners, a single session, and a laboratory identification task rather than a block. It is
entirely possible that sustained use builds skill; this trial cannot say, and says so.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2026-108180) · PMID 42760064

## 2. EIT-guided PEEP titration — phenotype conversion in 97%, and the direction of PEEP depends on the phenotype

**Journal · Published:** Anesthesiology, **17 September 2026** — **two days old**
Hoshino, Yoshida Y, Maezawa, Iwata, Taenaka, Hashimoto, Koyama, Yamada, **Yoshida T** (Osaka)

Prospective, single-centre, **non-randomised feasibility study**, August 2024 to June 2025.
**60 mechanically ventilated adults** admitted to ICU after surgery at high risk of postoperative
pulmonary complications, assessed by **electrical impedance tomography**.

**30 with heterogeneous ventilation** — 15 **phenotype 1, ventral-dominant**; 15 **phenotype 3,
dorsal-dominant** — received individualised PEEP titration guided by **dorsal fraction of
ventilation (DFV)** targeting **phenotype 2 (DFV 50–59%)**. **30 with homogeneous ventilation**
received no protocolised intervention.

- **Immediate phenotype conversion in 29 of 30 (96.7%, 95% CI 82.8–99.9%)**
- **At 4 hours, 28 of 30 (93.3%) remained within target**
- **Ventral-dominant (n = 15): PEEP increased 5 → 9 cmH₂O**; compliance **+9.2 mL/cmH₂O
  (6.1–12.3)**; **PaO₂/FiO₂ +61.9 mmHg (8.6–115.2)**
- **Dorsal-dominant (n = 15): PEEP DECREASED 7 → 3 cmH₂O**; ventral compliance **+3.4 mL/cmH₂O
  (1.1–5.8)**, **no change in global compliance or oxygenation**
- Individualised PEEP **reduced mean pulmonary artery pressure and increased cardiac index**
- **Exploratory**: severe PPCs in **25% of the interventional cohort vs 41% of a historical
  cohort** — absolute risk difference **−15.8% (−38.8 to 2.2), P = 0.17**

**Interpretation.** **The bidirectional PEEP finding is the whole point, and it is easy to miss.**

Half the intervention group had PEEP **raised** from 5 to 9; the other half had it **lowered** from
7 to 3. A conventional protocol — "high PEEP for high-risk postoperative patients" — would have been
wrong in one group and right in the other, and no trial randomising *a PEEP level* could detect that,
because the two effects cancel. **That is a plausible explanation for why the PEEP literature is
such a mess of neutral trials**, and this study is a concrete demonstration of the mechanism rather
than a speculation about it.

**Note also that the dorsal-dominant group got better without getting better on the usual
measures.** Lowering PEEP improved **ventral compliance** while leaving **global compliance and
oxygenation unchanged** — which means the standard bedside signals would have shown nothing, and a
trial using them as endpoints would have called it a null. Regional measurement found a change that
global measurement could not see.

**But this is a feasibility study and it says so.** Non-randomised, single centre, 60 patients, and
the outcome that matters — severe PPCs, 25% versus 41% — is **explicitly exploratory, against a
historical cohort, with a confidence interval crossing zero at P = 0.17.** That comparison should
not be quoted as evidence of benefit. The demonstrated claim is narrow and real: **phenotypes can be
converted, reliably, and the conversion holds for at least four hours.**

It sits beside the [neutrophil extracellular vesicle study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-16/)
sent on Wednesday — one-lung ventilation releasing inflammatory vesicles through stretch. **Both
argue that the injury of postoperative ventilation is regional, not global**, which is exactly why
global settings and global endpoints keep failing to find it.

## 3. CRRT in cirrhosis — higher prescribed dose, higher mortality

**Journal · Published:** Critical Care Medicine, **17 September 2026** — **two days old**
Karvellas, Lambert, Ouyang, **Bagshaw**, Cullaro, Fricker, Frederick, Goldstein, House, Juncos,
Heung, Patidar, Rewa, Robinson, Teixeira, Wadei, **Neyra**, Allegretti, and the **CRRTnet and
HRS-HARMONY Consortia**

Parallel analysis of contemporaneous data from **two independent North American consortium
databases**, describing CRRT process and outcome in **cirrhosis and acute-on-chronic liver failure**.

**HARMONY n = 245** (median age 57, 41% female); **CRRTnet n = 206** (58, 39% female).

- **Survival to hospital discharge: HARMONY 34%, CRRTnet 30%** — similar across both
- Commonest causes of AKI requiring CRRT: **acute tubular necrosis 73%**, **hepatorenal syndrome-AKI
  13%**, with **no difference between survivors and non-survivors (P = 0.24)**
- **Higher CLIF-C ACLF score independently associated with mortality — HR 1.03 (1.01–1.05),
  P < 0.001**
- Modality: **continuous venovenous haemodiafiltration 83.8%**; anticoagulation: **regional citrate
  58.0%**, **none 35.8%**
- **CRRT prescribed dose > 30 mL/kg/hr independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality —
  OR 2.69 (1.3–5.64), P = 0.01**
- **Of survivors, 64% transitioned to intermittent haemodialysis before discharge**

**Interpretation.** Two findings, and the headline one needs handling carefully.

**A dose above 30 mL/kg/hr associated with an odds ratio of 2.69 for death is almost certainly
confounding by indication, and is still worth knowing.** Sicker patients get prescribed more
clearance — that is the obvious reading, and the abstract gives no indication that severity was
fully adjusted for beyond CLIF-C. But the finding lands in a literature where **ATN and RENAL both
showed no benefit from higher-intensity CRRT**, and where the *Critical Care* letter of 15
September — *"Estimate selection determines the apparent mortality threshold at 13 mL/kg/h"* —
suggests the dose–outcome relationship is sensitive to how it is modelled. **The defensible
conclusion is that there is no signal anywhere that more clearance helps, and repeated signals that
it may not be free.** Prescribing above 30 in a cirrhotic patient needs a reason.

**The finding I would actually act on is the anticoagulation one.** **Regional citrate in 58%** of
these patients, in a population where citrate toxicity is the classic textbook contraindication
because the failing liver cannot metabolise it. The authors note it directly: *"Despite concerns for
citrate toxicity, citrate regional anticoagulation was commonly employed."* **Practice has moved
ahead of the teaching**, and this is the first large description of how far.

**Context for the mortality figure: 30–34% survival to discharge, in the absence of liver
transplantation.** That is the number for the consent conversation, and the near-identical result
across two independent databases makes it solid. The **64% of survivors leaving on intermittent
haemodialysis** is the other half of that conversation.

**Why this matters beyond hepatology:** this archive has carried CRRT on
[ECMO](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-14/) — where 71% of VA-ECMO survivors left dialysis-dependent —
and the [ADQI/ELSO consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-11/) calling for exactly this
kind of process data. **Three CRRT populations in three weeks, all showing high mortality, heavy
dialysis dependence in survivors, and wide practice variation in dose and anticoagulation.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1097/ccm.0000000000007363) · PMID 42752385

## 4. Carotid cannula resistance does not follow French size

**Journal · Published:** Perfusion, **18 September 2026** — **published yesterday**
Holmlund, Servin, Vikström, Johannesdottir, Zarrinkoob, Hellström, Appelblad (Umeå)

Bench measurement (**40–200 mL/min**) plus **computational fluid dynamics** of **four clinically
available carotid perfusion cannulas**, with the resulting resistance models integrated into
**patient-specific CFD models of the cerebral circulation in three patients of varying collateral
capacity**. Both flow-targeted and pressure-targeted perfusion simulated.

The clinical setup: in aortic arch surgery, **bilateral selective antegrade cerebral perfusion** is
often delivered from **a single pump through a Y-connector**, typically **left common carotid by
cannula and right subclavian by graft** — so flow division depends on the resistance of the
left-sided cannula.

- **A 3–4-fold difference in flow resistance between the lowest- and highest-resistance cannulas**,
  reproduced accurately in CFD
- **Resistance did NOT correlate with nominal French size**
- Increasing left-sided cannula resistance **roughly doubled pressure laterality — 7–9 mmHg to
  14–23 mmHg** — and **halved left-sided inflow — 135–160 to 70–100 mL/min**
- **Effects most pronounced in patients with limited collateral circulation**
- Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness

**Interpretation.** This is the most immediately actionable paper of the day, and it is the kind of
finding that only appears when someone measures something everyone assumed.

**"Resistance did not correlate with nominal French size" is the sentence.** French size describes
external diameter. It says nothing reliable about internal geometry, taper, tip design or length —
and those determine resistance. **A team selecting a cannula by French size, believing they are
selecting a flow characteristic, may be off by a factor of three to four.**

**The consequence is not abstract: halving left-sided inflow.** During bilateral SACP the entire
purpose is perfusing both hemispheres, and the left hemisphere's supply is being set by a component
nobody characterises. **In patients with an incomplete circle of Willis — which is a large minority
— there is no collateral route to compensate**, and the modelling says that is exactly where the
effect is largest.

**What makes this credible is the bench-to-CFD agreement.** Measured resistance reproduced in
simulation, then applied to real patient anatomies. The weakness follows from the same design:
**three patients, in silico**, with no clinical outcomes. Nobody has shown that the pressure
laterality translates into neurological injury — but pressure laterality of 14–23 mmHg during
circulatory arrest is not a quantity anyone would choose to accept blind.

**Practically: know which cannula your unit stocks, and know that its French size does not tell you
its resistance.** If you monitor bilateral cerebral oximetry during arch surgery — and the
divergence this paper predicts is precisely what NIRS asymmetry would show — this gives you a
mechanism for a finding that is usually attributed to the patient.
