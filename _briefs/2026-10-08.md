## 1. Agreement on the principles, disagreement on every operational number

**Journal · Published:** *Brain Injury*, **1 October 2026** — seven days old. Scoping review following
Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and PRISMA-ScR; PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, guideline repositories
and grey literature; **English or Spanish**, 2010 to 2026.
Arias-Obando, Casanova-Cadavid, Cardona-Collazos, Delgado-Caicedo, Piragauta-Barrera, Santaella-Tenorio,
Villarreal-Mondragón, Rubiano-Escobar

> **Results** **Sixteen documents met inclusion criteria**, including guidelines, consensus statements, and
> best-practice recommendations. **Broad agreement existed for early neurological assessment, airway
> protection, avoidance of hypotension and hypoxemia, and timely neuroimaging. Heterogeneity remained in
> blood pressure and cerebral perfusion pressure targets, repeat imaging, definitions of neurological
> deterioration, escalation criteria, observation protocols, and discharge processes. Anticonvulsant
> prophylaxis and venous thromboembolism prevention were inconsistently addressed. Blood-based biomarkers
> appeared in only three documents and were limited to computed tomography decision-making.**
>
> **Conclusions** Although the fundamental principles of care were largely consistent, **important
> differences remain in their operational implementation.**

**Interpretation. Read the two lists against each other and the pattern is exact: the agreements are
qualitative and the disagreements are quantitative.** Assess early, protect the airway, avoid hypotension,
image promptly — none of those requires a number. **Every item on the heterogeneity list does:** what
pressure, how often to repeat the scan, how much deterioration counts as deterioration, when to escalate,
how long to observe, when to discharge. **Sixteen documents agree on what to do and disagree on how much.**

**That is not a trivial observation, because the numbers are where the behaviour is.** A unit following two
different guidelines will give the same drugs in the same order and then target different pressures for
different durations. **And it explains how a ten-year-old canonical guideline can coexist with fifteen other
documents without anyone noticing the gap**: the principles have been stable since before 2016, so the
documents look concordant until you ask for a threshold.

**Three specifics worth pulling out.** **Anticonvulsant prophylaxis and venous thromboembolism prevention
were "inconsistently addressed"** — not disagreed about, but in some documents simply absent, which is a
worse failure mode for a reader using one guideline as their only source. **Blood-based biomarkers appeared
in only three of sixteen documents, and only for CT decision-making** — so the biomarker literature of the
past decade has reached the guidelines almost not at all, and where it has, it is used to decide whether to
scan rather than to guide treatment. **This is the same pattern as [Monday's
finding](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-06/) on effect modification: biomarkers are being used to
stratify who gets a test, not to select who gets a therapy.**

**One limitation to state, and it follows directly from yesterday.** **The last author is Rubiano-Escobar,
and Cardona-Collazos is a co-author** — and [yesterday's watch](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-07/)
reported **BOOTStraP** and **EXTRACCT**, two resource-stratified neurotrauma documents on which **Rubiano is
first author and Cardona-Collazos a co-author of the second.** So **the scoping review that maps the
guideline landscape shares two authors with two of the documents in that landscape.** The review's inclusion
criteria and its judgement of what counts as agreement are not independent of the documents being reviewed.
**That does not make the mapping wrong — the heterogeneity it reports is checkable against the documents —
but it should be stated by anyone citing it, and the review does not state it in its abstract.**

[Read the scoping review](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699052.2026.2733594) — paywalled beyond the abstract.

## 2. The tolerable lower limit of perfusion pressure depends on whether autoregulation is working

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **20 April 2026** — five and a half months old. **Open access**
(PMC13227617). Prospective observational study, **187 patients** with invasive neuromonitoring, first seven
days post-injury, **six-month GOSE**. Two-variable heatmaps and generalised additive models with cubic
splines.
Yuan, Yao, Yu, Guo, Ye, Wang, Song, Liu, Xi, Chen, Du, Chen, Wu, Yang, Hu, Wu

> **BACKGROUND:** Considering the significant heterogeneity of traumatic brain injury (TBI), **uniform
> treatment thresholds applied to all patients may be overly simplistic.**
>
> **RESULTS:** In total, **187 patients** … **PRx exhibited a U-shaped relationship with CPP, with the
> optimal PRx observed at a CPP of 70–80 mmHg.** … **In the PRx–CPP heatmap, outcomes worsened when CPP
> decreased and PRx deviated from 0. Lower CPP appeared better tolerated in patients with preserved
> autoregulation**, as indicated by lower PRx values. **Patients without decompressive craniectomy (DC), with
> diffuse injury or younger age demonstrated better tolerance to lower CPP** when autoregulatory function was
> preserved compared to patients with DC, focal injury or older age. When all physiological variables were
> incorporated into a single multivariable model, **only composite variable 2 remained independently
> associated with favorable outcome (odds ratio 1.026; 95% CI, 1.006–1.046, P = 0.012).**
>
> **CONCLUSIONS:** … **The acceptable lower limit of CPP appears to be directly proportional to the PRx.
> Consequently, patients with severe impairment of CA may require higher CPP targets** … **rather than
> relying on universally applied empirical thresholds.**

**Interpretation. This is the mechanistic answer to why item 1 found pressure targets heterogeneous: there is
no single right number, and the study says which way the number moves.** The pressure reactivity index is a
moving correlation between arterial pressure and intracranial pressure — near zero when autoregulation is
intact, positive when the vasculature is passive. **The finding is that a patient whose autoregulation is
working tolerates a lower perfusion pressure, and a patient whose autoregulation has failed needs a higher
one.** That is the opposite of how a fixed 60–70 mmHg floor behaves: it over-treats the patient who is
autoregulating and under-treats the one who is not.

**It fits the thread this archive reframed on [3 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-03/) — that
absolute pressure targeting does not work — and sharpens it.** The earlier framing was negative: fixed
targets fail. **This supplies the replacement variable.** The target is not a pressure, it is a pressure
relative to the patient's own autoregulatory optimum, and the optimum is measurable at the bedside.

**Two things to hold against it.** **"Composite variable 2" is never defined in the abstract**, and it is the
only variable that survived the multivariable model. **An odds ratio of 1.026 per unit of an undefined
composite is uninterpretable** — the effect could be large or trivial depending on the unit, and a reader
cannot tell which. **When the headline model reduces to one variable and that variable is not named, the
model is not reportable from the abstract**, and the heatmaps are doing the real work of the paper.

**And it is observational, 187 patients, one cohort.** The direction of the effect is biologically
compelling and the splines make it visible, but **"patients with impaired autoregulation may require higher
CPP" is a hypothesis about treatment derived from an association** — which is precisely the claim item 3 was
built to test.

[Read the study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06030-9) — open access.

## 3. The trial that tested it, what it was powered for, and the five years since

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Neurotrauma*, **16 August 2021** — **five years and two months old, at
the outer edge of this archive's window, and included because it is the only randomised test of the idea in
item 2.** 179 citations. Phase II parallel two-arm **feasibility** trial, four tertiary centres.
Tas, Beqiri, van Kaam, Czosnyka, Donnelly, Haeren, van der Horst, Hutchinson, van Kuijk, Liberti, Menon,
Hoedemaekers, Depreitere, Smielewski, Meyfroidt and colleagues

> Managing TBI patients with a CPP near to the CA-guided "optimal" CPP (CPPopt) value is associated with
> improved outcome and might be useful to individualize care, **but has never been prospectively evaluated.**
> … **Twenty-eight patients** were randomized to the control and **32 patients** to the intervention group.
> **CPP in the intervention group was in the target range for 46.5% (interquartile range, 41.2–58) of the
> monitored time, significantly higher than the feasibility target specified in the published protocol (36%;
> p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between groups for TIL** or for other safety end-points.
> **Conclusively, targeting an individual and dynamic CA-guided CPP is feasible and safe** … **This
> encourages a prospective trial powered for clinical outcomes.**

**Interpretation. Read the primary endpoint carefully, because it is a process measure and the trial is
honest about that.** The primary feasibility endpoint was **the percentage of monitored time with CPP within
±5 mmHg of the set target**, and it reached **46.5% against a pre-specified 36% threshold.** So the trial
demonstrated that **a unit given six software alerts a day can hold a moving target under half the time** —
which cleared the bar the investigators set, and is a modest absolute performance. **Nothing about outcome
was tested, and the paper says so in its last sentence.**

**The safety result is the substantive one.** **No difference in therapeutic intensity level** means
individualised targeting did not drive more sedation, osmotherapy or vasopressor use — the obvious fear when
you tell a unit to chase a variable target. That is what a feasibility trial is for, and it is a genuine
clearance.

**The follow-up is where it gets interesting, and it is a lesson about summary statistics.** A 2024 secondary
analysis asked the question the main trial left hanging:

> **The average values of the autoregulation index known as the pressure reactivity index (PRx) were not
> different between the intervention … and control … groups of the trial.** … **The median ΔPRx was
> significantly lower in the intervention group for negative PRxopt (p < 0.001). For each patient in this
> group, the median PRx was lower in periods when CPP was close to the CPPopt-based target (p < 0.001).** …
> **Despite no statistically significant difference in the grand mean PRx, our results suggest that targeting
> CPPopt does provide a way of improving cerebrovascular reactivity.**

**The intervention did what it was designed to do, and the grand mean hid it.** Comparing average PRx between
arms asks whether one group's autoregulation was better overall; the right question is **whether each patient
was closer to their own optimum**, which is what ΔPRx measures and what the within-patient comparison shows.
**The effect was present in the trial's own data and invisible to the trial's own summary statistic.**

**That is the archive's parameterisation rule in its sharpest form yet: the choice of summary measure was not
a detail, it was the difference between a null and a positive mechanistic result on identical data.** And it
is a specific warning for anyone designing the outcome trial — **the mechanistic endpoint has to be
distance-from-individual-optimum, not a group mean.**

**The last point is an absence, and it is the reason this five-year-old trial is today's item.** COGiTATE
ended by calling for *"a prospective trial powered for clinical outcomes."* **Searched today across 2022 to
2026 for randomised or protocol publications on CPPopt or autoregulation-guided targets: four records, of
which one is the secondary analysis above, one its preprint, one a paediatric visualisation study, and one a
cardiac-arrest analogue in design.** **No outcome-powered trial has appeared in five years.** So the position
is: **the guidelines disagree about the pressure target (item 1); observational data say the right target
depends on autoregulation (item 2); the one randomised study shows individualising it is feasible, safe, and
mechanistically effective (item 3); and nobody has run the trial that would put it in a guideline.** **That
is the whole shape of the problem yesterday's absence described, in three papers.**
