## 1. Lidocaine changes the EEG before BIS notices

**Journal · Published:** *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, **22 September 2026** — **new this week**.
Observational, prospective, single-centre; NCT06135688
Linassi, Carron, Zanatta, Aydin, Spano, Rizzetto, Polesello, Schneider, Bonhomme, **Kreuzer**

The question, from the abstract:

> Lidocaine administration during stable propofol-remifentanil anaesthesia **causes a decrease in
> bispectral index (BIS), although its influence on hypnosis is debated.** We evaluated
> dose-dependent lidocaine effects on spectral EEG features and the processed parameters BIS and 95%
> spectral edge frequency (SEF95).

**Twenty adult female patients undergoing breast surgery.** During stable propofol-remifentanil
target-controlled infusion: **lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg over 10 min, then 1.5 mg/kg/h for 30 min.**
Outcomes were the spectral composition of the EEG — **periodic and aperiodic features** — plus BIS,
SEF95 and the **Surgical Pleth Index (SPI)**.

> We observed an **increase in delta-band EEG activity and a decrease in beta-band EEG activity 4 to
> 6 min after the bolus, whereas BIS and SEF95 did not change significantly.** The alpha peak
> features bandwidth and centre frequency also decreased significantly in the early period. The
> **aperiodic slope** slightly but significantly increased after the bolus and during infusion.
> **During the infusion phase, BIS and SEF95 significantly decreased after 12 to 15 min**, as did SPI
> in the later stages.

> When lidocaine is given during stable propofol-remifentanil anaesthesia, **changes in spectral EEG
> parameters occur earlier than changes in BIS, SEF95 and SPI.** The observed EEG and SPI changes are
> **consistent with a predominant antinociceptive contribution to the anaesthetic-sparing effects of
> lidocaine**, although the physiological interpretation of the derived parameters remains an
> evolving area of research.

**Interpretation.** A small, careful mechanistic study that says something sharper than its size
suggests, and it is a different criticism of monitoring from the one this archive keeps making.

**The recurring complaint in these briefs has been that monitors measure things without changing
outcomes. This is a narrower and more concrete problem: the processed index is slower than the signal
it is computed from.** Delta rose and beta fell **within four to six minutes** while BIS and SEF95
sat still, and BIS only moved **12 to 15 minutes** into the infusion. Anyone watching a BIS number
during that window would have seen nothing happen while the cortical state was demonstrably changing.

**The interpretive claim — that the effect is antinociceptive rather than hypnotic — is the authors'
and it is reasonable but not established by this design.** What supports it is the SPI falling late
alongside BIS, and the absence of the alpha-power changes that usually mark deepening propofol
hypnosis; in fact **the alpha peak's bandwidth and centre frequency fell**, which is not the
signature of more hypnosis. What would settle it is a measure of responsiveness, which an
observational study during surgery cannot supply.

**The aperiodic slope is the methodologically interesting part.** Separating the EEG into periodic
(oscillatory peaks) and aperiodic (the broadband 1/f background) components is a recent move in
computational neuroscience that has only lately reached anaesthesia, and the finding that the
aperiodic slope shifts under lidocaine matters because **BIS and SEF95 are both computed in ways that
partly confound the two.** A monitor that mixes an oscillatory and a broadband effect into one number
can report no change while both change.

**Take it narrowly.** Twenty patients, all female, one operation, one anaesthetic technique, no
outcome beyond the traces. It does not show that lidocaine is safe or unsafe to give during
BIS-guided anaesthesia; it shows that **during the first ten minutes, BIS will not tell you what
lidocaine has done.**

## 2. A decision-support prompt that changed what clinicians wrote — half a million anaesthetics

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **22 September 2026** — **new this week**
Murphy, Colquhoun, Mentz, Burns, Mathis, Jiao, Shah, Wixson, Haydar, Klumpner

> ASA Physical Status classification is a widely used ordinal scale that captures comorbidity burden
> but has **significant inter-rater variability.** Previous efforts to enhance ASA PS documentation
> with Clinical Decision Support are limited by **inaccurate problem lists, poor user experience, or
> consideration of select comorbidities.** We developed a CDS which uses the **anesthesia clinicians'
> own documentation** to highlight potential **under-classification** at the time of ASA PS
> assignment.

Method: **51 conditions warranting an ASA PS of at least 3** were identified, *"building upon examples
from ASA Committee on Economics and institutional consensus"*, and embedded in an in-line prompt at
one large academic centre. **A second large midwestern academic centre served as a contemporaneous
external control**, with a **difference-in-differences** analysis.

> **251,944 anesthetics from Site 1 and 286,172 from Site 2.** At Site 1, the proportion classified
> ASA PS 3 or higher increased from **52.9% to 61.7% (aOR 1.42, 95% CI 1.38–1.45)**. Site 2 had a
> small absolute increase from **47.2% to 48.4% (aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.07)**. Among Site 1 patients
> with the 50 conditions addressed by the later phase of the CDS, the proportion classified ASA PS 3
> or greater increased from **83.6% to 94.0% (aOR 3.54, 95% CI 3.34–3.76)**.

**Interpretation.** Methodologically this is the strongest paper in today's brief, and its conclusion
is narrower than its title.

**The design is genuinely good.** More than half a million anaesthetics, a contemporaneous external
control site, difference-in-differences, and a prompt built on the clinician's own documentation
rather than on a problem list — which is the reason earlier attempts failed, and the authors say so.
The control site moving 1.2 percentage points while the intervention site moved 8.8 is about as clean
a behavioural signal as observational health-informatics work produces.

**But the outcome is documentation, not accuracy, and the title says accuracy.** The study measures
**whether the recorded ASA PS changed**, and infers improvement from the premise that the pre-CDS
state was under-classification. That premise is plausible — inter-rater variability in ASA PS is well
established and the direction of error is usually downward — **but it is an assumption, and the design
cannot separate "more accurate" from "higher".** There is no reference standard here: no adjudicated
classification, no outcome correlation, nothing that says the patients reclassified to ASA 3 were
sicker than those who stayed at ASA 2.

**And the elephant in the methods section is named in the methods section.** The 51 conditions derive
in part from the **ASA Committee on Economics** — the billing committee — because **ASA PS drives
anaesthesia reimbursement**. A prompt that reliably moves patients from ASA 2 to ASA 3 has a revenue
consequence, and the paper does not address it. That is not an accusation: under-classification is a
real documentation problem and correcting it legitimately increases billed complexity. But **a study
whose only measured outcome is a code that determines payment, reporting that the code went up, owes
the reader that sentence**, and it is missing.

**Set against the monitors thread, it is an interesting inversion.** Every device in this archive over
the last fortnight measured something accurately and changed nothing. **This intervention changed
behaviour decisively and measured nothing about the patient.**

## 3. Postoperative hoarseness — 676,641 general anaesthetics, and the nasogastric tube

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesia & Analgesia*, **21 September 2026** — **new this week**.
Retrospective, single centre, ten years
Xie, Chen, Chu, Jiang, Yao, Yin, Liang, Feng, Kang, Su

> **676,641 patients underwent general anesthesia between October 2015 and September 2025.** Of
> these, **1185 were referred to the Otolaryngology Department** with postoperative laryngopharyngeal
> complaints, **958 of whom were diagnosed with hoarseness** — an incidence of **958/676,641 (0.142%)**,
> about **1.5 per 1000.**

**Within the referral cohort**, distinguishing hoarseness from other laryngopharyngeal complaints:

> **Nasogastric tube placement (OR 2.34, 95% CI 1.63–3.38)** and **transoesophageal echocardiography
> (OR 1.84, 95% CI 1.15–3.02)** were independently associated with hoarseness.

Greater **vocal fold injury severity** was associated with **nasogastric tube (OR 2.11)**, **TEE (OR
1.61)**, **longer surgical duration (OR 1.10 per hour)**, **double-lumen tube (OR 1.55)** and **female
sex (OR 1.41)**. On recovery:

> **Delayed first consultation (≥6 days after symptom onset)** and greater vocal fold injury severity
> were associated with **slower recovery (both P < .001)**. The nasogastric tube association persisted
> in an expanded-cohort sensitivity analysis (**OR 4.20, 95% CI 3.00–5.95**).

**Interpretation.** A large, patient, unglamorous dataset on a complication that gets attributed to
the tracheal tube almost by reflex — and the two strongest associations are not the tracheal tube.

**The comparison group is the thing to understand before quoting any of these numbers.** The odds
ratios are **within the 1,185 patients referred to ENT**, comparing those diagnosed with hoarseness
against those referred with *other* laryngopharyngeal complaints. **They are not risk factors for
hoarseness versus no hoarseness across all 676,641 anaesthetics.** That is a much weaker design than
the numbers suggest, and it cannot tell you that a nasogastric tube raises your patient's risk of
hoarseness — only that, among patients whose throat problem was bad enough to reach ENT, the ones
with a nasogastric tube were more likely to have hoarseness specifically. The expanded-cohort
sensitivity analysis with 480 controls is a partial answer, and the association strengthened there.

**With that caveat the findings are plausible and useful.** The nasogastric tube crosses the same
space as the tracheal tube and sits against the posterior larynx for the whole case; **TEE is an
oesophageal probe manipulated repeatedly behind the larynx during cardiac surgery** and is the finding
most relevant to this project's scope. **Double-lumen tube** — larger, stiffer, deliberately
positioned — fits the same mechanical story, as does duration.

**The actionable finding is the recovery one, and it is the only one that changes practice today.**
**Consultation ≥6 days after symptom onset predicted slower recovery.** If a patient is hoarse after
an operation, the delay in getting them looked at is a variable under the anaesthetist's control, and
this is direct evidence it matters. **Nobody needs a randomised trial to refer a hoarse postoperative
patient promptly.**

**It also sits with the airway seam opened this week.** The [DAS 2025
guidelines](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/) reorganise around **maximising first-attempt
success** and include a named **post-intubation care** section. This is what post-intubation care is
for, and the finding that a *nasogastric* tube outperforms the tracheal tube as an association is the
sort of thing a guideline focused on intubation may not capture.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008300) · PMID 42772785

## 4. The antithrombin paper, written up because yesterday's review needed it

**Journal · Published:** *Shock*, **9 September 2026** — **two weeks old**. Flagged as a lead in the
[10 September brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-10/) and not written up then; reported now
because [yesterday's symmetrical peripheral gangrene
review](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/) arrived at the same mechanism from the other end
Matsumoto, Kan, Takezawa, Tanabe, Annen, Mukai, Ohshita, Nakabayashi, Kikuchi, Takeba, Sato

> Early antithrombin (AT) depletion after trauma may result from **thrombin-related consumption,
> vascular leakage, and systemic protein loss.** We examined the biomarker profile associated with
> early AT depletion and whether reduced AT activity identifies an adverse early injury phenotype.

Single-centre retrospective observational study, **100 adult trauma patients**, admission samples.
**AT activity, albumin, syndecan-1 (a glycocalyx shedding marker) and thrombin-antithrombin complex
(TAT)** measured in residual blood. LOESS and generalised additive models for the biomarker
relationships; **LASSO and elastic-net** for variable selection; exploratory Bayesian regression for
direction.

> **Reduced AT activity was present in 32% of patients** and remained associated with **shock
> (adjusted OR 9.42, 95% CI 1.32–109.00)** and **DIC (adjusted OR 12.20, 95% CI 2.17–113.00)** after
> adjustment for age, sex, ISS and lactate. **AT activity was positively associated with albumin and
> negatively associated with syndecan-1 and TAT. In regularized regression, albumin and syndecan-1
> were more consistently associated with AT activity, whereas TAT was not selected.** Bayesian
> analyses showed stronger directional evidence for albumin and syndecan-1 than for TAT.

> Early AT depletion after trauma was associated with **biomarker patterns consistent with endothelial
> injury and circulating protein depletion, beyond thrombin-related consumption alone.**

**Interpretation.** The mechanism is the finding; the effect estimates are close to uninformative and
should be quoted only with their intervals.

**The substantive result is a negative one about TAT.** If early antithrombin depletion were mainly
**consumption** — antithrombin being used up binding thrombin — then thrombin-antithrombin complex
should track it. **It did not: TAT was not selected by either regularised model, while albumin and
syndecan-1 were.** Antithrombin falls with the other plasma proteins and with glycocalyx shedding,
which points at **capillary leak and endothelial injury** rather than at the coagulation cascade
consuming it.

**That is the same mechanism, reached independently, as yesterday's review.** The
[symmetrical peripheral gangrene review](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/) by Warkentin, Iba and
Levy argues that ischaemic limb necrosis in shock is thromboinflammatory rather than
vasopressor-caused, and lists **endothelial injury, glycocalyx disruption, and natural anticoagulant
depletion — antithrombin, protein C, protein S** — as the chain. **This paper independently shows the
antithrombin arm of that chain behaving like a leak problem rather than a consumption problem, in a
different population, using a different method.** Two papers a fortnight apart converging on the same
picture is the reason to report a two-week-old observational study.

**The numbers, however, must not be quoted bare.** An adjusted odds ratio of **9.42 with a confidence
interval running from 1.32 to 109.00** is a way of saying "the sign is probably positive and the
magnitude is unknown"; 12.20 (2.17–113.00) is the same. **With 100 patients and 32 events these are
the intervals you would expect, and they are compatible with a modest association or an enormous
one.** The paper is hypothesis-generating about mechanism and should not be cited for an effect size.

**The therapeutic implication is the one nobody should draw yet.** Antithrombin concentrate exists,
and a paper showing antithrombin depletion marks bad outcomes invites the inference that replacing it
would help. **It does not follow** — if antithrombin is falling because the endothelium is leaking,
then the low level is a marker of the leak, and topping it up treats the number. That is the same
error this archive has flagged in four device papers this month, in a different medium.
