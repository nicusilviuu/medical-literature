## 1. GLP-1 receptor agonists before surgery — tested in the era before the guidance

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **25 September 2026** — **three days old.** Observational,
propensity-matched
Choi, Nicholson, Messinger, **Oprea**, **Sweitzer**, Hobai, **Connors**, **Hepner**

The design choice is the paper:

> The authors conducted an observational analysis with data between **June 1, 2013, to June 1, 2023**…
> **before the 2023 American Society of Anesthesiologists guidance to hold GLP-1 RAs preoperatively.**

Adult surgical patients with type 2 diabetes, **propensity-score-matched 1:1** by surgery type, patient
characteristics, diabetic biomarkers and morbidity risk factors, against three separate comparators —
**metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, and DPP4 inhibitors** — with no concomitant use of the comparator.
Outcomes at 14 days included mortality, **aspiration pneumonitis**, bacterial pneumonia, emergency
intubation, AKI, stroke, myocardial infarction and MACE.

> Preoperative GLP-1 RA use was associated with **reduced mortality (0.98% vs 2.20%; RR 0.44; 95% CI
> 0.31 to 0.64; Padj = 0.0002)** compared with metformin. Compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, **a numerical
> difference in MACE (3.86% vs 5.03%; RR 0.77; 95% CI 0.60 to 0.98; Padj = 0.13)**. Compared with
> DPP4is, **decreased mortality (1.84% vs 2.89%; RR 0.63; 95% CI 0.45 to 0.89; Padj = 0.04)** and
> **bacterial pneumonia (0.85% vs 1.80%; RR 0.47; 95% CI 0.29 to 0.76; Padj = 0.02)**.

> …and **no increased aspiration risk compared with any other drug class.**

**Interpretation.** The most important result here is the null one, and the mortality findings should be
treated with considerable suspicion.

**The null on aspiration is what the paper was built to find, and it is the credible part.** The entire
2023 ASA instruction to hold these drugs rests on aspiration risk from delayed gastric emptying. **This
cohort deliberately predates that guidance — so it captures an era when the drugs were simply
continued — and finds no excess aspiration pneumonitis against three separate comparator classes.**
Null results in observational data are far less vulnerable to the problem that afflicts the rest of
this abstract, because confounding usually manufactures benefit rather than erasing harm.

**The mortality results are not plausible as a treatment effect.** A **56% relative reduction in 14-day
postoperative mortality** from a diabetes drug, compared with metformin, is not something a GLP-1
agonist does in a fortnight. **It is the signature of who was taking these drugs between 2013 and
2023** — a period when they were expensive, variably reimbursed, and prescribed to patients with better
access to care and closer follow-up. Propensity matching on surgery type, biomarkers and comorbidity
**cannot match on the things that determined who received a GLP-1 agonist in that era.** The authors
close by saying confirmation in prospective studies is needed, which is the right position.

**Note the honesty in the SGLT2 comparison, which is easy to misread.** MACE 3.86% versus 5.03% with a
confidence interval of 0.60 to 0.98 — **an interval that excludes 1** — yet the adjusted p value is
**0.13** and the authors describe it as a *numerical difference*. **That is multiplicity correction
doing its job**, and reporting it as non-significant rather than quoting the interval is the
disciplined choice.

**Where this lands in the archive's GLP-1 thread.** [The 17 September guidelines
entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-17/) found the ADS/ANZCA recommendations saying continue
the drug; [the *Anaesthesia* multidisciplinary consensus of 20
September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/) says continue, with risk assessment and
aspiration-mitigation techniques. **This adds outcome data from the natural experiment those documents
were reasoning about without**, and it points the same way on the only question that matters for the
guidance.

**Authorship, again.** **Oprea, Sweitzer and Hepner are all authors of the SPAQI SGLT2 consensus**
reported on 20 September, and **Connors** is an author of the [symmetrical peripheral gangrene
review](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/) covered on the 22nd. The concentration this archive has
tracked since 21 September now spans ten documents.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1097/aln.0000000000006326) · PMID 42585629

## 2. Noradrenaline dose and the microcirculation, with pressure and ScvO₂ at target

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Critical Care*, **24 September 2026** — **four days old.**
Prospective observational; NCT07312071
Özakın, Şehirlioğlu, Özakın, Moralar, Dinç

> Septic shock may be complicated by **hemodynamic incoherence, in which macrohemodynamic targets are
> achieved despite persistent microcirculatory alterations.**

**Thirty mechanically ventilated adults with septic shock**, studied **after initial resuscitation, once
MAP ≥65 mmHg had been achieved with vasopressor support.** Thenar **near-infrared spectroscopy vascular
occlusion testing**; primary outcomes **StO₂ recovery slope** and **StO₂ hyperaemia area under the
curve**.

> **Despite similar MAP and ScvO₂ between dose groups, the High Dose group had lower StO₂ recovery
> slope and StO₂ hyperemia AUC.** Norepinephrine-equivalent dose was **inversely associated with StO₂
> recovery slope (rho = −0.728; Holm-adjusted p < 0.001)**, persisting after separate adjustment for
> severity, oxygen transport and metabolic variables. **ScvO₂ was not associated with dynamic
> VOT-derived indices** after correction for multiple comparisons.

**Interpretation.** The cleanest illustration yet of the thread this archive has been following since
early September, and it indicts the surrogate as much as the target.

**Two numbers were at goal and the tissue was not.** MAP ≥65, ScvO₂ preserved — the two things a bedside
clinician uses to decide that resuscitation is working — **and the more noradrenaline it took to get
there, the worse the microvascular reserve.** A Spearman rho of **−0.728** is a strong correlation in
any sample, and it survived adjustment.

**The finding about ScvO₂ is the practically useful one and is being under-sold by its own abstract.**
**ScvO₂ did not track dynamic microvascular reserve at all.** Central venous saturation is the standard
proxy for whether oxygen delivery is meeting demand; here it was normal in patients whose
microcirculation could not respond to a reperfusion challenge. **A normal ScvO₂ is not evidence of an
intact microcirculation**, and this is direct measurement rather than inference.

**The causal direction is genuinely unresolved, and the paper cannot settle it.** Patients needing more
noradrenaline have worse shock, and worse shock damages the microcirculation — so the dose may be a
marker rather than a cause. **The authors say "associated" throughout and do not overreach.** The
adjustment for severity is reassuring but adjustment in 30 patients does not eliminate confounding by
severity; nothing here should be read as a reason to withhold vasopressor from a hypotensive patient.

**It sits directly with [the beach-chair trial of 21
September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-21/)** — phenylephrine holding blood pressure while
cerebral oxygenation declined — and with the cannula, HPI and ETCO₂ items before it. **Same shape,
different organ: the number you are titrating to is not the thing you are trying to protect.**

*Thirty patients, single centre, observational, one vascular bed.*

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155762) · PMID 42790104

## 3. The trials behind perioperative guidelines have a median fragility index of 4

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **25 September 2026** — **three days old.** A methodologic
survey
Otalora-Esteban, Segura-Salguero, Zabida, Echeverri Mallarino, Diaz-Bohada, Artistizabal-Mayor,
Muñoz-Leyva, Gil and colleagues

> The **Fragility Index** quantifies how small changes in trial outcomes can alter statistical
> significance… **fragility has not been systematically examined in the perioperative evidence
> supporting anesthesiology guidelines.**

RCTs **cited in North American and European perioperative clinical practice guidelines** published
2012–2022, across adult, paediatric, obstetric, cardiovascular and regional anaesthesia populations.

> Of **1,868 identified RCTs, 639 met the eligibility criteria, and primary analyses included 161
> superiority trials.** The **median sample size was 120 participants (IQR 80 to 310)**, and the
> **median Fragility Index was 4 (IQR 2 to 8).** **Cardiovascular trials had the highest median FI (6),
> whereas pediatric trials had the lowest (1).** Obstetric trials had higher FI than adult trials (IRR
> 1.63); **single-centre trials had lower FI than multicentre (IRR 0.52).**

> Randomized superiority trials underpinning contemporary perioperative guideline recommendations are
> **generally small and demonstrate modest Fragility Index values, indicating that statistical
> conclusions often depend on relatively few outcome events.**

**Interpretation.** This archive has spent a month describing the evidence under perioperative guidance
qualitatively. **Here is the number.**

**A median fragility index of 4 means that, for the typical trial cited in a perioperative guideline,
moving four patients from one outcome column to the other would extinguish the result.** The median
trial enrolled 120 people. **And the paediatric median is 1** — a single patient — which deserves to be
read twice, because paediatric anaesthesia guidance is exactly where practice is most rule-bound and
least often revisited.

**It gives a quantitative frame for what this month's documents kept saying about themselves.** The
[videolaryngoscopy guidelines](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/) reported strong consensus on
low-quality evidence; the [paediatric lidocaine topicalisation
consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-26/) reported evidence grades C and D against moderate
to strong consensus; the [Brazilian perioperative hyperglycaemia
statement](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-27/) used a narrative review. **The fragility data
says the problem is not confined to documents that admit it** — even the recommendations resting on
randomised evidence often rest on a handful of events.

**The single-centre finding runs against intuition and is worth pausing on.** Single-centre trials had
*lower* fragility indices than multicentre ones (IRR 0.52) — which, since a low FI means a more fragile
result, says **single-centre trials cited in guidelines are the more fragile ones.** Smaller, likelier
to report a large effect, and likelier to be the study a recommendation hangs on.

**The authors' own caveat is important and should not be skipped.** *"Fragility metrics should
complement, rather than replace, established measures of trial quality and evidence certainty."* **The
fragility index is a function of sample size and p-value; it measures numerical instability, not bias,
and a large well-conducted trial with a modest true effect will have a low FI without being
unreliable.** It is a useful lens, not a verdict.

[Read the survey](https://doi.org/10.1097/aln.0000000000006311) · PMID 42565467

## 4. Antithrombin in cardiac surgery — the association does not survive adjustment

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Cardiothoracic and Vascular Anesthesia*, **24 September 2026** —
**four days old.** Prospective exploratory observational cohort
Hua, Abraha, Guerra, Barkley, **Callum**, Payne, Lieberman, **McCluskey**, **Karkouti**, Bartoszko

**103 adults** undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass at **two academic centres using
factor concentrate-based haemostatic algorithms without routine antithrombin supplementation.**
Fibrinogen concentrate and 4-factor PCC first-line; **only 2 patients (2%) received plasma.**
Antithrombin activity measured immediately before and after bypass; lowest quintile compared with the
rest.

> **Q1 patients had lower postbypass AT levels and higher rates of acute kidney injury,
> vasopressor/inotrope use, and longer hospital stay, but prebypass AT status was not an independent
> predictor in multivariable models.**

> …low perioperative AT activity was **associated with higher unadjusted complication rates but did not
> independently predict outcomes after accounting for patient and procedural factors. The routine use
> of factor concentrates over plasma as first-line hemostatic therapy, without additional routine AT
> supplementation, does not appear to increase risk.**

**Interpretation.** This closes a thread this archive opened five days ago, and it closes it in the
direction predicted.

**On 23 September the [*Shock* trauma paper](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-23/) showed early
antithrombin depletion tracking albumin and syndecan-1 rather than thrombin-antithrombin complex — a
leak and endothelial-injury signature rather than consumption.** The interpretation written here then
was: *"if antithrombin is falling because the endothelium is leaking, then the low level is a marker of
the leak, and topping it up treats the number."*

**This paper tests that in a different population and finds exactly that.** Low antithrombin marked
patients with more AKI, more vasopressor use and longer stays — **and stopped predicting anything once
patient and procedural factors were accounted for.** That is what a marker of sickness looks like when
you adjust for sickness. **Two papers, five days apart, different populations, different methods, same
conclusion.**

**The second finding is the practical one and is easy to miss.** These centres run **factor
concentrate-based algorithms with almost no plasma and no routine antithrombin supplementation** — and
that strategy **did not appear to increase risk.** Since plasma is the traditional route by which
antithrombin is incidentally replaced, a programme that has largely abandoned plasma might be expected
to produce antithrombin-related harm. **It did not.**

**The limitation is the one the authors flag by calling it exploratory, and it matters.** **With 103
patients, failing to find independent prediction is weak evidence of absence** — a multivariable model
in a cohort that size has little power to isolate one biomarker. **The right reading is that this is
consistent with the trauma data rather than that it independently proves the point.** The convergence
of two studies is the argument; neither alone would be.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jvca.2026.08.157) · PMID 42786025
