## 1. A noninferiority margin of ten points, against a control rate of eleven

**[Effect of remimazolam versus propofol anaesthesia on postoperative delirium in neurovascular surgery: a
randomised noninferiority trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2026.09.021)** — *British Journal of
Anaesthesia*, **first published 29 October 2026**. NCT06115031.

**The clinical setup is specific and well posed.** Neurovascular surgery uses propofol total intravenous
anaesthesia because intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring limits inhalational agents; **propofol causes
hypotension and prolonged infusions delay emergence; remimazolam is reversible with flumazenil and produces
less hypotension — but it is a benzodiazepine, and benzodiazepines are the drug class most associated with
delirium.** So the trial asks the right question: can you take the haemodynamic benefit without paying for it
in delirium?

**Design:** single-centre, **double-blind**, randomised noninferiority; adults undergoing elective aneurysm
clipping, AVM resection, microvascular decompression or STA-MCA bypass; remimazolam TIVA with flumazenil
reversal at emergence versus propofol TIVA, both with remifentanil. **Primary outcome: cumulative incidence
of delirium within 5 days, assessed twice daily by CAM-ICU or 3D-CAM.** **Prespecified noninferiority margin:
10 percentage points.**

**Result, among 686 patients in the modified intention-to-treat analysis:**

| | Remimazolam | Propofol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delirium within 5 days | **44 / 344 (12.8%)** | **37 / 342 (10.8%)** |
| Hypotensive burden below MAP 65 mm Hg (median) | **0 mm Hg·min** | **7.5 mm Hg·min** |
| Extubated in the operating room | **85.2%** | **67.3%** |

**Risk difference 1.97 percentage points (95% CI −2.85 to 6.80), one-sided P < 0.001 for noninferiority.**
The interval is exactly symmetric at 4.82 points either side, and the stated difference equals the difference
of the two proportions precisely.

**And the authors' own closing caveat is the most important sentence in the abstract:**

> **Interpretation should take into account that the observed incidence of postoperative delirium was lower
> than anticipated when the noninferiority margin was defined.**

**That is exactly right, and here is what it amounts to in numbers.** **A 10-point absolute margin against an
observed control rate of 10.8% is a relative margin of 92%** — the trial was built to declare noninferiority
even if remimazolam almost doubled the delirium rate. **And the upper bound of the confidence interval,
6.80 points, is 63% of the control rate**, so the data remain compatible with a 63% relative increase in
delirium. **The conclusion "noninferior" is true against the stated margin and should not be read as "no
difference".**

**My reading.** **The primary outcome is a safety check that passed weakly, and the trial's real content is
in the two secondary outcomes.** A median hypotensive burden of **zero** against 7.5 mm Hg·min, and **18 more
patients extubated in theatre per hundred**, are large, unambiguous, operationally meaningful differences in a
population where hypotension and delayed emergence are the specific hazards. **The honest summary is:
remimazolam gives markedly better haemodynamics and faster emergence in neurovascular surgery, and this trial
does not show that it causes more delirium — but neither does it exclude a substantial increase, and the
authors say so.** **That is a well-conducted trial that reports its own limitation instead of letting the
word "noninferior" do the work.**

**Archive context: this is the first remimazolam randomised trial here.** Remimazolam has appeared twice as
guidance and once as a lead — the ASGE clinical practice update in the
[17 October guidelines entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-17/), and a bronchoscopy sedation
comparison added to the brief queue two days ago.

## 2. The same comparator, a different alternative, and a real effect

**[Dexmedetomidine versus propofol for the prevention of postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing
regional anaesthesia: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.redare.2026.502211)** —
*Revista Española de Anestesiología y Reanimación*, **first published 29 October 2026**. PRISMA, PROSPERO
registered.

**Five randomised trials, 1,613 patients aged 60 or over, sedation during regional anaesthesia.**

> The incidence of POD was **7.7% in the dexmedetomidine groups and 16.7% in the propofol groups** …
> **RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.36–0.64; p < 0.001**, with **low heterogeneity (I² = 13.4%)**. Egger's test did not show
> significant evidence of publication bias, and **trial sequential analysis suggested that the available
> evidence is sufficient. The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate according to GRADE.**

**The interval is exactly symmetric on the log scale**, and the pooled risk ratio of 0.48 sits close to the
ratio of the crude rates, 0.46. **An absolute reduction of 9.0 percentage points gives a number needed to
treat of about 11.**

**Read this against item 1 and the pair is instructive.** **Two papers published the same day, both comparing
a sedative with propofol for postoperative delirium, reaching very different-looking conclusions — and the
difference is mostly in the question.** **The remimazolam trial asked "not worse" and set a margin that was
92% of the control rate. The dexmedetomidine review asked "better", and found a 52% relative reduction with
moderate certainty, low heterogeneity, no detected publication bias, and a trial sequential analysis saying
the data suffice.** **On the evidence as published, dexmedetomidine is the better-supported choice for
avoiding delirium, and remimazolam's case rests on haemodynamics and emergence rather than on delirium.**

**Two cautions, and neither is small.** **The populations are not the same** — dexmedetomidine here is
sedation for regional anaesthesia in patients over 60, and remimazolam is general anaesthesia for intracranial
surgery; **the comparison above is between questions and designs, not a head-to-head.** **And this archive
already holds the other side of the dexmedetomidine ledger:** the
[25 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-25/) reported a dexmedetomidine delirium meta-analysis
whose outcome joined delirium and cognitive impairment with a slash at 24 hours in patients given a sedative,
and the [27 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-27/) reported dexmedetomidine causing
significantly more intraoperative hypotension than its comparators during pulsed field ablation.
**Dexmedetomidine's delirium benefit is the best-supported thing about it, and its haemodynamic cost is the
recurring complaint — which is precisely the trade remimazolam is offering to reverse.**

## 3. A randomised trial whose randomisation broke on the one variable that matters

**[Perioperative Continuation Versus Withholding of Angiotensin Receptor Blockers and Postoperative Delirium
in Elderly Patients: A Randomized Exploratory Trial](https://doi.org/10.1177/00912174261501856)** —
*International Journal of Psychiatry in Medicine*, **first published 29 October 2026**.

**Patients aged 65 or over on chronic ARB therapy undergoing elective surgery, randomised to perioperative
continuation or temporary withholding; delirium assessed daily for 5 days with CAM; severity by DRS-R-98;
plasma NfL, GFAP, IL-6, IL-8 and CRP as exploratory biomarkers.**

**44 randomised, 42 analysed, 21 per group.**

| | ARB continued | ARB withheld |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delirium | **4 / 21 (19.1%)** | **12 / 21 (57.1%)** |
| DRS-R-98, median (IQR) | **0 (0–7)** | **18 (5–26)** |
| Delirium duration, median (IQR) | **0 (0–40) h** | **40 (12–100) h** |
| **Orthopaedic procedures** | **57.1% (12 of 21)** | **95.2% (20 of 21)** |

**Risk difference −38.1 percentage points (95% CI −59.7 to −8.7, P = 0.025)**, which equals the difference of
the two proportions exactly.

**And then the row that governs everything above it:**

> **Surgical category was markedly imbalanced between groups, with orthopedic procedures accounting for 57.1%
> of the ARB continuation group and 95.2% of the ARB withholding group (P = 0.009).**

**Twelve orthopaedic cases against twenty, in groups of twenty-one.** **Orthopaedic surgery in the elderly —
hip and knee work above all — is the highest-delirium setting in perioperative medicine**, so randomisation
failed on the single strongest confounder for the outcome. **With 21 per group that is not bad luck
compounding a good design; it is the expected behaviour of randomisation at this size, and it means the
38-point difference is unattributable.**

**The authors say so, unambiguously, twice:**

> the marked imbalance in surgical category and the limited sample size **preclude independent attribution of
> these differences to ARB management.** These findings are **hypothesis-generating**…
>
> These biomarker findings are exploratory and **should not be interpreted as evidence of causal or
> mechanistic effects.**

**My reading.** **This is a 42-patient exploratory trial that reports a dramatic result and then tells the
reader not to believe it, and the second half is the part to respect.** **The question is a good one** —
whether to hold an ARB on the morning of surgery is a decision made thousands of times a day, it is already
contested on haemodynamic grounds, and a delirium signal would be a genuine new consideration. **The biomarker
pattern is coherent in direction**: smaller rises in neurofilament light chain and GFAP, both markers of
neuronal and glial injury, with IL-8 but not IL-6 or CRP after false-discovery correction. **But a coherent
exploratory pattern in 42 patients whose groups differ 12-to-20 on surgical category establishes nothing, and
the paper is explicit that it is not trying to.**

**One technical note:** the risk-difference interval is **asymmetric** — 21.6 points below the estimate and
29.4 above it. **That is expected rather than wrong** for a risk difference computed by an exact or
continuity-corrected method at this sample size, and is recorded as another case where this archive's
symmetry check should not fire.

**Archive context.** This is the second paper in three days on a **modifiable preoperative medication exposure
and postoperative delirium** — the [28 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-28/) carried the
anticholinergic drug burden meta-analysis, 10,457 participants, OR 1.22 per burden point. **The contrast in
evidential weight between the two is the useful thing: a 10,000-patient graded association on one drug class,
and a 42-patient broken randomisation on another.**

## 4. A marker with an area under the curve of 0.871 that may be measuring the sedation rather than the brain

**[Impact of burst suppression duration on delirium and its diagnostic value in mechanically ventilated ICU
patients](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10877-026-01508-x)** — *Journal of Clinical Monitoring and Computing*,
**first published 29 October 2026**. Prospective observational, **300 mechanically ventilated adults**, single
centre, April 2021 to October 2024, **continuous video-EEG** with burst suppression duration quantified from
the recordings; delirium by CAM-ICU.

**130 of 300 (43.3%) developed delirium.** Burst suppression duration was **median 81.1 s (IQR 41.5–148.5) in
the delirium group against 17.2 s (IQR 5.8–32.9)**, P < 0.001.

| Independent risk factor | Adjusted OR | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burst suppression duration (per second) | **1.018** | 1.012–1.023 |
| BIS | 0.964 | 0.946–0.982 |
| Age | 1.024 | 1.002–1.047 |
| Hypertension | **1.748** | **1.002–3.051** |

**Discrimination: AUC 0.871 (0.829–0.913); optimal cutoff 41.5 s giving sensitivity 80.8% and specificity
78.2%; combined with BIS, AUC 0.914.**

**Three things the abstract does not do, and this archive's standing rules say to do them.**

**First, the predictive values.** **Sensitivity 80.8%, specificity 78.2% and a prevalence of 43.3% give a
positive predictive value of 73.9% and a negative predictive value of 84.2%.** At this unusually high
prevalence those are respectable numbers — **which is worth saying, because the same test in a population with
10% delirium would have a positive predictive value near 29%.** **The performance is a property of the
population as much as of the marker.**

**Second, the effect size in usable units.** **An odds ratio of 1.018 per second is uninterpretable as
printed.** Across the observed difference in medians — 63.9 seconds — it compounds to an odds ratio of about
**3.1**, which is the number a clinician would want.

**Third, and this is the substantive problem: burst suppression is a consequence of sedation depth, which is
itself the strongest modifiable risk factor for ICU delirium.** **So burst suppression duration may not be an
independent marker of cerebral vulnerability at all — it may be a mediator on the path from deep sedation to
delirium.** The paper describes it as *"an electroencephalographic pattern reflecting profound cerebral
dysfunction"*, which is one reading; the other is that it reflects how much sedative the patient received.
**And adjusting for BIS in the same multivariable model does not resolve this — BIS is another measure of the
same exposure**, so the two coefficients are competing to explain one thing. The near-significant hypertension
term, **OR 1.748 with a lower bound of 1.002**, is a reminder of how little room this model has.

**My reading.** **The design cannot support the word "diagnostic" in the title, and it is the design rather
than the analysis that is at fault.** Burst suppression was measured during the same ICU stay in which
delirium was assessed, so this is concurrent discrimination, not prediction — **it tells you that patients who
spent longer in burst suppression were more likely to be delirious, which is a strong argument for avoiding
burst suppression and a weak one for using it as a test.** **The actionable version of this finding is
already the recommendation: keep patients out of burst suppression.** That it discriminates with an AUC of
0.871 is interesting; that it is modifiable is what matters, and the paper's own framing as *"a practical
quantitative tool for early delirium risk stratification"* points away from the more useful conclusion.

## 5. A protocol that halved delirium, a comparison across time, and an outcome that is named and never reported

**[Impact of a Nurse-Led, Target-Directed Light Sedation Strategy on the Incidence of Delirium in Septic
Patients in the Emergency Intensive Care Unit](https://doi.org/10.1177/08850666261496192)** — *Journal of
Intensive Care Medicine*, **first published 29 October 2026**.

**221 consecutive septic patients ventilated for more than 24 hours at a single centre, January 2022 to
May 2024**, assigned **by a sedation protocol change** to conventional sedation (n = 123) or a nurse-led
target-directed light sedation strategy (n = 98).

| Outcome | Nurse-led | Conventional |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Delirium** | **20.4% (20 of 98)** | **37.4% (46 of 123)** |
| 28-day mortality | 28.6% (28 of 98) | 26.0% (32 of 123) |
| Ventilator-free days to 28 | — no significant difference — | |
| Ventilation duration, EICU stay | both shorter (P < 0.05) | |

**Adjusted OR for delirium 0.428 (95% CI 0.215–0.854, P = 0.016)**, log-symmetric; crude absolute reduction
**17.0 percentage points**. Airway-related adverse events did not differ. The protective association held
across age, severity and comorbidity strata.

**My reading, and the design is the whole of it.** **This is a before-and-after comparison described as a
protocol change: the two groups are sequential cohorts, not concurrent ones.** Everything else that changed at
that centre between January 2022 and May 2024 — staffing, case mix, the tail of the pandemic, familiarity with
the ventilator, any other quality initiative — is loaded onto the exposure and cannot be adjusted away by a
logistic model. **Subgroup consistency across age, severity and comorbidity is reassuring about confounding by
those variables and says nothing about confounding by time.** The authors call for multicentre randomised
confirmation, which is the right ask.

**The internal pattern is at least coherent: delirium down 17 points, ventilation duration and ICU stay
shorter, mortality unchanged (28.6% against 26.0%, P = 0.671), and no excess airway events.** **A process
intervention that shortens time on the ventilator without changing survival is the expected shape**, and the
absence of a mortality claim is to the paper's credit.

**But one defect should be named, because it is the third instance in five days.** **Delirium-free days to 28
is listed among the secondary outcomes and no result for it appears anywhere in the abstract.** **A
delirium-free-days figure is the one number that would distinguish "fewer patients became delirious" from
"delirium was shorter", and it is the outcome the intervention most plausibly affects.** **This archive
flagged the same defect in the prone-position airway trial on 26 October, which named conversion of airway
technique as a primary outcome and gave no result, and in the anticoagulation meta-analysis yesterday, which
named six secondary outcomes and reported none.** **Three papers in five days naming an outcome and omitting
its result is no longer a coincidence about one abstract.**
