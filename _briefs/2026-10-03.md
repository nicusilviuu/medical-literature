## 1. Relative, not absolute: 151,036 patients and 12 ways of defining hypotension

**Journal · Published:** *Anaesthesia*, **2 October 2026** — **one day old.** Retrospective multicentre
cohort, three Chinese hospitals.
Wang, Gu, Liu, Zhuang, Peng, Xiang, Cai, Liu, Lv

> Intra-operative hypotension is common during general anaesthesia, but its association with postoperative
> pneumonia remains uncertain, **partly because hypotension has been defined using different thresholds.** We
> evaluated whether **absolute thresholds or relative reductions from baseline** mean arterial pressure were
> associated more strongly with postoperative pneumonia…
>
> …**151,036 adults** undergoing non-cardiac surgery under general anaesthesia at three Chinese hospitals.
> Intra-operative hypotension was assessed using **12 metrics across nine thresholds**, including absolute
> mean arterial pressure thresholds of < 50 mmHg to < 70 mmHg and relative reductions of 20–50% from
> baseline. The primary outcome was postoperative pneumonia within 30 days.
>
> …postoperative pneumonia occurred in **3486 (2.3%). Relative reductions in mean arterial pressure were
> more consistently associated with postoperative pneumonia than absolute thresholds.** At a 20% reduction
> from baseline, the number of episodes was associated with pneumonia (**subdistribution hazard ratio 1.079,
> 95%CI 1.050–1.109**), as was the presence of hypotension (**sHR 1.108, 95%CI 1.068–1.149**). **Absolute
> thresholds were not consistently associated with postoperative pneumonia after adjustment.**
> **Duration-related metrics were generally less consistent than depth- or recurrence-related metrics.**
> Dose–response analyses showed that **risk increased rapidly at low exposure levels and then plateaued
> across relative thresholds of 20–40%.**
>
> …**maintaining intra-operative blood pressure close to baseline may be more relevant to pulmonary risk
> than relying on fixed absolute thresholds alone.**

**Interpretation.** This matters because of what this archive has spent the last week reporting. **Every
negative pressure-targeting result here used an absolute threshold.**

| Study | Target used | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Bayesian meta-analysis, 15 RCTs](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-29/) | Absolute MAP targets | Mortality **OR 1.00** |
| [HPI vs MAP ≤ 73 mmHg RCT](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-29/) | Absolute, 73 mmHg | **No superiority** |
| [PRESSURE trial, 1,900 children](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/) | Percentile-for-age, i.e. absolute | **No difference** |
| This cohort | **Relative, % fall from baseline** | **Relative tracks pneumonia; absolute does not** |

**So there is now a candidate explanation for a week of nulls: the trials may have been targeting the wrong
quantity.** A MAP of 65 mmHg is a different physiological insult in someone whose baseline is 75 than in
someone whose baseline is 110, and a fixed threshold treats them as identical. The paper's own conclusion —
*maintaining pressure close to baseline* — is a different intervention from *keeping pressure above a
number.*

**Four reasons to hold that loosely, and the first is the most important.**

**The effect sizes are very small.** A subdistribution hazard ratio of **1.079 per episode** on an outcome
with a **2.3%** incidence is a tiny absolute effect. This is not a paper showing that relative targeting
would save lives; it is a paper showing that relative definitions correlate with harm more consistently than
absolute ones.

**Twelve metrics across nine thresholds is up to 108 comparisons**, and the abstract reports no correction
for multiplicity. "More consistently associated" is a claim about a pattern across many tests, which is
exactly where a pattern can appear by chance. **The dose–response plateau across 20–40% is the part that
argues against pure noise** — a real exposure–response relationship that flattens is harder to generate by
multiple testing than a single significant threshold.

**Relative reduction is partly a measurement of the baseline.** A patient recorded with a high preoperative
baseline is a patient with untreated or labile hypertension, and that is itself a pneumonia risk factor.
Some of this association may be the baseline doing the work rather than the fall from it — the same
structural problem this archive noted in the [DO₂i cardiopulmonary bypass
study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/), where the delivery index is partly a haemoglobin measurement.

**And it is retrospective, in three hospitals in one country, with pneumonia as the only outcome.** Nothing
here tests mortality, and postoperative pneumonia is an outcome whose ascertainment varies between systems.

**What would settle it is a trial randomising relative to absolute targets**, and none exists. Until then
the honest statement is narrow: **the hypotension literature's choice of parameterisation is not neutral,
and the parameterisation that the trials chose is the one that performs worst in observational data.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.70426)

## 2. Four anaesthetics, sixteen rats, and a touchscreen vigilance task

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **1 October 2026** — two days old. Laboratory study.
Choi, Park, Vincent, Solt

> Cognitive recovery following general anesthesia is clinically important but incompletely understood.
> **Rodent studies in anesthesia research have primarily examined memory and learning, while vigilance and
> other neurocognitive domains remain underexplored.** … we employed the **rodent Continuous Performance
> Task (rCPT)**, a touchscreen-based translational paradigm…
>
> **Sixteen adult Sprague Dawley rats (8 male, 8 female)** were trained to proficiency… loss of righting
> was induced by **sevoflurane (3% for 20 min), propofol (10 mg/kg i.v. bolus), ketamine (50 mg/kg i.v. over
> 10 min), or dexmedetomidine (20 µg/kg i.v. bolus), delivered in a randomized order every other week.** …
> performance was recorded for four hours. **Vigilance recovery was defined as 20 consecutive trials with an
> average hit rate > 0.6.**
>
> **The latency to recover vigilance was delayed following ketamine and dexmedetomidine compared to baseline
> (Hazard Ratio in ketamine, 5.34; 95% CI, 2.07, 13.76; Hazard Ratio in dexmedetomidine, 5.33; 95% CI, 2.08,
> 13.62; both p<.0001).** Reward retrieval latency **recovered 4 hours after RORR following ketamine**
> (−24.14%; 95% CI, −54.69, 17.89%) **but not … following dexmedetomidine (−47.85%; 95% CI, −71.94,
> −20.23%).**
>
> **Dexmedetomidine showed prolonged impairment across behavioral measures, including visual processing speed
> (hit latency) and reward retrieval, suggesting sustained effects on psychomotor function, motor
> coordination, and behavioral engagement that persisted for at least 4 hours after RORR.**

**Interpretation.** The design is the strong part: **each rat received all four agents in randomised order**,
so every animal is its own control and between-animal variation is removed. **Sixteen animals with four
within-subject exposures is a better-powered comparison than the n suggests**, and sex was balanced 8/8.

**The result is counter-intuitive in a specific way.** Dexmedetomidine is used partly because it is believed
to give a cleaner, more rousable sedation than alternatives. Here **it produced the most prolonged
impairment of the four agents** — and the pattern separates: ketamine delayed vigilance recovery but reward
retrieval was back by four hours, while **dexmedetomidine's deficit persisted on every measure at four
hours after the righting reflex returned.**

**Note also what "recovery" means here, because it is the useful methodological point.** Return of righting
reflex is the rodent analogue of "awake," and the entire finding lives in the gap **after** that point —
the animals were righting themselves while still failing a vigilance task. **A patient who meets discharge
criteria and a patient whose vigilance has recovered are not the same patient**, and this design makes that
gap measurable. It pairs directly with [the human cortical assembloid paper reported two days
ago](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-01/): two mechanistic models of anaesthetic action in three days, one
in vitro and one behavioural, both probing what the clinical endpoints do not capture.

**The limits are the obvious ones and should not be softened.** These are **rats**, the doses are single
boluses or a 20-minute exposure rather than clinical regimens, and a touchscreen hit rate is not a human
cognitive outcome. **Nothing here licenses a change in practice.** What it does is generate a specific,
testable claim about dexmedetomidine's recovery profile — and item 3 is the largest human trial available
to test it against.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1097/aln.0000000000006398)

## 3. A2B — 1,404 patients, 41 ICUs, and no advantage for either α2 agonist

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 July 2025** — **fifteen months old**, 43 citations, in PubMed Central.
Pragmatic, open-label, phase 3 randomised trial across **41 UK ICUs**, recruiting December 2018 to October
2023.
Walsh, Parker, Aitken, McKenzie, Emerson, Boyd, Macdonald, Beveridge, Giddings, Hope, Irvine, Tuck, Lone et al.

> **Importance** Whether α2-adrenergic receptor agonist-based sedation, compared with propofol-based
> sedation, reduces time to extubation … is uncertain.
>
> **Design** … adults within 48 hours of starting mechanical ventilation, receiving propofol plus an opioid,
> expected to require ventilation for 48 hours or longer… The bedside algorithms targeted a **Richmond
> Agitation-Sedation Scale score of −2 to 1**… **If required, supplemental use of propofol was permitted.**
>
> **Results** Among the **1404 patients** (mean age 59.2; 64% male; mean APACHE II 20.3), the
> subdistribution hazard ratio for time to successful extubation was **1.09 (95% CI, 0.96–1.25; P = .20)**
> for dexmedetomidine (n = 457) vs propofol (n = 471) and **1.05 (0.95–1.17; P = .34)** for clonidine
> (n = 476)… Median time to successful extubation was **136 (117–150) hours for dexmedetomidine, 146
> (124–168) for clonidine, and 162 (136–170) for propofol.** …
>
> Among the secondary outcomes, **agitation occurred at a higher rate with dexmedetomidine vs propofol (risk
> ratio 1.54 [1.21–1.97])** and with clonidine (1.55 [1.22–1.97]). **Severe bradycardia (heart rate
> < 50/min) was higher with dexmedetomidine (RR 1.62 [1.36–1.93])** and clonidine (1.58 [1.33–1.88]).
> **Mortality was similar over 180 days.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **neither dexmedetomidine nor clonidine was superior to propofol in reducing time to
> successful extubation.**

**Interpretation.** **The medians look like a win and the statistics say otherwise, and the gap between those
two readings is the most instructive thing in the trial.** Median time to extubation was **136 hours with
dexmedetomidine against 162 with propofol — 26 hours, more than a day.** But the confidence intervals
overlap substantially (117–150 against 136–170), the subdistribution hazard ratio is **1.09 with a CI from
0.96 to 1.25**, and P = .20. **A 26-hour difference in medians that a 1,404-patient trial cannot distinguish
from chance is a reminder that medians are not estimates of effect.**

And the rule this archive wrote on 29 September applies in the other direction too: **a failed superiority
test is not a demonstration of equivalence.** A2B does not show the α2 agonists are the same as propofol; it
shows superiority was not demonstrated.

**What is demonstrated is harm, and it is the harm a guideline already warned about.** Severe bradycardia
**RR 1.62 (1.36–1.93)** and agitation **RR 1.54 (1.21–1.97)**, both with intervals well clear of 1. On
[7 September this archive reported the ASA 2025 practice advisory on older
adults](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-07/), which recommends *considering dexmedetomidine for
delirium prophylaxis, balancing benefit against cardiovascular risk.* **A2B is that caveat quantified in
1,404 patients.**

**The accumulation is now worth stating plainly, across four entries in this archive:**

| Reported | Document | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [7 Sep](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-07/) | ASA 2025 practice advisory | Consider dexmedetomidine, **balance against cardiovascular risk** |
| [12 Sep](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-12/) | RCT preprint, 300 CABG patients | Delirium **15.3% vs 20.0%**, OR 0.724 (0.398–1.317) — **null** |
| Today (item 3) | A2B, 1,404 patients, 41 ICUs | **No extubation benefit**; agitation RR 1.54; **severe bradycardia RR 1.62** |
| Today (item 2) | 16 rats, four agents, within-subject | **Most prolonged impairment of the four agents** |

**Four documents, four levels of evidence, and the reputed advantage has not appeared in any of them.**

**One caution against over-reading that table, because the findings are not the same phenomenon.** More
*agitation* with dexmedetomidine suggests **inadequate** sedation; prolonged impairment in rats suggests
**residual** drug effect. Those point in opposite directions mechanistically, and I am not going to pretend
they converge. **What they share is narrower and still substantial: none of these studies found the benefit
dexmedetomidine is prescribed for.** The delirium question specifically remains open — A2B's primary outcome
was extubation time, not delirium, and the 300-patient preprint was underpowered for a modest effect.

**Two design features worth noting before anyone treats A2B as a clean head-to-head.** It is **open-label**,
and **supplemental propofol was permitted in the intervention arms** — so the comparison is between
sedation *strategies*, not drugs, and contamination runs in one direction only. Also, the trial used the
**same subdistribution hazard framework** as item 1, which is the right choice when death competes with the
event of interest and is worth recognising as it becomes standard.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2025.7200)

## 4. Sedation trajectories and the ICU's broken clock

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Critical Care*, **30 September 2026** — three days old. Retrospective
cohort with external validation. **Carried on the outstanding list for three runs and reported now under the
rule that an item whose abstract is already retrieved should not be deferred again.**
Zhang, Wang, Wang, Chen, Zhang

> **Background** Whether ICU sedation strategies are associated with circadian rhythmicity of vital signs,
> and whether such disruption mediates delirium, remains unclear.
>
> **Methods** … **MIMIC-IV (n = 13,592** surgical and mixed medical-surgical ICU patients), with supportive
> external validation in **eICU-CRD (n = 12,004)**, **multivariate dynamic time warping (DTW)-based
> clustering classified four sedation trajectories over 48 h.** Rhythmicity parameters of four vital signs
> were quantified using **Lomb-Scargle, wavelet, entropy, and cosinor methods.** Marginal structural models
> estimated associations, and **exploratory** mediation analysis examined indirect pathways.
>
> **Results Sustained high-dose propofol showed the weakest vital-sign rhythmicity.** Compared with this
> group, the early high-dose propofol and propofol-to-dexmedetomidine transition groups had higher spectral
> power for heart rate (β = 0.033 and 0.015) and temperature (β = 0.055 and 0.020; all P < 0.001). With
> CAM-ICU as the primary delirium measure, **temperature wavelet power formed an exploratory indirect pathway
> (proportion mediated 23.5%, 95% CI 16.9–31.0%)**, **though this ranged from 0% to 34% across assumptions.**
> In eICU-CRD, temperature-derived parameters were **directionally consistent** (Cohen's d 0.263 to 0.426).
>
> **Conclusions** … **Temperature-derived rhythmicity was consistent with an exploratory indirect pathway to
> delirium, but estimates depended on analytic assumptions. These proxies should not be interpreted as direct
> measures of central circadian function.**

**Interpretation.** **This paper is included as much for how it reports itself as for what it found**, and
that is not a backhanded compliment.

The headline available here was obvious and tempting: *sedation disrupts the body clock and that is how it
causes delirium, with 23.5% of the effect mediated.* The authors declined it. They label the mediation
**exploratory**, give the range across analytic assumptions — **0% to 34%** — and state in the conclusion
that **their measures are proxies and not direct measures of central circadian function.** A mediation
proportion whose plausible range includes zero is a hypothesis, and they say so.

**Set that against a number from item 1 for contrast.** Both papers are large retrospective analyses
reporting modest associations. Item 1 runs up to 108 metric–threshold combinations and reports no
multiplicity correction in its abstract; this one runs a mediation analysis and volunteers that the estimate
collapses to zero under some assumptions. **The second is the more trustworthy document, and it is the one
whose headline is weaker.**

**The substantive finding worth keeping is the simplest one: sustained high-dose propofol had the weakest
vital-sign rhythmicity**, and the trajectories that moved off sustained high-dose propofol — including the
transition to dexmedetomidine — had more rhythmic heart rate and temperature. **Note the direction, given
items 2 and 3:** here the propofol-to-dexmedetomidine transition looks *better* than sustained high-dose
propofol on a physiological proxy. That is a genuine counterweight in the table above, and it is one more
reason not to read those four documents as a verdict on the drug.

**Two structural limits.** The exposure is a **trajectory derived by clustering**, not an allocated
treatment, so it is confounded by why the sedation was given — the sickest patients receive sustained
high-dose sedation, and the sickest patients are both less rhythmic and more delirious. And **vital-sign
periodicity is a proxy for circadian function in a setting where temperature and heart rate are being
actively manipulated** by cooling, vasoactives and ventilation. The authors name the second limitation
themselves; the first is inherent to the design.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155759)
