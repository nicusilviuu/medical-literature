## 1. Phenylephrine, ephedrine and the brain in the beach chair position

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **21 September 2026** — **new today**
Samaei, Shoemaker, Kamar, Milej, Drosdowech, **Murkin**, Diop, St Lawrence, Chui (St Joseph's
Hospital, London, Ontario)

The premise, from the abstract:

> **Ephedrine has been suggested to improve cerebral microcirculation and perfusion compared with
> phenylephrine. However, prospective intraoperative data are lacking**, and possible interactions
> with anesthetic choice remain untested.

**Forty adults undergoing shoulder surgery in the beach chair position**, recruited February to
November 2024, randomised in a **2 × 2 factorial design** to anaesthetic maintenance (**propofol vs
sevoflurane**) and vasopressor strategy (**phenylephrine infusion vs ephedrine boluses**).
Co-primary outcomes were **relative tissue oxygen saturation (rStO₂)** and the **relative cerebral
metabolic rate of oxygen (rCMRO₂)**, measured with an **in-house hybrid optical system combining
time-resolved near-infrared spectroscopy and diffuse correlation spectroscopy**. Only the **first 30
minutes after positioning** were analysed. Linear mixed-effects models.

> Of the 40 patients, 39 were analyzed. **Neither anesthetic nor vasopressor choice showed a
> significant main effect** on average rStO₂ or rCMRO₂ during the 30-min period. However, analysis of
> temporal changes revealed **rStO₂ declined over time** (β = -0.005 per minute; P = 0.001), while
> **rCMRO₂ increased** (β = 3.2 per minute; P < 0.001). There was a **time by vasopressor interaction
> for rStO₂** (P = 0.004), with **phenylephrine producing a 9% greater decline during 30 min compared
> to ephedrine**, while for rCMRO₂ (β = 2.2; 95% CI, 0.6 to 3.8; P = 0.007), **phenylephrine produced
> a 70% increase during 30 min.**

Authors' conclusion: no overall effect of either choice, *"suggesting that initial cerebral
physiologic responses were comparable across treatment combinations. However, **phenylephrine was
associated with progressive cerebral desaturation and increasing metabolic demand over time.**"*

**Interpretation.** The most interesting anaesthesia paper in a fortnight, and it needs reading in
the order the authors wrote it: **the trial is negative on its co-primary outcomes**, and the
striking finding is an interaction with time.

**That ordering matters because a time-by-treatment interaction on a co-primary outcome is a
different kind of claim from the co-primary comparison itself.** Averaged over 30 minutes, the four
combinations looked alike. The divergence is in the trajectory, in 39 patients, over half an hour.
It is a hypothesis-generating result presented as such, and the authors' conclusion is appropriately
worded — *"associated with"*, not *"causes"*.

**With that caveat, the finding is exactly what the perfusion-not-pressure thread in this archive
would predict.** Both arms were given a vasopressor to hold blood pressure, and **both presumably
held it** — that is what these drugs are titrated to. Yet the brain's oxygenation diverged. This is
the same lesson as the [carotid cannula study on 19 September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-19/),
where flow varied three- to four-fold at the same nominal cannula size, and as the
[Hypotension Prediction Index analysis](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-10/), where the benefit
tracked treatment intensity rather than prediction: **the number on the monitor is not the thing you
care about, and an intervention that fixes the number can leave the thing unfixed.**

**The rCMRO₂ result deserves more scepticism than the rStO₂ result, and I want to be explicit that
this is my reading rather than something the abstract addresses.** A 70% rise in cerebral metabolic
rate over 30 minutes under general anaesthesia is not physiologically expected — anaesthesia
suppresses cerebral metabolism. In this technique rCMRO₂ is a *derived* quantity, computed from a
blood-flow index and an oxygen extraction term. If phenylephrine reduces cortical microvascular
flow while extraction rises to compensate, the computed metabolic rate can rise without cerebral
metabolism changing at all. **That would make the rCMRO₂ signal a restatement of the desaturation
rather than an independent finding** — which, if true, strengthens rather than weakens the paper's
practical message. The full text would settle it; the abstract does not.

**What this does not tell you is whether it matters.** Thirty minutes, 39 patients, no neurological
or cognitive outcomes. Beach-chair cerebral desaturation has a long and contested literature, and
this is a mechanistic contribution to it, not a resolution of it. The honest practical reading:
**if you are using a pure α-agonist to defend pressure in a seated patient and you have cerebral
oximetry on, watch the trend rather than the number.** That is a smaller claim than the headline
invites, and it is the one the data support.

*Murkin's presence in the author list is worth noting — he has spent a career on cerebral oximetry
in cardiac surgery, and this is that question moved into a different operation.*

## 2. Balanced crystalloids before ICU admission in sepsis — 3,688 patients

**Journal · Published:** *Chest*, **19 September 2026** — **new this week**
Kim, Kim, Baek, Kim, Ko, Suh, Lim, Jeon, Kim, and the **Korean Sepsis Alliance** investigators

> The optimal crystalloids for initial fluid resuscitation in sepsis remain uncertain. **Most
> previous studies have focused on intensive care unit (ICU), leaving the pre-ICU admission impact of
> crystalloid type unclear.**

Nationwide multicentre retrospective cohort, adults with sepsis, September 2019 to December 2023.
Patients receiving initial fluid resuscitation **before ICU admission** classified as balanced
crystalloid or 0.9% saline. Primary outcome **30-day mortality.**

> Among **3,688 patients** (mean age 71.9 years; 58% male), **1,381 (37.4%) received balanced
> crystalloids and 2,307 (62.6%) received saline.** After propensity score matching, 1,335 patients
> remained in each group. **Balanced crystalloid use was associated with lower 30-day mortality,
> adjusted odds ratio 0.69 (95% CI 0.56-0.83)**, consistent after inverse probability of treatment
> weighting (**aOR 0.78; 95% CI 0.69-0.88**).

Consistent across age, sex, disease severity, liberal fluid management (>30 mL/kg pre-ICU) and
pre-ICU vasopressor use. **Larger where mechanism predicts it would be**: in patients **without
chronic kidney disease**, those given fluids **in the emergency department or ward** rather than
theatre or another ICU, those with **baseline chloride ≤110 mmol/L**, and those with **baseline pH
<7.35**. Also associated with **less CRRT during ICU stay, less renal replacement therapy at ICU
discharge, and more 28-day ventilator-free days.**

**Interpretation.** A good question, asked in the right place, with an effect size that should stop
you.

**The question is genuinely underexplored.** SMART, SALT-ED, BaSICS and PLUS all randomised in the
ICU or on admission; the litres given in the ambulance and the emergency department before anyone
writes an ICU order have largely escaped study, and in sepsis those are the litres that arrive
earliest and in the greatest hurry.

**The effect size is the problem.** An adjusted odds ratio of **0.69** for 30-day mortality from a
choice between two crystalloids is far larger than anything the randomised literature has produced —
SMART found roughly a one percentage point absolute difference in its composite renal outcome, and
neither BaSICS nor PLUS showed a mortality benefit. **A 31% relative reduction in death from the anion in the bag is not a plausible
treatment effect; it is the signature of confounding.** The IPTW estimate drifting to 0.78 points
the same way: the two estimators disagree by more than they should if the adjustment were complete.

**What is credible is the subgroup pattern, and it is the reason to keep reading.** The association
was stronger in patients with a **normal baseline chloride** and an **acidaemic baseline pH** — that
is, in exactly the patients in whom a chloride load could plausibly do harm and in whom it has
somewhere to go. Subgroup findings that line up with mechanism are worth more than subgroup findings
that do not, though they remain subgroup findings.

**My reading: this is a well-constructed argument that the pre-ICU window deserves a randomised
trial, dressed as an answer.** The right response is not to change the fluid in the ambulance on
this evidence; it is to notice that **62.6% of septic patients in a national cohort were still
getting saline before ICU admission** in 2019–2023, years after the ICU trials, and ask why the
pre-hospital and ED end of the pathway moves so much more slowly than the ICU end.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.09.008) · PMID 42762986

## 3. Vasopressors probably are not what causes the gangrene

**Journal · Published:** *European Heart Journal — Cardiovascular Pharmacotherapy*, **19 September
2026** — **new this week**. A review
**Levy**, Shaw, Connors, Draxler, Maier, **Iba**, **Warkentin**

> Ischemic limb necrosis and resulting **symmetrical peripheral gangrene (SPG)** are devastating
> complications of severe shock states, particularly septic shock… These complications usually occur
> in patients requiring vasopressor support… **In this setting, however, thromboinflammatory
> microvascular injury represents the principal mechanism underlying ischemic tissue injury.**

The mechanism, quoted:

> Life-threatening shock is characterized by **endothelial injury, glycocalyx disruption,
> disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), impaired fibrinolysis, and ischemic hepatic injury
> ("shock liver") with endogenous natural anticoagulant factor depletion (antithrombin, protein C,
> protein S)** that promotes diffuse thromboinflammatory microvascular thrombosis and **impaired
> tissue perfusion independent of vasopressor therapy.**

And the discriminating observation:

> **Most critically ill patients receiving prolonged or high-dose vasopressor therapy do not develop
> ischemic limb necrosis in the absence of severe coagulopathy, DIC, or shock liver.** In contrast,
> patients developing SPG typically exhibit **DIC (severe thrombocytopenia, greatly elevated D-dimer
> levels), hepatic dysfunction, and multiorgan failure.**

The review also *"evaluate[s] key limitations and confounders in the literature on
vasopressor-associated ischemia."*

**Interpretation.** A review rather than new data, reported because it takes a piece of received
bedside wisdom apart carefully, and because the author list is the one you would want on it —
**Warkentin** has defined much of the SPG literature, **Iba** much of the DIC literature, **Levy**
the perioperative haemostasis literature.

**The argument is a clean piece of causal reasoning.** The drug and the outcome co-occur, but the
outcome occurs almost exclusively in the subgroup with DIC, thrombocytopenia, high D-dimer and shock
liver — and prolonged high-dose vasopressor therapy without that coagulopathy mostly does not produce
it. **Confounding by severity is the obvious explanation for the association, and the review says so
directly.** What makes patients need 0.8 μg/kg/min of noradrenaline is the same thing that makes
them clot off their digits.

**It matters clinically because the wrong causal model changes what you do.** If the vasopressor is
the cause, you reduce the dose — in a patient whose organs are already hypoperfused. If
thromboinflammatory microvascular thrombosis with natural anticoagulant depletion is the cause, dose
reduction does not help and the question becomes one about the coagulopathy.

**This connects directly to a lead sitting on this archive's outstanding list** — the *Shock* paper
of 9 September on **early antithrombin depletion after trauma**, which found reduced antithrombin in
32% of patients and tracked it to albumin and syndecan-1, a glycocalyx marker, rather than to
thrombin-antithrombin complex. The same three elements appear in this review's mechanism:
**glycocalyx disruption, natural anticoagulant depletion, microvascular thrombosis.** Two independent
routes to the same picture in two weeks.

*Access note: this is a review and the summary above is from its abstract, which is unusually
substantive. **No treatment recommendations are stated in the abstract** — the review says it
addresses clinical and treatment implications, and those are in the full text, which was not
reachable.*

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjcvp/pvag078) · PMID 42760619

## 4. Older but relevant: the 128 case reports the SGLT2 stop-rule was built on

**Journal · Published:** *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica*, **1 July 2026** — **just under three
months old**, not new. Reported now because [yesterday's guidelines
watch](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/) established what the perioperative SGLT2 guidance
actually says, and this is the evidence underneath it
Snel, Li, Jamaludin, Siegelaar, Holleman, Vriesendorp, DeVries, Hoekstra, **Preckel**, van Raalte,
**Hermanides**, Hulst

> The use of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) in the perioperative setting may lead
> to **SGLT2i-associated postoperative ketoacidosis (SAPKA)** in patients with type 2 diabetes.
> **Therefore, cessation of this drug is recommended before surgery.** We aimed to study reported
> cases to **assess the causality** of SGLT2i…

Method: systematic search for case reports of metabolic acidosis with ketones in perioperative SGLT2i
users; cases summarised, quality-assessed, and sent to **a panel of diabetes experts** who rated the
likelihood of SAPKA by questionnaire.

> **Ninety-three papers containing 128 case reports** fulfilled the inclusion criteria. The expert
> panel found SAPKA to be **'likely' in 53 (41%), 'possible' in 38 (30%) and 'unlikely' in 27 (21%)**
> cases; **10 cases (8%) could not be validated** due to insufficient data or implausible timing.
> **SAPKA was therefore considered likely or possible in 71% (91/128).** Common factors: **type 2
> diabetes (n = 115), impaired perioperative intake (n = 30) and insufficient insulin supplementation
> (n = 10).** Treatment with insulin was effective and **ketoacidosis resolved in all surviving
> patients**, although **significant morbidity, including ICU admission**, was reported in a
> substantial proportion.

Their conclusion: confirming SAPKA is difficult given variable reporting quality and perioperative
confounders, and **"focusing on early recognition and treatment represents a potential alternative
strategy to routine preoperative SGLT2i discontinuation, though this requires further prospective
evaluation."**

**Interpretation.** This is the paper that should be read alongside every guideline on this subject,
and it is the reason the SGLT2 story has been worth four entries in this archive.

**The whole edifice — an FDA instruction to stop the drug for 3–4 days, and every society document
that has had to respond to it — rests on 128 published case reports.** That is now stated as a
number rather than an impression. And when a diabetes expert panel read them, **a fifth were judged
unlikely to be SAPKA at all** and another 8% could not be assessed. The causal signal is real but
smaller and softer than the rule built on it.

**The common factors are more actionable than the headline.** Type 2 diabetes in 115 of 128 cases —
against an FDA instruction that applies **irrespective of a diabetes diagnosis**, as the
[SPAQI consensus noted yesterday](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/). Impaired perioperative
intake in 30. Insufficient insulin in 10. **Every one of those is a fasting-and-insulin problem
rather than a tablet problem**, which is precisely what the *BJA* editorial on the SPAQI consensus
argued — that euglycaemic ketoacidosis is *"driven more by metabolic context, particularly diabetes
mellitus and fasting, than by drug continuation alone."* Two independent analyses, the same
conclusion.

**And the outcome data cut both ways honestly.** Ketoacidosis **resolved in all surviving patients**
with insulin — the authors' argument for detection over prevention — but the qualifier *"all
surviving"* means some did not survive, and a substantial proportion needed intensive care. **This is
not a harmless complication that can simply be caught late.** The authors' proposal is early
recognition, not relaxed vigilance, and it requires point-of-care ketone testing to be available,
which the SPAQI consensus also makes a condition of its stratified approach.

**Read together with [the Basel discontinuation cohort](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-05/), the
picture is coherent for the first time**: the harm from stopping is a cardiac harm, measurable in a
large cohort with a dose-response; the harm from continuing is a metabolic harm, concentrated in
people with type 2 diabetes who fast badly, documented in 128 case reports of which 71% hold up.
**Those are not the same population, and a single blanket rule cannot be right for both.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1111/aas.70254) · PMID 42130079
