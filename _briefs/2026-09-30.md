Two blocks today, and they are not about the same thing.

The first is the **third consecutive day** on which a protocolised arterial pressure target fails to
change patient outcomes — this time in paediatric intensive care, in a trial nearly twenty times the
size of the one reported here yesterday.

The second opens a topic this archive has **never covered**: where the greenhouse gas emissions of
anaesthesia actually come from. Two papers appeared within two days of each other, and neither is
interpretable without a third that is eighteen months old. All three are below.

## 1. Permissive mean arterial pressure targets in critically ill children — the PRESSURE trial

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **28 September 2026** (**new this week**). Multicentre, unblinded,
pragmatic randomised clinical trial, 23 paediatric intensive care units in 3 countries
Inwald, Laing, Broomhall, Viola, Au, Brown, Camsooksai, Chang, Chester, Darnell, Ghattoray, Johnson,
Keattch, Parke, Manning, Ramnarayan, Ray, Sadique, Scholefield et al.

> **Importance** Vasoactive drugs are often given to critically ill children to treat hypotension.
> However, optimal mean arterial pressure (MAP) targets are unknown.
>
> **Objective** To evaluate whether adopting a permissive MAP target, and thereby potentially
> reducing exposure to vasoactive drugs, improves outcomes in hypotensive critically ill children.
>
> **Design, setting, and participants** This multicenter, unblinded, pragmatic randomized clinical
> trial was conducted in 23 pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) in 3 countries. Critically ill
> children receiving invasive ventilation and vasoactive drugs for hypotension were enrolled between
> November 2021 and May 2026. Follow-up to the 30-day outcome was completed in June 2026.
>
> **Interventions** Patients randomized to the intervention (n = 957) were assigned a permissive MAP
> target greater than the fifth percentile for age. Patients in the control group (n = 943) received
> usual care with MAP target set by the attending physician.
>
> **Main outcomes and measures** The primary outcome was a composite of mortality and duration of
> invasive ventilation at 30 days.
>
> **Results** Of 1900 randomized patients, 910 in the permissive MAP target group and 904 in the
> usual care group were included for analysis of the primary outcome. Participants had a median (IQR)
> age of 4 (0-10) years and 44.8% were female… Median (IQR) MAP while receiving vasoactive drugs was
> **58 (51-63) mm Hg** in the permissive MAP target group and **61 (54-67) mm Hg** in the usual care
> group. A permissive MAP target led to a **37.5% reduction in median norepinephrine-equivalent total
> dose** compared with the usual care group. There was **no significant difference between the groups
> in the primary outcome (probabilistic index, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.49-0.54]; P = .23)**. Six of the 7
> secondary outcomes were similar between the groups, except median (IQR) PICU length of stay was
> significantly shorter in survivors in the permissive MAP target vs usual care group (159 [79-286]
> vs 166 [92-319] hours; probabilistic index, 0.53 [95% CI, 0.50-0.56]). Receipt of kidney
> replacement therapy and adverse events did not differ between the groups.
>
> **Conclusions and relevance** Adopting a permissive MAP target resulted in no significant
> difference in mortality and duration of invasive ventilation at 30 days compared with usual care.

Trial registration ISRCTN20609635; Swiss National Clinical Trial Portal SNCTP000006590.

**Interpretation.** Read the two numbers in the results together, because the trial's real finding
sits between them. The arms separated by **3 mmHg of achieved MAP** — the usual complaint about
pressure-target trials, and normally the reason to discount them. But the permissive arm received
**37.5% less vasoactive drug**. So the exposure being tested did separate substantially even though
the pressure barely did, and that inversion is the useful result: in these children, **a large
reduction in vasopressor dose bought only a small fall in blood pressure, and no detectable outcome
penalty.** If you believe the dose reduction, the trial is informative regardless of the narrow
pressure gap.

What it does not show is equivalence. The stated objective was whether a permissive target
**improves** outcomes; it did not, with a probabilistic index of 0.52 and a confidence interval
(0.49–0.54) that crosses the no-difference value of 0.50. [As noted here
yesterday](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-29/), a failed superiority test is not an equivalence
demonstration, and that applies in this direction too. What is genuinely reassuring is narrower and
concrete: **kidney replacement therapy and adverse events did not differ**, which is where a
deliberate de-escalation strategy would be expected to show harm first.

The single "significant" secondary outcome deserves less weight than its P value suggests. PICU stay
in survivors was **159 versus 166 hours** — about 7 hours on medians near 160, a 4% difference, with
a probabilistic index of 0.53 whose lower bound touches 0.50 exactly. It is one of seven secondary
outcomes, and **the abstract does not state whether these were adjusted for multiplicity**; without
the full text that cannot be settled either way.

**Where this sits in the run of evidence reported here.** Three findings on three consecutive days:

| Setting | Design | Result on pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noncardiac surgery, adults ([28 Sep](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-29/)) | Bayesian meta-analysis, 15 RCTs | Mortality **OR 1.00** |
| Noncardiac surgery, adults ([29 Sep](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-29/)) | RCT, n=100, HPI vs MAP ≤73 | **No demonstrated superiority** |
| Paediatric critical care (today) | Pragmatic RCT, n=1,900 | **No significant difference** |

Against which, from the same window, the one variable that *did* track mortality was a **delivery**
variable rather than a pressure one — oxygen delivery index on cardiopulmonary bypass, adjusted
OR 1.16 per 10 ml/min/m² decrease ([29 Sep](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-29/)). The
perfusion-not-pressure thread this archive has been following since August now has its largest
randomised data point, and it is in children.

One caution on generalising: **the permissive target was defined as MAP above the fifth percentile
for age**, not as a fixed number, and usual care was whatever the attending chose. This is a
pragmatic trial of a *strategy*, and the control arm is not a defined threshold. It tells you that
targeting lower is not detectably worse; it does not tell you how low is too low.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.19747)

## 2. Sevoflurane versus carrier gas and CO₂ absorbent — the contributions are two orders of magnitude apart

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*. **Europe PMC gives first publication as 29 September 2026,
but the record entered the index on 17 July 2026** — 74 days earlier. That gap is the signature of a
paper that was online months ago and has now been re-dated to an issue. **I could not confirm the
original online date: the publisher's page returns HTTP 402 (payment required).** So this is
reported as **recent but not new this week**, and the 29 September date is the aggregator's, not
necessarily the paper's.
Jouwena, De Wolf, Hendrickx, Hendrickx

> **Background** The absolute and relative effects of lowering fresh gas flows (FGFs) on the carbon
> dioxide equivalent (CO₂e) emissions of sevoflurane, carrier gases (oxygen/air) and prepacked carbon
> dioxide canister remain poorly quantified. The authors quantified these factors across a 0.2 to
> 4-l/min FGF range during the first hour of anesthesia.
>
> **Methods** Data were compiled from three studies including **132 American Society of
> Anesthesiologists Physical Status I to III patients** receiving a constant end-tidal sevoflurane
> concentration (FETsevo) using manual (n = 50) or target-controlled delivery (n = 48) with 0.2 to
> 4 l/min FGF or during automated closed-circuit delivery (n = 34). Sevoflurane consumption was
> normalized to both 1.3 and 2.0% FETsevo, and carrier gas use to an inspired oxygen concentration of
> 30% and 60%. Prepacked carbon dioxide absorbent usage was calculated using a previously described
> model for both 130 and 160 ml/min carbon dioxide output. Published CO₂e values were used to derive
> CO₂e of sevoflurane.
>
> **Results** Sevoflurane CO₂e **increases linearly with FGF (range, 2.4 to 18.6 kg CO₂e)**, except
> when a brief wash-in period was used due to low FGF (FGF less than 1 l/min). Carrier gas CO₂e
> decreases with lower FGF but in a more complex manner, and increases with higher inspired oxygen
> concentration (**range, 0.010 to 0.124 kg CO₂e**). Absorbent CO₂e decreases linearly with FGF
> (**range, 0 to 0.070 kg CO₂e**). The CO₂e of sevoflurane is **two orders of magnitude higher** than
> CO₂e of carrier gas and carbon dioxide absorbent, which are similar.
>
> **Conclusions** When delivering sevoflurane in oxygen/air, the CO₂e contribution of the carrier gas
> and carbon dioxide absorbent is **less than 3%, even during closed-circuit delivery**. While CO₂e is
> only one element of a comprehensive life cycle analysis, the presented CO₂e data underscore that the
> key to minimizing the global warming potential of sevoflurane is **lowering FGF and decreasing
> FETsevo**.

**Interpretation.** This paper exists to close down a specific departmental argument. Once a
department starts counting carbon, attention tends to scatter across everything visible on the
machine — the absorbent canister, air versus oxygen, the disposables. This quantifies those and the
answer is that **together they are under 3% of the total, even in a closed circuit.** The entire
lever is the two dials: **fresh gas flow and end-tidal concentration.** One hour of sevoflurane
anaesthesia spans **2.4 to 18.6 kg CO₂e depending on FGF alone** — a 7.75-fold range from a setting
that costs nothing to change.

Three things it is not. It is **not a trial**: consumption data come from 132 patients in three
previously published studies, and CO₂e is then derived by applying published conversion factors. It
is therefore a quantification exercise, and it **inherits whatever uncertainty attaches to the global
warming potential value adopted for sevoflurane** — the authors say themselves that CO₂e is "only one
element of a comprehensive life cycle analysis." And it says nothing about the comparison patients
and departments actually face, which is inhalational versus intravenous; for that, see items 3 and 4.

The practical reading is narrow and firm: **if you are giving sevoflurane, the flow meter is the
intervention.** Nothing else on the machine is close.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1097/aln.0000000000006238)

## 3. Central nitrous oxide deactivation — 94.6% of the piped gas never reached a patient

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesia & Analgesia*, **28 September 2026** (**new this week**).
Multicentre observational interrupted time-series across 11 NYC Health + Hospitals facilities
Sutin, Jain, Gajic, Young, Chang, Klesitz, Geara, Ostberg, Dilos, Golden, Robinson, Nunnally

> **Background** Volatile anesthetic agents and nitrous oxide (N₂O) are greenhouse gases and a
> modifiable source of health care-related carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e) emissions; **losses from
> central N₂O pipeline systems are a major contributor.**
>
> **Methods** … case-level data aggregated to monthly group-level observations from **11 NYC Health +
> Hospitals facilities between April 1, 2022, and December 31, 2025**. After exclusions, **148,762 of
> 158,881 screened general anesthetic cases (93.6%)** were analyzed… Two interventions were
> evaluated: a **clinical decision support Advisory** prompting FGF reduction and **deactivation of
> central N₂O pipelines at three hospitals.**
>
> **Results** During Phase 1 (pre-intervention), median FGF was **3.90 L·min⁻¹**, N₂O was used in
> **27.2% of cases**, and sevoflurane in **97.8%**. **N₂O generated an annualized CO₂e impact
> equivalent to 77% of all volatile agents combined.** System-level hourly CO₂e emission rate declined
> over the study period by 0.143 kg CO₂e·h⁻¹ per month (95% CI, 0.11-0.18; P < .001). The system-wide
> Initial Advisory was associated with step reductions in FGF (0.163 L·min⁻¹; 95% CI, 0.11-0.21;
> P < .001) and in total hourly CO₂e emission rate (0.78 kg CO₂e·h⁻¹; 95% CI, 0.24-1.32; P = .006).
> **Central N₂O pipeline deactivation was the dominant structural effect:** complete deactivation was
> associated with a **1.67 kg CO₂e·h⁻¹ reduction** in N₂O-attributable hourly emission rate at the
> group level (95% CI, 1.23-2.12; P < .001). **At Bellevue, 94.6% of centrally supplied N₂O was lost
> before reaching patients, generating approximately 2136 metric tons of CO₂e annually, 2.58 times the
> combined annual emissions from all clinically administered anesthetic gases across the 11-hospital
> system.**
>
> **Conclusions** Central N₂O pipeline deactivation was associated with the largest reduction in the
> N₂O-attributable hourly CO₂e emission rate; **the association with total emissions in the primary
> model was not statistically significant (P = .069).** Concurrent secular declines in N₂O and
> desflurane use contributed additionally, whereas the clinical decision support Advisory was
> associated with a modest but statistically significant reduction in FGF rate and total hourly CO₂e
> emission rate. These findings support the 2024 American Society of Anesthesiologists recommendation
> to deactivate centrally piped N₂O to reduce health care emissions.

**Interpretation.** The Bellevue figure reframes the problem. **The largest single source of
anaesthetic greenhouse gas at that hospital was gas that never reached a patient** — 94.6% lost to
pipeline leakage, an estimated 2,136 tonnes of CO₂e a year, which the authors put at **2.58 times the
emissions of every anaesthetic gas actually administered across all eleven hospitals combined.** Put
next to item 2, the two papers divide the problem cleanly: **inside the breathing circuit, flow and
end-tidal concentration are the levers; outside it, the pipeline is a bigger lever than anything a
clinician can do at the machine.** Nothing an anaesthetist changes intraoperatively competes with
turning off a leaking supply.

The paper's own reporting is a model worth naming, because it undercuts its own headline in the
conclusion rather than in a limitations paragraph nobody reads. **Deactivation produced the largest
fall in the N₂O-attributable emission rate, and yet the association with *total* emissions in the
primary model was not statistically significant (P = .069).** Both statements are true and the
tension between them is the honest result: the N₂O-specific effect is large and clearly estimated,
the whole-system effect is not demonstrated at conventional thresholds.

Three limits follow. This is an **interrupted time series, not a randomised comparison**, and the
authors identify a **concurrent secular decline** in both N₂O and desflurane use — so attribution
between the intervention and the trend is genuinely contested by the design. The 94.6% loss is **one
hospital's supply audit**, and the 2,136-tonne figure is an annualised extrapolation from it; that
single number carries most of the paper's rhetorical weight and is the one least protected by the
statistical model. And the clinical decision support effect is real but small — **0.163 L·min⁻¹** off
a median flow of 3.90, roughly a 4% reduction in flow from prompting clinicians.

The asymmetry between those two findings is the point. **Prompting clinicians moved the flow meter by
4%; closing the pipeline removed an emission source larger than all clinical use put together.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008319)

## 4. The anchor: TIVA versus optimised inhalational anaesthesia across 35,242 procedures

**Journal · Published:** *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, **online 4 April 2025**, print June 2025
(volume 134, issue 6, pages 1620–1627). **Older but actively cited** — 24 citations at the time of
writing, and in PubMed Central with a PDF. Included because items 2 and 3 are not interpretable
without a whole-technique baseline.
Bernat, Cuvillon, Brieussel, Roche, Remacle, Leone, Lukaszewicz, Bouvet, Zieleskiewicz

> **Background** … Previous studies have compared **non-optimised** anaesthesia strategies
> (desflurane, nitrous oxide, or both) to evaluate the impact of green initiatives on reducing the
> carbon footprint of anaesthesia. However, modern halogenated anaesthesia techniques, including **low
> fresh gas flow and target-controlled inhalation anaesthesia (TCIA)**, offer potentially more
> environmentally friendly alternatives. Thus, we aimed to compare the GHG emissions of total
> intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) with these newer techniques.
>
> **Methods** This multicentre study compared GHG emissions per hour of general anaesthesia in adult
> surgical patients between three anaesthetic strategies: TIVA with propofol, sevoflurane in TCIA
> mode, and manually optimised sevoflurane. The study was conducted in **three French university
> hospitals, each using one anaesthesia strategy.** The quantity of anaesthetic drugs used was
> obtained from **pharmacy procurement records** and converted to carbon dioxide equivalents (CO₂e).
> The primary outcome was the total GHG emissions per hour of anaesthesia for each strategy,
> including sevoflurane, propofol, and syringe consumption.
>
> **Results** TCIA, manually optimised sevoflurane, and TIVA strategies were used in **7873, 15 461,
> and 10 717 anaesthetics**, respectively. The carbon footprint of the principal anaesthetic drugs per
> hour of anaesthesia was significantly lower in the TIVA strategy, at **0.4 kg CO₂e per hour**,
> compared with **3.1 kg CO₂e per hour in the TCIA strategy and 3.8 kg CO₂e per hour in the manually
> optimised sevoflurane strategy.**
>
> **Conclusions** TIVA with propofol was the most effective approach for minimising greenhouse gas
> emissions in anaesthesia practices. However, if TIVA were used exclusively globally, it could lead
> to issues such as **stock depletion, plastic pollution, and water contamination.**

**Interpretation.** This is the number the other two papers need. Item 2 tells you how to make
sevoflurane as cheap as sevoflurane gets; this tells you what that ceiling is worth. **Even
target-controlled inhalational delivery — the optimised form — sat at 3.1 kg CO₂e per hour against
0.4 for TIVA**, roughly an eightfold gap that optimising flow narrows but does not close. And it is
worth noticing that **TCIA at 3.1 and manual optimisation at 3.8 are much closer to each other than
either is to TIVA**: within inhalational practice, technique refinement moves the number by about
18%, while changing technique class moves it by about 87%.

**Why this matters now rather than in April.** [The JAMA pragmatic trial reported here on 24
September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-24/) randomised **2,508 patients across 49 NHS
hospitals** to TIVA or volatile anaesthesia and found **no difference** in days alive and at home.
Those two papers together produce a conclusion neither states: **if the clinical outcomes are
indistinguishable, the emissions gap is no longer a trade-off — it is a tiebreaker.** That is the
strongest environmental argument in this entry, and it only exists because a null clinical trial and
a positive environmental comparison are read side by side.

Two things stop this being decisive, and the second is the more interesting.

**The design is confounded by centre.** Each of the three hospitals used one strategy, so this is a
between-institution comparison, not a within-institution or randomised one — case mix, duration and
practice culture differ with the exposure. Drug quantities came from **pharmacy procurement records**
rather than per-case measurement, which measures what was bought, not what was given. (The per-arm
counts also sum to 34,051 rather than the 35,242 in the title; the abstract does not reconcile the
difference.)

**And the accounting boundary decides the answer.** This paper counted "sevoflurane, propofol, and
syringe consumption." A modelling study in *Anaesthesia* (**14 October 2025**,
[10.1111/anae.70023](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.70023), retrieved in the same search as the papers
above) counted **all consumables** and found the ordering reverses for short cases: median-consumption
sevoflurane had a **lower** footprint than every TIVA scenario for the first **22 minutes**, with
TIVA lower beyond that, and in the TIVA scenarios **consumables contributed 50.9–60.4% of the total
footprint** against 39.6–45.4% for the drugs. In the sevoflurane scenario, by 360 minutes the agent
itself was **99.5%** of the footprint.

So the honest composite of all four papers is conditional rather than slogan-shaped. **For a long
case, intravenous anaesthesia is substantially lower-carbon and the clinical outcomes now appear
equivalent. For a short case, the plastic may outweigh the gas.** And the authors' own closing
caution deserves repeating, because it is the kind of thing that gets dropped in the retelling:
universal TIVA would bring **stock depletion, plastic pollution and water contamination** — propofol
is lower-carbon, not consequence-free.
