## 1. Forty-two reviews, 121 trials, and most pairs share nothing

**[Discordant evidence on corticosteroids in sepsis: a meta-research
study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06471-2)** · *Critical Care* · **published 19 November 2026** ·
42 systematic reviews published 2015–2025, 121 unique randomised trials

> "Forty-two SRs including 121 unique RCTs were identified. More than half of pairwise SR comparisons shared
> no RCTs, and only three pairs showed high overlap (J > 0.8). SRs addressing similar intervention and
> target population profiles frequently relied on different study pools. Among 38 SRs with short-term
> mortality meta-analyses, 15 (39%) reported benefit and 23 (61%) no evidence of effect. Discordance
> occurred exclusively among SRs evaluating broad, non-specific corticosteroid strategies; conclusions were
> consistent for hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone (benefit) and hydrocortisone, ascorbic acid, and
> thiamine (no evidence of effect)... No single methodological or clinical factor consistently explained
> discordance."

**Interpretation — this paper settles something this archive got to criticise only four days ago.** On
16 November a systematic review of albumin in septic shock was reported here, and its conclusion was reached
by counting: "two of the three meta-analyses demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in 90-day
mortality." That was called out as vote counting — the practice meta-analysis exists to replace. **What this
meta-research study adds is the reason the practice is not merely weak but void: three reviews of the same
clinical question may have no trial in common.** More than half of all pairwise comparisons among 42 reviews
of corticosteroids share **zero** randomised trials. **Counting how many reviews reached significance
counts how many disjoint evidence bases happened to tip one way.**

**And it reframes the phrase "conflicting evidence", which is used constantly in this field.** The reviews
are mostly not disagreeing about the same trials. They are summarising different trials and reporting
different answers, which is not conflict so much as **non-comparability wearing the costume of conflict.**
Only three of all possible pairs reach a Jaccard similarity above 0.8.

**The most useful finding is where discordance stops.** It occurs **exclusively** among reviews of
"corticosteroids" as a broad category. Where the intervention is specified exactly, the field agrees:
hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone, benefit; hydrocortisone with ascorbic acid and thiamine, no effect.
**So the discordance is a property of the question, not of the drug** — and a reader confronted with
conflicting steroid reviews should check which steroid regimen, in which population, before anything else.

**One subgroup signal, and the authors bound it themselves.** Reviews of adult sepsis with or without shock
reported benefit more often than those restricted to septic shock, 62% against 22% — **"although this
comparison was based on only 22 SRs ... and estimates were imprecise."** The hedge is theirs, it is in the
abstract, and it is the right one: 62% of a subset of 22 is a handful of reviews.

**What the study cannot do, and does not claim: it explains nothing.** "No single methodological or clinical
factor consistently explained discordance." **A meta-research study that sets out to find the source of
disagreement and reports that it could not isolate one has produced a negative result about its own
hypothesis, and says so in the abstract rather than promoting the 62-versus-22 contrast into an
explanation.**

**The operational recommendation is unusually concrete for this genre:** when choosing a review to inform a
decision, consider "not only methodological quality and reported PICO, but also whether the included RCTs
adequately represent the intended clinical question." **That is a third criterion most appraisal tools do
not have, and this paper is the demonstration of why it is needed.**

## 2. Ninety-eight studies, twenty-four thousand patients, six of them poolable

**[An absent or weak cough doubles the odds of extubation failure after acute brain injury: an updated
systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accpm.2026.102005)** ·
*Anaesthesia, Critical Care & Pain Medicine* · **published 18 November 2026** · 98 studies, 24,505 patients

> "98 studies (24 505 patients) yielded 415 predictor-outcome estimates; 194 carried a confidence interval,
> and 6 studies contributed to pooled adjusted estimates. Absent or weak cough was associated with failure
> (4 studies; OR 2.47, 95% CI 1.46-4.19; posterior median 2.55, 95% CrI 1.54-4.71, P(OR > 1) = 0.999;
> I² = 0%; moderate certainty)... Below-threshold motor GCS was associated with failure in two concordant
> studies (posterior median OR 5.99, 2.42-16.1; very low certainty). The pre-specified comparison between
> families could not be validly estimated from published data... Fewer than one in ten eligible studies are
> poolable, mainly for remediable reporting reasons."

**Interpretation — the denominators are the story.** 98 studies and 24,505 patients produced **415
predictor-outcome estimates, of which 194 carry a confidence interval.** That is **47%: more than half of
the published estimates in this literature are point values with no measure of precision.** And of the 98
studies, **6 contributed to the pooled adjusted estimates — 6.1%.** The headline cough result rests on
**four studies**, drawn from a field of ninety-eight.

**The authors name the cause and it is not biology: "mainly for remediable reporting reasons."** A paper
that reports an unadjusted percentage instead of an adjusted estimate with an interval is unpoolable, and
twenty-four thousand patients' worth of data is unusable for that reason alone. **They propose a minimum
reporting set, which is the right response and the one that costs the field nothing.**

**The cough finding itself is well-behaved and modest.** OR 2.47 (1.46–4.19) frequentist with
Hartung-Knapp, posterior median 2.55 (1.54–4.71) Bayesian, the two methods agreeing, P(OR > 1) = 0.999, and
**GRADE moderate rather than high** — the authors do not confer high certainty on a four-study pool, which
is the distinction this archive recorded on 10 November.

**The projection reconciles, read correctly.** The abstract's "about 30% versus 15%" at an illustrative 19%
failure rate is consistent if 15% is the rate with an intact cough: odds 0.1765 × 2.47 = 0.436, which is a
risk of 30.4%. **The 19% is the mixed overall rate, not the reference group**, and the arithmetic only works
on that reading.

**One caution and one exception to it.** I² = 0% across four studies would normally be a warning in this
archive — zero heterogeneity in a small pool is more often a sign of too little data to detect it than of
genuine consistency. **Here the exception recorded on 11 November applies: the pool was restricted by
design to one scale and one instrument**, so the studies are measuring the same thing in the same way, and
zero heterogeneity is what that restriction is for.

**The motor GCS result is the one to read with the authors' own label attached.** Posterior OR 5.99
(2.42–16.1) from **two studies**, graded **very low certainty**. A 6.6-fold interval span on two studies is
a hypothesis; the paper says so, and the number will nonetheless be quoted without the label.

**Credit for the sentence most syntheses would have buried: "The pre-specified comparison between families
could not be validly estimated from published data."** The study's headline purpose was to compare
neurological against respiratory predictors, pre-specified, **and the literature could not support it. They
report it as unestimable rather than estimating it badly.** Registration provenance is complete to the point
of self-incrimination — PROSPERO submitted 6 July, registered 2 September, searches through July 2026, with
the two-month gap visible rather than hidden, protocol v2.3 dated before the searches, and all deviations
tabulated.

## 3. A score that stratifies without discriminating, and a field that illustrates more than it calibrates

**[Machine Learning vs. Clinical Judgement for Extubation Failure: A Rigorous
Re-evaluation](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11244575/v1)** · **Research Square preprint, posted
19 November 2026, not peer reviewed** · 800 adults extubated in a Bogotá ICU at 2,600 m, 71 failures (8.9%)

> "The published points score reproduced exactly, separating four risk strata with observed failure rates of
> 4.4%, 7.7%, 11.6% and 23.0%, but the score distributions of patients who failed and succeeded overlap
> almost completely. Given the same 12 candidate variables, random forest reached 0.630 and gradient
> boosting 0.627 against 0.655 for ordinary logistic regression, a spread of 0.028 in a design that cannot
> detect less than 0.068... SMOTE oversampling left discrimination unchanged while inflating predicted risk
> by 75% and driving net benefit below the treat-no strategy. Among the 14 published studies, 12 displayed
> an explainability figure, 7 reported calibration, 4 had any external validation, 3 reported net benefit,
> 3 shared code and none used repeated or nested cross validation."

**Interpretation — the best sentence in today's five papers is the one that states its own detection
limit.** A spread of 0.028 **"in a design that cannot detect less than 0.068"**: the authors computed what
difference their study could resolve, observed a difference less than half that, and therefore reported no
difference rather than ranking three models. **Most comparisons of this kind rank the models.**

**The second-best finding is about a validated score and it is uncomfortable.** The ABCDMed score
reproduces exactly and separates four strata from 4.4% to 23.0% — a five-fold gradient, which looks like
success — **"but the score distributions of patients who failed and succeeded overlap almost completely."**
**Stratification and discrimination are different properties**, and a score can deliver clinically
meaningful group rates while being nearly useless for the individual patient in front of you. The AUROC of
0.655 is what that overlap looks like as a number.

**And it bounds what any model on this data could achieve.** Given all 42 variables, hyperparameter
tuning, ensembling and class rebalancing, **nothing exceeded the original parsimonious score.** The
published literature on this outcome reports AUROCs "of up to 0.98". **Against 0.655 here, under repeated
stratified cross-validation with imputation, scaling, resampling, variable selection and hyperparameter
search all performed inside the training folds.** The gap between 0.98 and 0.655 is a measurement of how
much of the published performance is leakage and optimism.

**One concrete harm from a routine step, worth keeping:** SMOTE oversampling **left discrimination
unchanged while inflating predicted risk by 75% and pushing net benefit below the treat-nobody strategy.**
A rebalancing step that does not change ranking but destroys calibration makes a model actively worse than
doing nothing, and decision-curve analysis is what shows it.

**The appraisal of the field is the finding that will outlive the paper.** Of 14 published machine-learning
studies of extubation failure: **12 have an explainability figure, 7 report calibration, 4 have any
external validation, 3 report net benefit, 3 share code, and none used repeated or nested
cross-validation.** **Explainability figures outnumber calibration reports.** A field that illustrates why
its models decide more often than it checks whether their probabilities mean anything has its priorities
inverted, and the ordering of those five numbers says it without argument.

**Two bounds, and the second is unusual enough to state plainly.** This is a single-centre cohort at
2,600 m altitude with 71 events, so the absolute discrimination is not transportable even if the comparison
between methods is. **And the methods disclose that artificial intelligence was used both to appraise the
14 published studies against the reporting items and to produce an adversarial peer review of the
manuscript, "every claim of which was verified by re-analysis."** The verification is the right safeguard
and it is self-reported. **The 14-study appraisal is therefore a machine-generated extraction, which bounds
the five numbers above, and an AI adversarial review is not peer review — this is a preprint.**

## 4. The SEPSISPAM subgroup, tested as an interaction and essentially excluded

**[Chronic Hypertension and the Effect of Mean Arterial Pressure Strategies in Septic Shock: A Two-Cohort
Target Trial Emulation Using Bayesian g-Computation](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11493996/v1)** ·
**Research Square preprint, posted 18 November 2026, not peer reviewed** · 13,714 adults, MIMIC-IV (7,181)
and eICU-CRD (6,533)

> "For RRT, the interaction odds ratio was 1.20 (95% credible interval [CrI] 0.88-1.65); for mortality, 1.04
> (0.73-1.48). Additive interactions were + 0.5 (- 1.1 to 2.1) and - 1.3 (- 2.6 to - 0.1) percentage
> points. The posterior probability of an RRT interaction as protective as SEPSISPAM implied (odds ratio
> <= 0.53) was below 0.01%, and the probability that it lay between 0.80 and 1.25 was 59%. Estimates were
> stable across 17 sensitivity analyses, including a prior centred on the SEPSISPAM subgroup (0.89,
> 0.69-1.16)."

**Interpretation — this is the interaction test this archive keeps asking for, pre-specified as the
estimand.** The standing rule here is that a difference in significance between two subgroups is not a
significant difference, and only the interaction speaks to effect modification. **SEPSISPAM's hypertensive
subgroup is one of the most-cited subgroup findings in critical care, and this study was designed to
estimate the interaction rather than to re-split the data.** The answer is 1.20 (0.88–1.65) for renal
replacement therapy and 1.04 (0.73–1.48) for mortality.

**The decisive sentence is not the null, it is the exclusion.** A Bayesian analysis can answer a question a
confidence interval cannot: **the posterior probability that the interaction is as protective as SEPSISPAM
implied — an odds ratio of 0.53 or better — is below 0.01%.** That is a far stronger statement than "we
found no interaction". **And a sensitivity analysis using a prior centred on the SEPSISPAM subgroup itself
still lands at 0.89 (0.69–1.16)** — the result survives being given the benefit of the doubt it is
testing.

**The honest counterweight is in the same sentence and should not be skipped: the probability that the
interaction lies between 0.80 and 1.25 is 59%.** So a region of practical equivalence holds only moderate
posterior mass. **The study excludes a large protective interaction confidently and does not establish exact
equivalence** — those are different claims and the abstract reports both.

**One internal tension the abstract states and does not reconcile.** The multiplicative interactions are
both null, while the additive mortality interaction is **−1.3 percentage points (−2.6 to −0.1)**, excluding
zero. **Additive and multiplicative interaction need not agree, and which one matters depends on whether
the decision is about absolute or relative benefit** — but a paper concluding "chronic hypertension did not
modify the effect" has one measure on its primary outcome's scale pointing otherwise, and the reconciliation
is not attempted in the abstract. **It is a narrow interval close to zero and I do not read it as a finding;
I read it as something the full text needs to address.**

**Where this sits in the archive's vasopressor series, now four entries.** An observational hazard ratio of
2.55, an emulation giving 2.87 above 0.75 µg/kg/min, VASSPR's randomised 0.98 across the window that
emulation called therapeutic, and now **a target trial emulation that reaches a confident null on a
subgroup hypothesis and does so without any apparent confounding signature** — the opposite outcome from
the vasopressin emulation, which controlled immortal time impeccably and still reported the shape of
confounding. **The method is capable of both results, which is the argument for it.**

## 5. The same journal, one day later, and the instructive opposite of yesterday's paper

**[Dexmedetomidine-associated bradycardia in perioperative and critical care settings: a cross-database
pharmacovigilance study using FAERS and JADER](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00210-026-06119-6)** ·
*Naunyn-Schmiedeberg's Archives of Pharmacology* · **published 20 November 2026** · 2,505 dexmedetomidine
reports, 2004Q1 to 2025Q3

> "Among 2505 DEX reports, 717 involved bradycardia-associated events (FAERS, n = 327; JADER, n = 390).
> Bradycardia showed prominent disproportionality signals in both databases. Among 198 reports with valid
> TTO data, the median onset time was 0 days in both databases, with similar Weibull shape parameters
> (FAERS, beta = 0.849; JADER, beta = 0.847)... Pre-existing cardiovascular disease showed the strongest
> adjusted association with bradycardia reporting (OR 4.88, 95% CI 3.92-6.09), followed by concomitant
> opioid use (OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.37-2.22) and beta-adrenoceptor blocker exposure (OR 1.85, 95% CI
> 1.08-3.13)... spontaneous reporting data cannot establish causality or quantify incidence."

**Interpretation — read this against yesterday's brief, because the contrast is the lesson.** Yesterday's
item 1 was a FAERS study from this same journal, published three days earlier: 158 exposed reports, not one
confidence interval anywhere, and a null. **Today's has 717 events, two independent national databases,
confidence intervals on every estimate, a time-to-onset model, and seventeen-ish fewer ways to go wrong.**
**Same method family, same journal, three days apart, and the difference between them is almost entirely
sample size and reporting discipline.** A reader who concluded from yesterday that pharmacovigilance cannot
answer anything would be wrong, and today's paper is why.

**The single most persuasive number is one nobody would design for: the Weibull shape parameters are 0.849
and 0.847.** Two independent spontaneous-reporting systems, different countries, different reporting
cultures, agreeing to three decimal places on the shape of the onset distribution. **A shape parameter below
1 means a decreasing hazard — events concentrate early — and the median onset of 0 days in both databases
says the same thing.** Bradycardia after dexmedetomidine is an induction-period event, and that is a
monitoring recommendation rather than a statistical curiosity.

**But two of the three risk factors are not findings about dexmedetomidine at all, and the design is why.**
This is a case-non-case analysis **within** dexmedetomidine reports, so it asks which dexmedetomidine
reports mention bradycardia. **Beta-blocker exposure at OR 1.85 is a statement that beta-blockers cause
bradycardia**, which was known, and **pre-existing cardiovascular disease at OR 4.88 includes people with
conduction disease who were going to be bradycardic on any sedative.** The design identifies who is
reported with bradycardia, not what dexmedetomidine does to them. **Useful for monitoring, not evidence of
interaction.**

**And a pattern across two days worth flagging precisely because it may be an artefact.** Concomitant opioid
use was the one significant covariate in yesterday's GLP-1 aspiration study (aOR 2.12) and is significant
again here (OR 1.74), for a completely different adverse event. **Opioids plausibly cause both aspiration
and bradycardia — and they also mark sicker, more intensively treated, more heavily documented patients, so
a covariate that turns up significant in consecutive spontaneous-reporting studies of unrelated outcomes
may be telling us about who gets reported.** Two instances is a pattern worth watching, not a conclusion.

**The authors' own bound is stated in the abstract and is the correct one:** spontaneous reporting "cannot
establish causality or quantify incidence". **717 bradycardia reports out of 2,505 is not a 29% incidence
and nothing in this paper is a rate.**

### The scope filter is now in the repository, and its own test was run

**The title-and-journal scope filter validated on 16 November was the last of the three instruments from
that week still living in a scratch directory.** It is now `claude/tools/scope_filter.py`, with both journal
lists, the reasons for keying on title **or** journal written into the file, and the measured cuts recorded.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 16 October set, guidance journal list | 304 | **16** | **5 / 5** |
| 16 October set, brief journal list | 304 | **19** | **5 / 5** |

**The 16 November validation reported 60 → 16 on that day's test set. Today's run starts from 304 records —
a five-fold larger set from a broader query — and lands on the same 16.** That is a stronger result than the
original: **the filter's output is determined by the records, not by the query that fetched them.**

title**, which was verified by deliberately feeding it an `idlist` result:
`recall test aborted: records carry no title — wrong resultType`.
