## 1. Twenty-two years of kidney trials, split in half by their endpoint

**[Pharmacological Prevention of Cardiac Surgery-Associated Acute Kidney Injury: Why Trials Failed and a
Reimagined Endpoint Framework](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.athoracsur.2026.10.022)** · *The Annals of Thoracic
Surgery* · **published 5 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**An invited narrative review of 33 pivotal randomised trials of pharmacological prevention published from
2004 to 2026** — twenty-two years — grouped by mechanism, with population, timing and endpoint related to
outcome. Kidney injury after cardiac surgery affects **about one adult in four.** From the abstract:

> Trials using short-term creatinine-based kidney injury endpoints produced the positive signals, whereas
> trials using major adverse kidney events at 90 days as the primary endpoint were negative. This divergence
> reflects both the low power of that composite for detecting injury prevention and unresolved biology, since
> no agent has shown benefit on both types of endpoint. Positive trials shared a matched mechanism, a defined
> population, and an aligned endpoint. Recurrent failure modes were endpoint misalignment, mismatch between
> population and the timing of injury, confounding by postoperative cardiac recovery, and direct drug effects
> on creatinine or urine output that mimic or mask injury.

**The central sentence is the one that should reorganise how this archive reads the whole field: no agent has
shown benefit on both types of endpoint.** **Twenty-two years and 33 trials have produced two literatures —
one of short-term creatinine successes and one of 90-day composite failures — with no overlap.** **That is not
a weight-of-evidence problem; it is a definitional one.**

**And the fourth failure mode named is the sharpest, because it attacks the positive literature from
inside.** **"Direct drug effects on creatinine or urine output that mimic or mask injury."** **A drug that
alters creatinine handling will move a creatinine-defined endpoint whether or not it protects a nephron** —
which means some of the positive short-term trials may be measuring pharmacology rather than protection.
**This archive published the dapagliflozin trial on 10 October, where perioperative dapagliflozin reduced
acute kidney injury after elective cardiac surgery; an SGLT2 inhibitor alters tubular handling by design, and
this review's fourth failure mode applies to it directly.** **That is not a reason to discount the trial; it
is the question the trial's own endpoint cannot answer.**

**The proposed framework is specific, which is what makes the review worth publishing rather than
summarising.** **Persistent stage 2 or 3 acute kidney disease at 30 days as the primary endpoint;
preoperative albuminuria as an enrichment criterion; attenuation of the postoperative albuminuria rise at 30
and 90 days as a key secondary.** **All three choices address a named failure mode: a 30-day persistence
endpoint is immune to transient creatinine pharmacology, enrichment fixes the population mismatch, and
albuminuria is a mechanistic rather than a filtration measure.**

**My reading.** **The most useful thing here is a rule a reader can apply immediately: before crediting any
kidney-protection trial, ask which of the two literatures it belongs to, and whether its drug could move its
endpoint without touching the kidney.** **The honest limit is that this is a narrative review with an
authorial argument, not a systematic synthesis** — the 33 trials are described as pivotal, and which trials
those are, and who chose them, is not in the abstract. **The framework stands on its reasoning rather than on
a reproducible search.**

## 2. A two-minute margin and an eleven-minute difference

**[Remimazolam versus Propofol for General Anesthesia in Elderly Patients Undergoing TAVI: A Randomized
Controlled Trial](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11033086/v1)** · **preprint, Research Square** ·
**posted 5 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**Single-centre, prospective, randomised non-inferiority trial. Patients aged 65 to 80 having transcatheter
aortic valve implantation under general anaesthesia with a laryngeal mask. 118 assessed, 76 randomised — 39
remimazolam, 37 propofol, summing exactly — so 42 patients, 35.6%, were excluded at screening.** Primary
outcome time to laryngeal mask removal, **with a prespecified non-inferiority margin of 2 minutes.** From the
abstract:

> LMA removal time was 9.7 ± 1.5 minutes versus 20.8 ± 2.0 minutes in the propofol group (mean difference
> − 11.1 minutes, 95% confidence interval (CI) − 11.9 to − 10.3; p < 0.001), indicating both non-inferiority
> and, as a post-hoc observation, superiority. Recovery of consciousness (6.7 ± 1.6 vs. 16.6 ± 2.2 minutes,
> p < 0.001) and time to spontaneous respiration (8.5 ± 1.5 vs. 19.4 ± 2.2 minutes, p < 0.001) were also
> significantly shorter with remimazolam.

**20.8 minus 9.7 is the stated 11.1, and the margin was 2 minutes — so the observed difference is 5.6 times
the margin, in the favourable direction.** **A non-inferiority trial whose result overshoots its own margin
by a factor of five and a half was designed for the wrong question.** **The authors handle this correctly and
unusually well: they label superiority as a post-hoc observation rather than claiming it**, which is the
right call, because a trial powered for non-inferiority has not prespecified a superiority test.

**Two things make the effect size itself worth a second look.** **The confidence interval is 1.6 minutes
wide — from −11.9 to −10.3 — implying a standard error of about 0.41 minutes on a difference of 11.1.**
**That is an extraordinarily precise estimate from 76 patients, and it comes from the standard deviations:
1.5 and 2.0 minutes on recovery times in 65-to-80-year-olds having structural heart intervention.**
**Emergence times that tight in an elderly cardiac population are not what most units see**, and a reader
should want the measurement protocol before transporting the number.

**And the person recording the times was not blinded.** The abstract says so: patients were blinded,
**"the treating anesthesiologist, who administered the agents and recorded the emergence times, was not
blinded."** **An unblinded assessor recording the primary outcome, which is a judgement about when a patient
is ready for mask removal, is the single most consequential limitation here** — and it is disclosed rather
than hidden.

**Three further limits, all stated by the authors.** **Retrospectively registered** (ChiCTR2500109737,
registered 24 September 2025); single-centre; **and the norepinephrine difference — 0.06 against 0.17 mg — is
explicitly not interpreted as haemodynamic superiority because of a prophylactic-infusion protocol.**
**Declining to claim a haemodynamic advantage that the numbers would seem to support is the most disciplined
sentence in the paper.** Length of stay did not differ: 10 against 11 days, p = 0.06.

**My reading.** **Remimazolam wakes elderly TAVI patients up faster than propofol, and the direction is not
in doubt.** **What this trial cannot establish is the magnitude**, because the assessor was unblinded on a
subjective endpoint and the precision is implausibly good. **This archive's remimazolam thread now runs
through a first randomised trial, a hepatic-recovery cohort noted on 31 October, and this** — and the open
question in all three is the same: whether faster emergence converts into anything a patient notices.
**Here it did not convert into a shorter hospital stay.**

## 3. A confidence interval that excludes the null and a prediction interval that does not

**[Effect of cardiac-index guided intraoperative haemodynamic therapy on postoperative outcome in non-cardiac
surgery patients](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13741-026-00760-8)** · *Perioperative Medicine* · **published
7 November 2026** · open access

**Twenty-one randomised trials, twenty contributing to the meta-analysis of postoperative complications,
2010 to 2025, PRISMA, PROSPERO-registered, RoB 2, funnel plots, Egger's test, trim-and-fill, leave-one-out.**
**The methods are close to a checklist of everything a meta-analysis should report, and the paper is here for
one number it reports that most do not.** From the abstract:

> CI-GDT significantly reduced the incidence of postoperative complications (OR .59 [.45-.78]; PI .20-1.74;
> I2 = 62.9%). Mortality did not differ significantly between groups (OR 0.77 [.57-1.05]; I2 = 0%). …
> Certainty of evidence was graded high. … However, substantial heterogeneity and a prediction interval
> crossing the null indicate that the benefit may not be consistent across clinical settings.

**The confidence interval runs 0.45 to 0.78 and excludes one. The prediction interval runs 0.20 to 1.74 and
does not.** **Those two intervals answer different questions and the difference is the lesson.** **The
confidence interval asks where the average true effect lies across these twenty trials. The prediction
interval asks what effect the next trial, in the next hospital, should expect** — and it spans 8.7-fold and
includes harm. **With I² at 62.9%, the average is real and the next instance is unpredictable.**

**New rule for this archive, and it is overdue: read the prediction interval, not only the confidence
interval, whenever a random-effects meta-analysis reports one.** **Most do not report one, which is itself
informative** — this archive has published perhaps twenty meta-analyses in three months and this is the first
to give a prediction interval in its abstract. **Where it is absent and I² is substantial, the confidence
interval is being read as though heterogeneity were zero.**

**One tension the paper leaves standing.** **Certainty of evidence was graded high**, and the same abstract
says the benefit may not be consistent across settings. **GRADE's inconsistency domain exists precisely to
downgrade for this, and an I² of 62.9% with a prediction interval crossing the null is the textbook case for
doing so.** **High certainty alongside those two statements is internally inconsistent, and the abstract does
not reconcile it.**

**My reading.** **The clinically defensible statement is the authors' last one, and they should have led with
it:** these findings *"do not establish an independent advantage of CI targeting over other GDT strategies."*
**Goal-directed haemodynamic therapy reduces complications; this analysis does not show that targeting cardiac
index specifically is the reason.** And mortality at 0.77 with an interval reaching 1.05 and I² of zero is
the familiar shape — **a direction with no precision, in which the zero heterogeneity is uninformative rather
than reassuring, because there are too few deaths per trial to detect any.**

## 4. The patients with a small oxygen deficit died less often than the patients with none

**[Early hypoxemia and hypocapnia burdens and 28-day mortality in ICU patients with cardiac
arrest](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rmed.2026.109271)** · *Respiratory Medicine* · **published
5 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**1,028 MIMIC-IV ICU patients with cardiac arrest who survived to 24 hours, of whom 487 — 47.4% — died within
28 days; complete-case models used 972, losing 56 patients, 5.4%.** **Burdens were computed as the integrated
deficit below a PaO2 of 75 mmHg or a PaCO2 of 35 mmHg over the first 24 hours** — area under a threshold,
divided by time. From the abstract:

> In the joint model, the adjusted odds ratio was 1.71 (95% CI 1.20-2.45) per 10 mmHg higher hypoxemia burden
> and 1.88 (1.42-2.47) per 5 mmHg higher hypocapnia burden. For hypoxemia, the high-exposure group had higher
> adjusted odds of death than the no-exposure group (1.95, 1.33-2.85), but the low-exposure group had lower
> odds (0.68, 0.47-0.98). Hypocapnia showed evidence of nonlinearity (P = 0.014); its low-exposure group had
> lower odds than the no-exposure group.

**The categorical analysis contradicts the continuous one, and the paper reports it rather than suppressing
it.** **The continuous model says more oxygen deficit means more death. The categorical model says patients
with a little deficit died less than patients with none at all — odds ratio 0.68, interval 0.47 to 0.98,
which excludes one.** **A monotonic exposure-response and a protective low-exposure category cannot both be
describing the same causal relation.**

**The most likely explanation is not biological and the paper does not offer it, so I will.** **Having zero
hypoxaemia burden over 24 hours in a post-arrest patient is itself a marker, and probably not of health:** it
requires either unusually good lungs or unusually aggressive oxygenation, and in a cohort selected on
surviving 24 hours in intensive care, **the no-exposure group is defined by a treatment decision as much as
by a physiological state.** **The same reading applies to hypocapnia, where the identical pattern appears
with formally tested non-linearity at p = 0.014.** **"No exposure" is not a neutral reference category here;
it is a group constituted by its own management.**

**My reading.** **The headline continuous estimates — 1.71 per 10 mmHg of oxygen deficit, 1.88 per 5 mmHg of
carbon dioxide deficit — should not be carried forward as they stand**, because the paper's own categorical
analysis shows the relation is not monotonic, and a per-unit odds ratio assumes that it is. **What survives
is narrower and still useful: a large oxygen deficit in the first 24 hours after arrest is associated with
death, the relation at low exposure is not what a linear model implies, and the right reference group is not
"none".** **The authors' closing sentence — that the findings warrant prospective validation — is the
correct one, and the burden metric itself, an integrated deficit rather than a single worst value, is a good
idea worth testing properly.**

## 5. Six hundred and eighteen extubation failures, and stridor at three times the background rate

**[Extubation Failure in Pediatric Critical Care: A Secondary Analysis of the 2018-2019 Sedation and Weaning
in Children (SandWiCh) Trial](https://doi.org/10.1097/pcc.0000000000004075)** · *Pediatric Critical Care
Medicine* · **published 5 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**A post hoc, non-prespecified secondary analysis of a cluster-randomised trial across 18 UK paediatric
intensive care units: 9,215 patients with at least one planned extubation attempt.** Extubation failure
defined as emergency reintubation within 48 hours. From the abstract:

> Extubation failure occurred following first planned extubation in 618 of 9215 (6.7%). On multivariable
> analysis, Pediatric Index of Mortality (PIM-3) score and duration of ventilation before extubation attempt
> were associated with greater odds of extubation failure. Of the entire cohort, 874 children (9.4%) had
> postextubation stridor, but this figure rose to 173 of 618 patients (28%) who had extubation failure.

**All three proportions reconstruct: 618 of 9,215 is 6.71%, 874 is 9.48%, and 173 of 618 is 27.99%.**

**The stridor finding is the usable one and the paper states it the right way round.** **Stridor runs at 9.4%
across the whole cohort and 28% among children who failed extubation — a threefold enrichment** — which makes
it a marker rather than a cause, since most children with stridor did not fail. **The number a clinician
needs from this is the reverse conditional, and it is computable from what is given: 173 of the 874 children
with stridor failed extubation, which is 19.8%.** **So four children in five with post-extubation stridor do
not need reintubation.** **That is a materially different message from "28% of failures had stridor", and the
abstract gives only the latter.**

**The predictors are honest and unsurprising.** **Severity at admission and duration of ventilation before
the attempt** — both already known, both unmodifiable at the moment of decision. **The paper does not claim a
novel predictor, and a 9,215-patient dataset confirming that sicker children ventilated longer fail more
often is worth having precisely because it is unglamorous.**

**Two limits, both disclosed.** **This is a post hoc, non-prespecified secondary analysis**, so every
association is exploratory. **And the denominator excludes unplanned extubations and children who died
after first extubation without reintubation** — a reasonable restriction that removes the two groups in whom
the decision was not a weaning judgement.

**One timely connection.** **The core outcome set for ventilation trials published in yesterday's guidelines
watch put "no reintubation within 72 hours" as the second tier of its hierarchical endpoint** — above days
alive without ventilation and below intensive care survival. **This paper uses 48 hours and measures 6.7%.**
**The two definitions are not interchangeable, and a field that has just agreed a 72-hour standard will not
be able to pool the 48-hour literature into it without stating which it used** — which is exactly the
heterogeneity the core outcome set was built to end.
