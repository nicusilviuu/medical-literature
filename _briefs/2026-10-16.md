## 1. Sodium bicarbonate in in-hospital cardiac arrest: nothing, plus alkalosis

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 August 2026** — **eleven weeks old**, 1 citation. **Randomised,
parallel-group, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 21 hospitals in Denmark**, enrolling February 2023 to
February 2026. **Adults with in-hospital cardiac arrest who had received at least one dose of epinephrine**;
**sodium bicarbonate up to 100 mmol or placebo.** 2,913 screened, **913 randomised, 779 in the primary
analysis** — 372 bicarbonate, 407 placebo. Median age 73, 64% male.

> **Results** … **Sustained return of spontaneous circulation occurred in 146 patients (39%) in the sodium
> bicarbonate group and 150 (37%) in the placebo group (risk ratio, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.88-1.24]; P = .62).** At
> 30 days, **45 patients (12%)** in the sodium bicarbonate group and **37 (9.1%)** in the placebo group were
> alive (**risk ratio, 1.25 [0.84-1.88]**); a favorable neurologic outcome at 30 days occurred in **30 patients
> (8.1%) and 22 patients (5.4%)**, respectively (**risk ratio, 1.39 [0.82-2.34]**). **Alkalosis and
> hypernatremia after cardiac arrest were more common in the sodium bicarbonate group.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **These findings do not support routine administration of sodium bicarbonate** for patients
> with in-hospital cardiac arrest.

**Interpretation.** **This is the trial the drug has needed for about forty years, and the primary outcome is
flat.** Sodium bicarbonate has been given in cardiac arrest on physiological reasoning — correct the acidosis,
restore catecholamine responsiveness — with essentially no randomised evidence in adults. **A 39% versus 37%
return of spontaneous circulation, risk ratio 1.05 with an interval from 0.88 to 1.24, is as close to nothing
as a trial of this size can report.** And the harms are not hypothetical: **alkalosis and hypernatraemia**, both
plausible mechanisms for worse cerebral and myocardial recovery, and both more common in the treated arm.

**The honest complication is the secondary outcomes, and they need handling carefully because they all point
the same way.** Thirty-day survival **1.25 (0.84–1.88)** and favourable neurological outcome **1.39
(0.82–2.34)**: both non-significant, both favouring bicarbonate, and **the effect size grows as the outcome
gets harder and rarer.** A monotonic trend across three nested outcomes is the shape a real small benefit
would make — and it is also the shape noise makes when the events thin out and the intervals widen. **The
trial was powered for return of spontaneous circulation, which it answered; it was not powered for 30-day
neurological outcome, where the difference is 30 events against 22.** Nothing here justifies giving
bicarbonate, and nothing here closes the question of whether a larger trial would find a small survival
signal. **Both of those statements are true at once and the paper says so by labelling its conclusion against
routine administration rather than against the drug.**

**Two structural details.** **The arms are unbalanced — 372 against 407 in a 1:1 trial**, after 134 of 913
randomised patients were excluded from the primary analysis. In a cardiac arrest trial with deferred consent
that is unremarkable, but a 35-patient gap is worth noticing. **And the reported risk ratios sit consistently
below the crude ones computed from the counts** — 1.05 against a crude 1.07, 1.25 against 1.33, 1.39 against
1.49. **The most likely explanation is adjustment for site in a 21-hospital trial**, which would shrink
estimates in exactly this way; the abstract does not say, so that is offered as the probable reason rather
than asserted.

## 2. COVALENT: video laryngoscopy wins, and the confidence intervals cannot be right

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA Network Open*, **3 August 2026** — **eleven weeks old**, 0 citations, open
access. **Three-arm randomised trial, six centres in Germany and Austria**, March 2022 to February 2025.
**2,532 adults** undergoing general anaesthesia requiring tracheal intubation, randomised 1:1:1 to **direct
laryngoscopy (848), video laryngoscopy with a Macintosh blade (841), or video laryngoscopy with a
hyperangulated blade (843)**; **2,423 (95.7%) in the modified intention-to-treat analysis.** Devices from
multiple manufacturers were allowed on purpose. **Primary outcome: first-pass success, with success defined by
positive capnography and any withdrawal of blade or tube counting as a failed attempt.**

> **Results** … **Both video laryngoscopy modalities were superior to DL regarding first-pass success (VLM:
> 82.9%, VLH: 87.6%, and DL: 78.2%; all P < .001). Unadjusted absolute risk differences** for first-pass
> intubation success were **4.66 (95% CI, 4.45-4.78) percentage points for DL vs VLM, 9.34 (95% CI, 9.22-9.46)
> percentage points for DL vs VLH, and 4.68 (95% CI, 4.57-4.79) percentage points for VLM vs VLH.** VLM and
> VLH had **faster intubation success after a failed first attempt** (146.6 and 147.5 vs 170.3 seconds;
> P = .008). **There were fewer complications of lip or dental injuries or blood on the blade for VLH compared
> with VLM and DL (10.9% [11 of 101] vs 23.2% [32 of 138] and 24.1% [42 of 174]).**
>
> **Conclusions** … **The findings support the use of video laryngoscopy as a new standard of care for routine
> airway management.**

**Interpretation.** **The result is almost certainly right and the stated precision is not.** Take the
published first-pass rates and arm sizes and compute the risk differences with the method the paper names —
"the z test for unpooled variance" — and the point estimates come out almost exactly as published: **4.70
against 4.66, 9.40 against 9.34, 4.70 against 4.68.** So the differences are reproducible. **The intervals are
not:**

| Comparison | Published 95% CI | Width | Recomputed 95% CI | Width |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DL vs VLM | 4.45 – 4.78 | **0.33 pts** | **0.93 – 8.47** | 7.54 pts |
| DL vs VLH | 9.22 – 9.46 | **0.24 pts** | **5.84 – 12.96** | 7.12 pts |
| VLM vs VLH | 4.57 – 4.79 | **0.22 pts** | **1.32 – 8.08** | 6.76 pts |

**The published intervals are between twenty-three and thirty-one times too narrow.** With roughly 840
patients per arm and proportions near 0.8, the standard error of a risk difference is about **1.8 to 1.9
percentage points**, so a 95% interval spans about **seven points** — not a third of one. **An interval of
4.45 to 4.78 around a difference of 4.66 is the kind of number that comes from a standard error used where a
95% interval was meant, or from a decimal error, or from the interval of something else entirely.** I cannot
tell which from the abstract, and I am computing from rounded percentages and whole-arm denominators rather
than the modified intention-to-treat counts — **but no rounding changes a width by a factor of twenty-three.**

**What matters clinically is that the conclusion survives the error.** The recomputed intervals for all three
comparisons still exclude zero: **DL versus VLM 0.93 to 8.47, DL versus VLH 5.84 to 12.96, VLM versus VLH 1.32
to 8.08.** **Video laryngoscopy is better than direct laryngoscopy at first-pass success, and the
hyperangulated blade is better than the Macintosh blade, on 2,423 patients.** The error is in how precisely
the paper claims to know the size of the advantage, and the honest version is "about 5 points for VLM and
about 9 for VLH, give or take 3 or 4" rather than the two-decimal certainty printed.

**The complication denominators are a second problem and point the same way.** The arms contain 848, 841 and
843 patients, but the complication rates are reported over **174, 138 and 101.** Those numbers are close to the
**number of failed first attempts** in each arm — 185, 144 and 105 by computation from the published success
rates. **So "10.9% versus 23.2% and 24.1%" appears to be the rate among patients who needed more than one
attempt, not the rate among patients intubated.** The abstract does not say so. **On the whole-arm denominator
the same counts give 1.3%, 3.8% and 5.0%** — the same ordering, a completely different magnitude, and the one a
clinician would want. **This is the sixth trial in two weeks whose percentages had to be divided back out to
find out what they were percentages of.**

**For this archive's monitoring thread, this trial is the candidate positive case the thread has been missing —
and it does not quite qualify.** The thread's first entry was videolaryngoscopy as "the measurement changes":
a device that improves the view without clear evidence it improves outcomes. **COVALENT shows the device
improving first-pass success and shortening time to capnography after a failure.** But **first-pass success is
a process outcome**, and the thread asks for a monitored variable moved and a *patient* outcome improved. The
closest thing to a patient outcome here is the airway-trauma comparison, which has the denominator problem
above. **So the entry becomes: the measurement changes, and so does the procedure's efficiency — which is
worth having, and is not the same as showing a patient does better.**

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.25965) · *JAMA Network Open*, NCT05228288

## 3. Two BOX substudies: raising the pressure moves every number except the ones that matter

**Journals · Published:** *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica* and *European Heart Journal: Acute
Cardiovascular Care*, **both 1 August 2026** — **eleven weeks old**, 0 and 2 citations, both open access.
**Two substudies of the same randomised, double-blinded trial: BOX, comatose out-of-hospital cardiac arrest
survivors allocated to a mean arterial pressure target of 63 mmHg or 77 mmHg.** The first uses **pulmonary
artery catheters in 730 patients** over 48 hours; the second takes the **258 patients meeting criteria for
infarction-related cardiogenic shock** (132 low target, 126 high target).

> **Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica** — Among 730 included patients … **mPAP was consistently higher in
> the high-MAP group (mean difference 1.11-1.71 mmHg).** **Calculated PVR was transiently lower in the
> high-MAP group during the first 24 h** … before converging. **RV-CPO was lower in the low-MAP group
> throughout.** **PCWP … was significantly lower in the low-MAP group during the first 12 h.** …
> **Conclusions** … targeting a higher MAP **increased pulmonary artery pressures, PCWP, RV-CPO, heart rate,
> and cardiac output. The proportionally greater increase in cardiac output over pulmonary artery pressures
> resulted in a decreased calculated PVR.**
>
> **European Heart Journal: Acute Cardiovascular Care** — **The high-target group received significantly higher
> doses of noradrenaline.** CI increased over time in both groups … **Despite 8-19% higher CIs during the first
> 24 h, the high-target group consistently exhibited higher PCWPs. SvO2 was higher in the high-target group.
> Lactate levels were comparable and, on average, normalized in both groups within 6 h. Mortality, need for
> RRT, and neurological outcomes did not differ significantly.** **Conclusion** … higher noradrenaline-mediated
> MAP **increased CI and SvO2 but did not enhance tissue perfusion or clinical outcomes, and were associated
> with higher left-sided filling pressures.**

**Interpretation.** **Read together these two papers say something sharper than either says alone: pushing the
mean arterial pressure up with noradrenaline moves essentially every number a pulmonary artery catheter can
report, and moves neither tissue perfusion nor outcome.** Pulmonary artery pressure up. Wedge pressure up.
Cardiac index up by 8 to 19%. Mixed venous saturation up. Right ventricular power output up. **Lactate: the
same, normalising in both groups within six hours. Mortality at 365 days, renal replacement therapy, and
neurological outcome: no difference.**

**The wedge pressure is the detail that makes this more than a null.** Higher filling pressures on the left
side, sustained, in patients who have just had a myocardial infarction and a cardiac arrest, is not a neutral
cost — it is the physiology of pulmonary congestion. **So the high target is not "no benefit"; it is a dose of
noradrenaline that buys numbers and pays in filling pressure.**

**And the calculated pulmonary vascular resistance is a textbook case of a derived index moving for the wrong
reason, which the authors say outright.** PVR is the pressure gradient across the lung divided by cardiac
output. **The intervention raised cardiac output proportionally more than it raised the gradient, so the
quotient fell** — and the authors write exactly that. **Nothing in the pulmonary vasculature need have
changed.** For this archive's monitoring thread that is an eighth failure mode and the most mechanical one
yet: **the index is a ratio, and the intervention changed the denominator.** Anyone reading "PVR fell on the
higher target" as pulmonary vasodilation would be reading an arithmetic consequence as a biological one.

**One loop to close, because this archive opened it against itself.** On **9 October** I wrote, from memory and
without verification, that "COMACARE, NEUROPROTECT and the Danish BOX trial all compared higher against lower
MAP targets and all were neutral", then removed it and recorded it as the closest this archive had come to
fabricating a result. **The BOX part of that sentence is now verified and citable: BOX did randomise 63 against
77 mmHg, and in the cardiogenic shock subgroup mortality, renal replacement therapy and neurological outcome
did not differ.** **COMACARE and NEUROPROTECT remain unverified here and are still not being claimed.** One
third of a bad sentence turning out to be true is not a vindication of writing it.

**With these two, the pressure-targeting thread stands at seven documents** and the hole in it is unchanged:
**every one of them measures physiology or reports a neutral outcome, and none is an adequately powered trial
with an outcome primary endpoint.** BOX comes closest, and these are its substudies rather than its primary
report.
