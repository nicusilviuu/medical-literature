## 1. Four null outcomes, and one of them is a day and a half of ventilation

**[Safety and efficacy of volatile sedatives in critically ill patients receiving mechanical ventilation: A
systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057x261496935)** — *Anaesthesia and Intensive
Care*, article 310057X261496935, **first published 26 October 2026**. Abstract read through the Europe PMC
index; no PMCID and not flagged open access, so the full text is not recoverable.

**From 3,182 identified publications, fifteen studies** — randomised and observational — comparing volatile
with intravenous sedation in ventilated adults, searched to July 2025. **All four outcomes are reported as
null:**

| Outcome | Effect | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delirium | RR **0.72** | 0.38 to 1.34 |
| Mortality | RR **0.97** | 0.80 to 1.17 |
| ICU length of stay | MD **+0.28 days** | −1.74 to +2.30 |
| **Duration of mechanical ventilation** | MD **−37.20 hours** | **−78.60 to +4.20** |

**Three of these are genuinely null and one is not the same kind of thing.** Mortality at RR 0.97 with an
interval from 0.80 to 1.17 is a tight null. ICU stay at +0.28 days inside ±2 days is a tight null. Delirium at
RR 0.72 looks like a 28% reduction, but the interval runs from 0.38 to 1.34 — **reconstructing from the
interval, the implied z is about 1.0 and the two-sided p about 0.31** — so it is an imprecise estimate with no
signal, not a suppressed benefit.

**Ventilation duration is different, and the abstract does not say so.** **−37.20 hours is one and a half
days, and the interval misses zero by 4.2 hours.** **The interval is exactly symmetric — 41.40 hours either
side — so the implied standard error is 21.12 hours, the z is 1.76 and the two-sided p is about 0.078.**

**That is reported twice as "not associated with significant differences", once in the results and once in the
conclusion, and it is listed first in the conclusion's list of nulls.** **A point estimate of a day and a half
less ventilation at p = 0.08 across fifteen heterogeneous studies is not a demonstrated benefit — but it is
also not the same statement as "no difference in mortality", and putting the two in one sentence makes them
look alike.**

**My reading.** **The honest summary is that volatile sedation has not been shown to change anything that
matters, and that the outcome most likely to be real is the one closest to the drug's mechanism.** A volatile
agent is eliminated by the lungs rather than metabolised, so a shorter time to wakefulness and extubation is
the effect one would predict; delirium and mortality are several steps downstream. **The mechanistic ordering
and the statistical ordering agree here, which is mildly reassuring about the 37 hours and says nothing about
the other three.** The call for "large-scale, high-quality randomised trials" is right, and the trial it
implies should be powered on ventilator-free days rather than on delirium.

**Archive context: volatile ICU sedation has not appeared in this archive before.** The nearest items are
sedation-adjacent rather than the same question — the dexmedetomidine delirium meta-analysis in the
[25 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-25/) and the ASGE remimazolam practice update in the
[17 October guidelines entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-17/).

## 2. Two sedation papers, one journal, one day — and both are really about who is giving it

*Pacing and Clinical Electrophysiology* published both of these on **26 October 2026**. They are separate
studies on separate populations, and the reason to read them together is that each one's finding is a property
of the arrangement rather than of a drug.

### 2a. The anaesthetic suppresses the arrhythmia the procedure exists to find

**[Sedation Versus General Anesthesia During Pediatric Electrophysiology Studies: A Paired
Comparison](https://doi.org/10.1111/pace.70474)**

**Fifty-one patients aged 5–21 with supraventricular tachycardia underwent electrophysiology study under both
procedural sedation and general anaesthesia in the same session** — a paired design, which removes
between-patient confounding almost entirely and is the right way to ask this question. Sedation was midazolam,
fentanyl and supplemental propofol boluses; general anaesthesia was propofol and fentanyl induction with
sevoflurane maintenance.

> **Overall tachycardia inducibility was higher during sedation than during general anesthesia (40/51 [78.4%]
> vs. 29/51 [56.9%], p = 0.007).**

**No electrophysiological parameter differed** — not atrial or ventricular effective refractory period, not the
atrioventricular nodal Wenckebach point, not jump, echo interval or ventriculoatrial block duration. **The
measured substrate looked the same; what changed was whether the arrhythmia could be provoked.**

**The subgroups, and the arithmetic reconstructs exactly.** AVNRT fell from 84.0% to 60.0% (p = 0.109) and
WPW/AVRT from 73.1% to 53.8% (p = 0.063). **Those percentages are only possible with n = 25 and n = 26, which
sum to the 51 patients exactly: 21→15 and 19→14, a loss of six and of five, totalling the eleven lost
overall.** So the two subgroups partition the cohort and each contributes about half the effect.

**The paper's own description of this is a model of how to report an underpowered subgroup:**

> The AVNRT and WPW/AVRT subgroups showed **numerically lower inducibility under general anesthesia in the same
> direction as the overall finding, although these subgroup differences did not reach statistical
> significance.**

**It claims direction and consistency and explicitly declines to claim significance** — which is what two
subgroups of twenty-five support. One subgroup finding does reach significance and is mechanistically
coherent: **the accessory pathway effective refractory period was prolonged under general anaesthesia
(p = 0.018)** in the WPW group, which is a plausible route to lower inducibility in exactly the patients where
it was measured.

**My reading.** **This is the cleanest example this archive has logged of an intervention that alters the
instrument measuring its own effect** — the pattern recorded repeatedly over the past fortnight, here in its
purest form. **The anaesthetic is not a background condition of the electrophysiology study; it is part of the
measurement.** A non-inducible study under general anaesthesia cannot be read as absence of substrate, and —
more consequentially — **post-ablation validation depends on failing to induce the arrhythmia, so an anaesthetic
that suppresses inducibility can make an incomplete ablation look successful.** The conclusion's call for
"individualized anesthetic planning that considers the arrhythmia substrate" is the right instruction and
understates the problem: **the plan has to be fixed before the diagnostic phase and held constant through the
validation phase, or the two measurements are not comparable with each other.**

### 2b. A sedation comparison in which the sedationist is the operator

**[The Effectiveness and Safety of Deep Sedation Protocols for Pulsed Field Ablation of Atrial
Fibrillation](https://doi.org/10.1111/pace.70457)**

**Prospective, single centre, 166 patients undergoing focal-form pulsed field ablation**, allocated to three
**electrophysiologist-led** deep sedation protocols: midazolam + fentanyl (n = 53), flurbiprofen axetil +
midazolam + fentanyl (n = 67), or dexmedetomidine + fentanyl (n = 46). Primary endpoint: **Ramsay Sedation
Score ≥ 3 at ablation initiation.**

| Protocol | Target sedation reached | Reconstructed count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Midazolam + fentanyl | **47.2%** | 25 of 53 |
| Flurbiprofen + midazolam + fentanyl | **73.1%** | 49 of 67 |
| Dexmedetomidine + fentanyl | **82.6%** | 38 of 46 |

Overall p < 0.001; the two better arms did not differ from each other. **Dexmedetomidine gave the lowest
intraoperative pain scores (p < 0.001) and the most intraoperative hypotension (p = 0.047).** Both of the
better arms scored lower on sedation difficulty and higher on operator satisfaction.

**Three things are worth saying plainly.** **First, the authors pre-empt the obvious objection and the
wording is careful:** the conclusion says the comparison is against *"the specific low-dose Midazolam
regimen"*, not against midazolam. **A 47.2% target attainment rate is a statement about a dose, and the paper
declines to generalise it to a drug.**

**Second, operator satisfaction is a secondary endpoint in a study whose sedation is given by the operator.**
That is not a criticism of the measurement — satisfaction matters, and an unsettled patient during ablation is
a real safety problem — but **it is the kind of endpoint whose value depends on who is answering, and here the
person answering is the person who chose and delivered the drug.**

**Third, the hypotension finding is the one to carry forward and it is the weakest-powered.** **p = 0.047 in a
46-patient arm**, in a non-randomised allocation, for the complication that matters most in a deeply sedated
patient undergoing left atrial instrumentation. **It is a signal to be confirmed, not a quantified risk** —
and dexmedetomidine's haemodynamic cost is already a recurring item in this archive.

**Archive context, and it is direct.** **Yesterday's guidelines entry reported the 2026 APHRS atrial
fibrillation guidelines, whose Part III contains a periprocedural management section for catheter ablation**
([26 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/)). **Today's paper is evidence for exactly that
section, arriving the day after, and it concerns a procedural choice the guideline does not settle.** The
dexmedetomidine hypotension signal also lands against the same guideline's **Class IIb, Level B** recommendation
for intravenous dexmedetomidine-class agents in haemodynamically unstable patients — different population,
same drug, same direction of caution.

## 3. A resuscitation comparison whose contribution is deciding who counts as a control

**[Earlier Versus Later Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation in Refractory Out-of-Hospital Cardiac
Arrest: A Nationwide Sequential Landmark Target Trial Emulation](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjacc/zuag132)** —
*European Heart Journal: Acute Cardiovascular Care*, article zuag132, **first published 25 October 2026**.

**The stated problem is a methodological one, and it is the reason the paper exists:**

> …the optimal timing of initiation remains uncertain, **partly because prior comparisons did not restrict
> controls to patients still in refractory arrest at the same decision point.**

**That sentence is the whole study.** If you compare patients who received extracorporeal CPR against all
patients who did not, the control group contains people who achieved return of circulation before the question
of cannulation ever arose — the ones who were going to do well. **The comparison then measures the selection,
not the treatment.**

**The fix: target trial emulation with a sequential landmark design**, in the Korean nationwide out-of-hospital
cardiac arrest registry, 2016–2023. Among patients with presumed cardiac aetiology **still receiving emergency
department CPR 15 minutes after arrival**, landmarks were set at **15, 30 and 60 minutes**, each comparing ECPR
started within the following window against **no ECPR during that window among patients still in refractory
arrest**. Propensity score overlap weighting with **22 covariates**.

| Landmark | 30-day survival, ECPR | Control | Adjusted risk difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 15 min | **31.0%** | 16.0% | **+15.0 points** (8.0 to 22.0) |
| 30 min | **21.6%** | 7.2% | **+14.4** (10.4 to 18.4) |
| 60 min | **10.9%** | 3.2% | **+7.7** (3.7 to 11.7) |

All p < 0.001; favourable neurological outcomes followed the same pattern; the exploratory gradient analysis
found the benefit shrinking with longer door-to-ECMO time **but remaining significant through 120 minutes**.

**Every number checks.** Each adjusted risk difference equals the difference of the weighted survivals exactly,
and each interval is exactly symmetric about its point estimate.

**The headline claim is about the 60-minute rule:**

> Benefit was largest with early initiation but **did not disappear at the commonly applied 60-minute
> benchmark, which may be overly restrictive for selected patients in capable systems.**

**My reading.** **The design genuinely fixes the control-group problem and does not fix the selection
problem, and the paper's own numbers show why.** **909 patients of 58,668 received ECPR — 1.55%.** Twenty-two
covariates and overlap weighting can balance what was recorded; they cannot balance the judgement that made a
team cannulate this patient and not the forty-nine like them. **The patients chosen for ECPR after an hour of
refractory arrest are, by construction, the ones in whom an experienced team still saw a reason to continue** —
witnessed, shockable, young, a recognised reversible cause. **That is precisely the quantity the adjusted
estimate cannot see.**

**So the 60-minute finding should be read in the direction the authors wrote it and no further.** They say the
benchmark *"may be overly restrictive **for selected patients in capable systems**"* — two qualifiers, both
load-bearing. **The comparator at that landmark is a 3.2% survival with continued conventional CPR, which is
the strongest part of the case: at one hour of refractory arrest, the alternative is close to nothing**, so even
a heavily selected 10.9% is worth having. **The weakest part is the implication that the rule should move for
unselected patients, which these data cannot support and do not claim.**

**Archive context: this is the first ECPR timing study in this archive.** ECMO has appeared in the backlog —
the SCCM/ELSO joint adult ECMO consensus statement is still listed as in development, and an ECMO-in-COVID
cohort sits in the brief queue — but the question of when to cannulate a refractory arrest has not.

## 4. Three findings about cancelled operations, all of which reduce to whether the bed can be taken

**[The Association Between Perioperative Enhanced Care Unit Characteristics and Surgical Cancelation: A
Nationwide Observational Study](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008309)** — *Anesthesia & Analgesia*,
**first published 26 October 2026**.

**The question is organisational and the outcome is concrete:** elective operations cancelled, counted as
cancellations divided by elective referrals to each facility, over three months in late 2023, across UK
hospitals with both an enhanced care unit and a critical care unit. **65 sites with complete data; 28 had an
enhanced care facility with enough activity to analyse; 4,112 patients referred.** Multilevel regression, cases
nested within hospitals.

**The three significant features:**

| Feature of the enhanced care unit | Odds ratio for cancellation | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beds **reserved exclusively for elective cases** | **0.54** | 0.39 to 0.76 |
| Clinical care involving **multiple specialties** | **0.48** | 0.33 to 0.69 |
| **Emergency admissions permitted** | **1.61** | 1.06 to 2.43 |

All three intervals are symmetric on the log scale, so the reporting is internally consistent.

**My reading, and it is two observations.**

**First, the finding is close to a tautology, and that is not a criticism.** **A bed ring-fenced for elective
work halves the odds of cancellation; a bed that emergencies can occupy raises them by 61%.** Those are the
same fact stated twice, and the "multiple specialties" result is plausibly a third statement of it — a unit
serving several specialties is a unit with enough throughput to protect capacity, rather than one whose single
list collapses when one case overruns. **Nobody needed a study to suspect that protected capacity protects
elective work. What was missing was the size of the effect**, and an odds ratio of about 0.5 against about 1.6
is a usable number for a business case in a way that the intuition never was.

**Second, the precision of those intervals should not be read as the precision of the inference.** **The
exposures are properties of hospitals and there are 28 hospitals.** The intervals are computed over 4,112
patients nested within those 28 sites, and a two-level model is the right tool for exactly that, but **the
effective sample size for a facility-level characteristic is the number of facilities.** **Three structural
features estimated across 28 units, in a three-month window, are a small and correlated set** — a hospital that
ring-fences elective beds is likely also the hospital that keeps emergencies out and runs a multi-specialty
unit, so the three "separate" findings may be one organisational type observed three ways. The abstract gives
no correlation between features and no count of how many of the 28 had each.

**The conclusion is appropriately hedged** — *"may explain why cancelation rates in enhanced care are typically
lower than within critical care and may have important policy implications"* — and the policy implication is
the honest one: **if an enhanced care unit is built and then used as an overflow for emergencies, the thing
that made it work has been removed.**

**Archive context: this is the second organisational document in two days.** Yesterday's guidelines entry
carried the **Swedish national consensus on follow-up after ICU stay**
([26 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/)), which is also about who enters a pathway and under
what rule, and which also makes no outcome claim — it argues that the absence of a rule produces inequity.
**Today's paper is the same argument with a measured effect size attached, from the front end of the admission
rather than the back.**
