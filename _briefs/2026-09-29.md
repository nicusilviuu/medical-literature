**Three papers published within two days, all in *Anesthesiology*, all about what to titrate to
during surgery.** Two are negative about arterial pressure. The third, from cardiac surgery, is
positive about oxygen delivery. Read together they make an argument neither makes alone.

## 1. HPI versus a higher MAP target — the trial the September evidence called for

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **29 September 2026** — **published today.** Open-label,
parallel-group, two-centre randomised controlled trial
Wu, Chen, Tu, Wang, Tsai, Chan, Hsu, Wu

**The background section is, almost verbatim, the paper this archive reported on 10 September:**

> Although previous randomized trials reported reduced hypotension with HPI-guided management, these
> findings **may partly reflect performance bias from open-label designs and selection bias in
> algorithm validation. Recent observational evidence suggests that much of HPI's apparent benefit may
> result from earlier treatment at higher mean arterial pressure (MAP) thresholds, supporting direct
> randomized comparison with a proactive higher MAP target.**

**100 adults** undergoing major noncardiac surgery with invasive arterial monitoring, randomised to
**HPI-guided management (trigger: HPI ≥ 85)** or a **higher MAP-targeted strategy (trigger: MAP ≤ 73
mmHg)** — **both following the same predefined haemodynamic protocol.** Primary endpoint
**time-weighted average hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg)**, testing **superiority** of HPI.

> Median TWA hypotension was **0.07 (0 to 0.20) mmHg in the HPI group and 0.16 (0.02 to 0.50) mmHg in
> the higher MAP target group (P = 0.119)**. Area under the threshold **22 (0 to 96) vs 59.7 (5.7 to
> 119.3) mmHg·min (P = 0.172)**. Hypertension burden did not differ. **Noradrenaline dose, length of
> hospital stay and 30-day mortality did not differ.**

> **HPI-guided management was not superior to a proactive MAP ≤ 73 mmHg strategy. Because this trial
> was not designed to test equivalence or noninferiority, the findings indicate no demonstrated
> superiority rather than clinical equivalence** between the trigger strategies.

**Interpretation.** This is the cleanest example this archive has seen of a hypothesis being posed and
then tested, and the design choice that matters is the comparator.

**The [10 September paper](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-10/) argued that HPI trials were
comparing two MAP thresholds rather than prediction against no prediction** — because **HPI alerts
fire at a MAP of roughly 70–75 mmHg**, while the usual comparator arm waits for 65. It found that
among 13 trials reducing hypotension, 9 reported significantly greater haemodynamic treatment in the
HPI arm, and among the 5 that failed, none did. **This trial sets the comparator trigger at MAP ≤ 73
mmHg — squarely inside that 70–75 window — and runs both arms on the same protocol.** It is the
experiment the earlier analysis specified, and it answers the question it was designed to answer:
**once you let the control arm treat at the pressure the algorithm effectively treats at, the
algorithm's advantage is no longer demonstrable.**

**The authors' final sentence is the model of how to report this, and it should not be paraphrased
away.** *"No demonstrated superiority rather than clinical equivalence."* **A P value of 0.119 in 100
patients is not proof of no difference** — the point estimates numerically favour HPI (0.07 versus
0.16 mmHg TWA; 22 versus 60 mmHg·min), and a trial this size could easily miss a real effect. **What
it does establish is that the simple strategy is not obviously worse**, which is a different and more
useful claim than "the monitor does not work."

**The commercial reading is unavoidable and is the practical point.** HPI requires a proprietary
algorithm and a specific monitoring platform. **Setting a MAP alarm at 73 requires changing a number.**
If a randomised comparison at the relevant threshold cannot show superiority for the former, the
burden shifts: **the case for the device now has to be made on something other than hypotension
burden**, and no such case is offered here.

**Note the honesty about the trial's own limitations in the background.** The authors name
**performance bias from open-label designs** as a reason to doubt the earlier positive trials — and
**their own trial is open-label**, which cuts both ways and which they do not hide.

## 2. Protocolised pressure targets, across fifteen trials, do not beat usual care

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **28 September 2026** — **yesterday.** Bayesian
random-effects meta-analysis
**Ripollés-Melchor**, Espinosa, **Monge-García**, Aldecoa, **Malbrain**, Lorente, Quintana-Villamandos,
Navarro-Pérez, Abad-Motos and colleagues

Searched to January 2026 with surveillance to June 2026; **randomised trials comparing protocolised
intraoperative arterial pressure-targeting strategies with usual care or lower-pressure approaches**.
Primary outcome **all-cause mortality**; secondary **AKI** and **myocardial injury after noncardiac
surgery**. Bayesian random-effects as the prespecified primary framework.

> **Fifteen randomized trials.** Mortality (13 trials) **centered around an OR of 1.00** (frequentist
> 1.00, 95% CI 0.83–1.21; Bayesian median 1.00, 95% CrI 0.73–1.38), with **wide Bayesian prediction
> intervals (OR 0.56–1.78)**. **AKI (14 trials) showed the most favorable estimates** (OR 0.87, 95% CI
> 0.73–1.03; Bayesian median 0.88, 95% CrI 0.70–1.05), **but estimates remained compatible with no
> clinically important benefit**, heterogeneity was moderate, and **leave-one-out analyses showed
> sensitivity to individual-trial exclusion.** MINS (8 trials) **compatible with no treatment effect**
> (OR 1.04).

**Interpretation.** The meta-analytic frame around item 1, and it widens the conclusion considerably.

**Item 1 says a particular algorithm does not beat a particular pressure threshold. This says
protocolised pressure targeting as a whole does not beat usual care** — on mortality, on myocardial
injury, and probably on kidney injury too. **Fifteen randomised trials is a mature literature**, and
an odds ratio centred on exactly 1.00 for mortality is not an ambiguous result.

**The Bayesian prediction interval is the number to take away, and most readers will skip it.** A
prediction interval of **0.56 to 1.78** describes the range within which the effect in a *future*
trial is expected to fall. **It means the next trial could plausibly show a 44% reduction or a 78%
increase in mortality** — which is a statement about how heterogeneous this literature is, not just
how uncertain the pooled estimate is. **Pooling trials whose true effects differ that much produces an
average that may describe no actual patient.**

**The AKI handling is exemplary and should be read as a template.** It is the most favourable estimate
in the paper; the authors report that it is **compatible with no clinically important benefit**, that
**heterogeneity was moderate**, and that **excluding single trials in turn moves it** — three separate
reasons for caution attached to their own most positive finding. **Compare that with the papers this
archive has criticised for quoting a favourable interval and stopping.**

**And the exploratory finding points somewhere specific**: *"any potential renal benefit may depend on
the broader hemodynamic context in which arterial pressure targets are implemented."* **Which is to
say: pressure alone is the wrong variable — the question is what else is happening when you achieve
it.** That leads directly to the third paper.

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1097/aln.0000000000006395) · PMID 42803396

## 3. Oxygen delivery on bypass does track mortality — 4,358 patients

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **28 September 2026** — **yesterday.** Retrospective cohort
Robitaille, Sahli, Wachtendorf, Braeuer, Liebich, Spear, Khabbaz, Kai, **Mahmood**, Picker,
**Schaefer**, Marcus

**4,358 adults** undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass at a tertiary centre,
2018–2024. **Time-weighted average indexed oxygen delivery (DO₂i)** calculated from **pump flow,
arterial oxygen saturation, haemoglobin concentration and body surface area.** Primary outcome
**30-day mortality**.

> **75 patients (1.7%) died within 30 days.** Each **10 ml·min⁻¹·m⁻² decrease in DO₂i** was associated
> with **increased odds of mortality (adjusted OR 1.16; 95% CI 1.09–1.23; P < 0.001)**. Adjusted
> 30-day mortality was **1.69% at the 25th percentile of DO₂i (226.2)** compared with **0.74% at the
> 75th percentile (295.6)**. Lower DO₂i was also associated with **AKI (OR 1.05)**, longer ICU and
> hospital stay, **but not with stroke (OR 1.00; 95% CI 0.95–1.06)**.

**Interpretation.** The counterweight to the first two papers, and the contrast is the point.

**In noncardiac surgery, targeting a pressure does not improve outcomes. In cardiac surgery, the
amount of oxygen actually delivered tracks mortality.** DO₂i is not a pressure — it is **flow ×
haemoglobin × saturation**, a genuine delivery quantity. **This archive has run a "perfusion, not
pressure" thread since early September** — the [carotid cannula
study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-19/), the [beach-chair
trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-21/), [noradrenaline dose versus microvascular
reactivity](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-28/) — **and this is its most quantitative instance: the
variable that is about delivery behaves, where the variable that is about pressure does not.**

**But the design cannot carry a causal claim, and two features argue for caution.**

**First, DO₂i is partly a haemoglobin measurement.** A patient with a low DO₂i on bypass is often a
patient who is anaemic — and preoperative anaemia is one of the best-established predictors of death
after cardiac surgery, for reasons that have nothing to do with pump flow. **Adjustment for
"demographic, clinical and procedural factors" may not separate those.**

**Second, the stroke null is informative and under-discussed.** If low DO₂i were causing global
hypoperfusion injury, stroke would be a natural place to see it; **the odds ratio is 1.00 (0.95–1.06),
as flat as a result gets.** That is compatible with DO₂i marking patient substrate rather than driving
organ injury — or with stroke on bypass being embolic rather than hypoperfusive, which is the
conventional view. **Either way it constrains the mechanism**, and the paper's own conclusion is
appropriately limited to providing *"a rationale for future mechanistic and interventional studies."*

**Seventy-five deaths supporting a multivariable model is the other limit.** A rule of thumb of ten
events per variable leaves little room in a model adjusting for demographics, clinical and procedural
factors simultaneously.
