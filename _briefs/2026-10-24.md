## 1. PROSPECT names the proprietary formulation it will not endorse

**Journal · Published:** *Anaesthesia*, **22 October 2026** — **two days old**, 0 citations. **Systematic
review using PROSPECT (PROcedure SPECific Postoperative Pain ManagemenT) methodology**, issuing
procedure-specific recommendations for total shoulder arthroplasty. **1,809 articles screened, 30 randomised
trials and 7 systematic reviews included — 2.05%.** **This is the first PROSPECT document in this archive.**

> **Interventions that improved postoperative pain relief included: paracetamol and non-steroidal
> anti-inflammatory drugs; dexamethasone; interscalene brachial plexus block and, as an alternative, local
> infiltration analgesia. Conflicting evidence was found for continuous interscalene brachial plexus block
> (catheter) or liposomal bupivacaine for interscalene block or local infiltration analgesia. No evidence was
> found for a specific surgical technique.**
>
> In the absence of contraindication, the analgesic regimen should include: **paracetamol; a non-steroidal
> anti-inflammatory drug administered peri-operatively and continued postoperatively; intravenous
> dexamethasone (8-10 mg); and postoperative opioids for rescue analgesia. As a regional technique, an
> interscalene brachial plexus block with a single-bolus injection of long-acting local anaesthetic
> (bupivacaine or ropivacaine), but not liposomal bupivacaine, should be provided.**

**Interpretation.** **The structural achievement is the three-way sort, and it is the thing this archive has
spent a week looking for in guidelines.** **Interventions are placed in one of three bins — improved,
conflicting evidence, no evidence — and then a regimen is issued.** **A reader learns not only what to do but
which of the available options the evidence does not support, which is the disclosure that most guideline
abstracts omit.** Yesterday's guidelines watch noted that a Canadian spinal-procedures guideline used USPSTF
grades A to D plus insufficient precisely because GRADE has no explicit "do not do this" category. **PROSPECT
reaches the same place by a different route: it does not grade, it bins.**

**And it uses that structure to do something unusual: name a specific proprietary formulation and decline to
recommend it.** **"An interscalene brachial plexus block with a single-bolus injection of long-acting local
anaesthetic (bupivacaine or ropivacaine), but not liposomal bupivacaine."** **Liposomal bupivacaine is
expensive, heavily promoted, and the subject of a long argument about whether its extended-release profile
delivers clinical benefit.** **PROSPECT found the evidence conflicting and wrote the negative into the
recommendation rather than leaving it in the discussion.** **That is the most consequential sentence in the
document and the one most likely to be contested.**

**The clinically actionable detail is specific enough to use, which is also rarer than it should be.**
**Intravenous dexamethasone 8–10 mg**, a named dose range rather than "consider dexamethasone". Paracetamol
and an NSAID started intraoperatively and continued. Opioids for rescue only. **Interscalene block as the
regional technique, single bolus, with local infiltration analgesia as the fallback where there is a
contraindication or no expertise** — and naming absence of expertise as a legitimate reason to choose the
alternative is a practical concession most recommendations avoid making.

**Two limits.** **"No evidence was found for a specific surgical technique"** is reported plainly and means the
review found nothing to say about how the operation itself affects pain — a null about surgery inside an
analgesia review, which is honest and leaves a gap. **And the open question named is the one the conflicting
evidence created: whether a continuous interscalene infusion adds anything over a single bolus.** **Thirty
trials and seven reviews were not enough to settle a question about a catheter**, which says something about
how the regional anaesthesia literature allocates its effort.

[Read the recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.70427) · *Anaesthesia*

## 2. A mortality odds ratio of 0.35, which the authors themselves call selection bias

**Journal · Published:** *European Journal of Pediatrics*, **23 October 2026** — **one day old**, 0 citations.
**Systematic review and meta-analysis**, searched to 30 October 2025, risk of bias by ROBINS-I. **Twelve
retrospective single-centre studies, 1,323 patients — 1,158 unique children after correcting for two
centre-level cohort overlaps.** Immediate extubation in the operating room versus delayed extubation after
paediatric orthotopic liver transplantation.

> **IE was associated with lower odds of reintubation (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.17-0.59) and mortality (OR 0.35, 95%
> CI 0.18-0.68); both estimates were confirmed by a correction-free Peto odds ratio (0.29 and 0.31,
> respectively).** … **IE was associated with shorter PICU LOS (SMD −0.88, 95% CI −1.39 to −0.37), hospital LOS
> (SMD −1.16, 95% CI −2.00 to −0.31).** … **Meta-regression identified intraoperative transfusion differences
> as predictor of hospital LOS (β = −0.045, p = 0.006, R² = 57.1%), a pattern more consistent with confounding
> by indication than with a beneficial mechanism of transfusion itself.** …
>
> **these findings derive exclusively from retrospective studies at high risk of selection bias, and causality
> cannot be established. Children undergoing IE were likely older and in better preoperative condition** …
> **These associations likely reflect selection bias rather than a proven causal benefit.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the most self-critical abstract this archive has reported, and the discipline is
worth setting out because it is the opposite of what this week has mostly found.** **A mortality odds ratio of
0.35 is a headline most authors would defend. These authors report it, confirm it with a second estimator, and
then write in their own key points that it probably is not real.**

**Three specific pieces of method deserve naming.** **First, the cohort-overlap correction: 1,323 records
reduced to 1,158 unique children, 165 patients — 12.5% — removed as double-counted across overlapping
single-centre series.** **Double-counting across retrospective series from the same transplant centres is the
classic silent inflation in this literature, and almost nobody checks.** **Second, the correction-free Peto
odds ratio, reported alongside the main estimate for both outcomes.** Peto avoids the continuity corrections
that distort sparse-data odds ratios, and 0.29 against 0.32 and 0.31 against 0.35 shows the finding is not an
artefact of the correction. **Third, and best, the meta-regression interpretation.** They found intraoperative
transfusion predicting hospital stay with an R² of 57.1% and said that is **"more consistent with confounding
by indication than with a beneficial mechanism of transfusion itself."** **Most papers report a significant
meta-regression predictor as a mechanism. This one reports it as evidence that the whole analysis is
confounded.**

**The authors are right to disown the mortality figure, and there is an argument they do not make that
strengthens their case.** **The causal pathway from "extubated in the operating theatre" to "does not die" is
thin.** Reintubation, plausibly yes — extubation timing is directly upstream of it. Length of stay, plausibly
yes. **But a halving of mortality cannot sensibly be produced by the timing of tracheal tube removal in a
child who has just received a liver.** **The implausibility of the mechanism is itself evidence for the
confounding they describe: immediate extubation is what you choose for the older, better-conditioned child,
and that child was always going to do better.**

**The subgroup handling is correct and worth noting as a contrast with this week.** **Donor-type subgroups
showed effects in the same direction but no within-subgroup estimate reached significance, and the abstract
says so without claiming effect modification.** **Compare the vasopressor meta-analysis reported here on
22 October, whose "benefit mainly appeared in more severe patients" rested on a subgroup with a point estimate
identical to the overall one.**

**What the review does establish, and it is useful: immediate extubation appears feasible and safe in selected
children.** **That is a statement about absence of signal for harm in the group who get it, which is all
twelve retrospective single-centre studies can support, and it is what the authors conclude.** **Prospective
multicentre work is named as the requirement.**

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00431-026-07544-8) · *European Journal of Pediatrics*

## 3. Fospropofol exists to be gentler than propofol, and the review cannot show that it is

**Journal · Published:** *European Journal of Clinical Pharmacology*, **22 October 2026** — **two days old**,
0 citations. **Systematic review** (PROSPERO CRD42023477740, registered before initiation), searched to
26 August 2026, RoB 2, GRADE, and SWiM for narrative synthesis. **Ten randomised trials: six procedural
sedation, four ICU sedation. Quantitative synthesis for only two comparable evidence groups — eight of the ten
studies are synthesised narratively.**

> **In three endoscopic procedural-sedation trials, fospropofol 6.5 mg/kg achieved a higher sedation success
> than 2 mg/kg (RR 3.27, 95% CI 2.62-4.08). In mechanically ventilated ICU patients, hypotension risk did not
> clearly differ between fospropofol and propofol (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.67-1.28).** … **Paresthesia/pruritus was
> commonly reported, while rates of cardiorespiratory adverse events varied across settings and doses and
> showed no consistent comparative advantage for fospropofol.** … **Certainty of evidence ranged from high to
> very low** … **Current evidence does not establish a consistent cardiorespiratory safety advantage for
> fospropofol over other sedatives.**

**Interpretation.** **Fospropofol is a water-soluble prodrug of propofol, and the reason to make such a thing
is tolerability: no lipid emulsion, no injection pain, and a hoped-for gentler cardiorespiratory profile.**
**The review's conclusion is that the cardiorespiratory advantage is not established, and the one pooled
comparison with propofol — hypotension risk, RR 0.93 (0.67–1.28) — is a null with an interval wide enough to
contain a 33% reduction and a 28% increase.** **The drug's rationale is the thing the evidence does not
support.**

**This is yesterday's rule applied to a drug rather than a device, and it is worth stating as the general
form.** **[Yesterday's haemoadsorption review](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-23/) measured interleukin-6
and tumour necrosis factor — the cytokines the cartridge exists to remove — and found no change in either, and
this archive recorded that a mechanistic null is a stronger result than an outcome null.** **Here the
mechanism is pharmacological rather than biochemical, and the finding is the same shape: the property the
molecule was designed to have is the property not demonstrated.**

**The only statistically clean finding is a dose-response within the drug, and that is not the question.**
**Fospropofol 6.5 mg/kg beats fospropofol 2 mg/kg for sedation success, RR 3.27 (2.62–4.08)** — a tight
estimate, standard error about 0.11, and entirely unsurprising. **It establishes that the drug works better at
a higher dose, which no one doubted.** **The clinically live question is whether it beats propofol, and on the
one outcome where that was poolable the answer is a null.** **The authors also note that higher doses "may
improve procedural-sedation efficacy but may also increase adverse effects in some settings", which is the
trade the dose-response conceals.**

**And the tolerability profile it does have is a swap, not an improvement.** **"Paresthesia/pruritus was
commonly reported."** Fospropofol's characteristic adverse effect is perineal paraesthesia and itching, a
known consequence of the phosphate prodrug's metabolism. **So the drug trades propofol's injection pain for a
different unpleasant sensation, and the review found the evidence on injection pain and patient satisfaction
"limited"** — meaning the comparison that would settle whether the swap is worth making has not been done
properly.

**Set beside the remimazolam thread, now at six documents, a pattern is forming across the newer sedatives.**
**On [20 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-20/) this archive reconciled two contradictory remimazolam
trials and found that its emergence advantage over propofol exists only when flumazenil is given.** **Today a
second novel sedative's claimed advantage over propofol turns out unestablished.** **Two drugs, two
propofol-beating claims, and under scrutiny one is conditional on an antagonist and the other is not
demonstrated.** **Propofol remains remarkably hard to improve on, and the honest reading of both reviews is
that the newer agents are alternatives rather than upgrades.**

**Credit where it is due: ten studies, two pooled, eight narrative under declared SWiM principles, a certainty
range stated as high to very low, and a conclusion that declines the claim.** **A review that pools 20% of its
evidence and says so is more trustworthy than one that pools all of it.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00228-026-04200-w) · PROSPERO CRD42023477740

## 4. Eleven candidate therapies, twenty-one studies, seventeen of them animal, and not one patient outcome

**Journal · Published:** *Molecular Biology Reports*, **23 October 2026** — **one day old**, 0 citations.
**Systematic review** of interventions to preserve or restore endothelial glycocalyx integrity in sepsis,
searched through August 2026, risk of bias by RoB 2, SYRCLE and ROBINS-I. **Twenty-one studies: seventeen
animal, four human — 81% animal.** **Eleven named interventions.**

> **Interventions included ascorbic acid, sulodexide, doxycycline, activated protein C, heparin-based
> therapies, antithrombin, recombinant thrombomodulin, therapeutic plasma exchange, colivelin, liposomal
> nanocarriers of preassembled eGCX, and rhamnan sulfate.** … **Outcomes included circulating biomarkers such
> as syndecan-1, heparan sulfate, and hyaluronan, microcirculatory measures including perfused boundary
> region, and structural assessment by microscopy.** … **Several interventions reduced eGCX shedding, improved
> endothelial barrier or microvascular function, and, in some experimental models, preserved or restored eGCX
> ultrastructure. Overall, the eGCX represents a promising therapeutic target in sepsis; however, most evidence
> remains preclinical, and well-designed clinical studies using standardized eGCX outcomes are needed.**

**Interpretation.** **This review and yesterday's haemoadsorption meta-analysis measure the same class of
thing and carry opposite evidential weight, and the asymmetry is the most useful idea in today's reading.**
**Yesterday: a cartridge designed to remove cytokines did not change interleukin-6 or tumour necrosis factor,
and that null is close to decisive, because a therapy that does not do its own mechanism cannot work through
it.** **Today: eleven interventions did reduce glycocalyx shedding markers, and that positive establishes very
little, because doing the mechanism is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.** **A mechanistic biomarker is a
strong instrument for killing a therapy and a weak one for establishing it.** **Both reviews are honest; only
one of them could have produced a conclusion.**

**Every outcome listed is the instrument.** **Syndecan-1, heparan sulfate and hyaluronan are shedding markers.
Perfused boundary region is a microcirculatory index. Structural assessment by microscopy is a picture of the
glycocalyx.** **There is no mortality, no organ failure, no vasopressor duration, no length of stay anywhere
in the outcome list.** **Against the three conditions this archive applies to a monitored variable, this is
the most complete condition-three failure yet recorded: not an outcome measured partly by the instrument, but
a review in which no non-instrument outcome exists at all.**

**And "promising" is in the title of a review that is 81% animal studies.** **Four human studies across eleven
candidate interventions is 0.36 human studies per intervention; twenty-one studies across eleven interventions
is 1.91 studies each.** **This is a research agenda — a list of things worth trying — presented with a word
that readers will take as a claim about patients.** **The abstract's own last clause says so plainly: "most
evidence remains preclinical, and well-designed clinical studies using standardized eGCX outcomes are
needed."** **The title and the final clause disagree, and the final clause is right.**

**The underlying biology is not in doubt and that is why the framing matters.** **This archive reported on
[21](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-21/) and [23 September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-23/) that
syndecan-1 tracks with albumin and antithrombin in shock, and that glycocalyx disruption sits alongside
natural anticoagulant depletion and microvascular thrombosis in the pathophysiology of life-threatening
shock.** **Those documents established the marker. This one intervenes on it. The step after — whether moving
the marker moves a patient — has not been taken for any of the eleven agents.** **That is the interesting
state of affairs, and calling the target promising is a reasonable thing for a researcher to say and a
misleading thing for a clinician to read.**

**One practical note for anyone scanning the intervention list: several of these are already licensed drugs
being repurposed** — ascorbic acid, doxycycline, heparin, antithrombin, recombinant thrombomodulin, therapeutic
plasma exchange. **Ascorbic acid in sepsis has been through large randomised trials with discouraging results
on patient outcomes, and its appearance here on a glycocalyx-marker basis is a reminder that a mechanism can
survive the failure of the therapy that was supposed to exploit it.**
