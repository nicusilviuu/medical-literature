**Yesterday's brief established the mechanism of this archive's index measure and set a test: today's reading
would show whether 22 October's MEDLINE batch had merely been late.** **It had not. It never came.**

**Twenty-two October received zero MEDLINE and zero PMC records, permanently** — the same as 20 October.
**Two weekdays in one month on which no journal record entered the database at all, and both were read
correctly at the time by the source test rather than guessed at.** **Today the batch landed: 6,087 MEDLINE
records by 14:06, so the test says new material exists, and the sweep found it.** Both decompositions sum
exactly to their totals.

**The whole database rose 7,052 to 49,118,306, residual zero** — `MED` **+6,017**, `PPR` +1,096,
`PMC` **−61**, every other source unchanged, and the three changes sum to 7,052 exactly. **The gross-versus-net
gap recorded yesterday shows up again in both directions: today's MEDLINE bucket holds 6,087 records against a
net MEDLINE gain of 6,017, so about 70 MEDLINE records were removed elsewhere; and PMC contributed 317 records
to today's bucket while falling 61 overall, so roughly 378 PMC records went.**

**And a correction, which is the fifth consecutive day one has been needed.** **Yesterday I wrote: "every
backward-looking number in this instrument is an upper bound."** **That is true of `FIRST_IDATE` buckets, which
can only shrink, and it is false of `FIRST_PDATE` windows, which is what I generalised it to.** The 1–30
September reference window fell 103 on Wednesday and 82 the day before; **today it rose 399, to 163,339.** The
1–7 October window rose 1,235 to 44,914. **The reason is obvious once stated: a record indexed today can carry
a publication date from September, so a publication-date window grows by ingest and shrinks by deletion and is
monotonic in neither direction.** **An index-date bucket cannot grow because the index date is assigned at
entry. The two measures behave differently and I collapsed them.**

**The pattern across the five corrections is now consistent enough to name: each one has been an
over-generalisation from a single observation, not a measurement error.** The measurements have held up every
time; the sentences built on them have not.

## 1. Haemoadsorption does not lower the cytokines it exists to remove, and the review says its evidence base is less than half-built

**Journal · Published:** *Anaesthesia, Critical Care & Pain Medicine* — the SFAR journal — **21 October 2026**,
**two days old**, 0 citations. **Systematic review, meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis**
(PROSPERO CRD42025634991, registered 31 January 2025). **Six randomised trials, 798 patients**; intraoperative
haemoadsorption during cardiopulmonary bypass versus standard care. **Primary outcome: cardiac
surgery-associated acute kidney injury within 7 days.**

> **A total of six RCTs comprising 798 patients were included. Four RCTs (n = 723; 355 hemoadsorption, 368
> control) reported CSA-AKI incidence, which did not significantly differ between groups (RR = 0.93 [95% CI:
> 0.71-1.21], p = 0.57; I² = 42.0%). Hemoadsorption was not associated with significant differences in
> secondary outcomes: interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor, 28-day mortality, SOFA score, and ICU length of
> stay.** … **However, the accrued sample represented only 48% of the heterogeneity-adjusted required
> information size (1,491 patients), and the cumulative Z-curve crossed neither the monitoring nor the futility
> boundary.**

**Interpretation.** **The negative that matters here is not the kidney outcome — it is interleukin-6 and
tumour necrosis factor.** **Haemoadsorption is a cartridge that removes circulating cytokines; that is the
entire mechanism and the entire rationale.** **This review measured both principal cytokines and found no
significant change in either.** **An outcome null tells you the therapy did not help. A biomarker null tells
you the therapy did not do the thing it is sold as doing**, and the second is the more fundamental result.

**This is the fifth document in an arc this archive has been assembling since August, and it is the first to
test the mechanism rather than the outcome.** The [Pittaway BJA
meta-analysis](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-29/) found no benefit from haemoadsorption during
cardiopulmonary bypass across **12 RCTs and 713 patients**, only one at low risk of bias.
[CLEANSE](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-20/) reported HR 0.68 (0.44–1.07) in septic shock. And on
[3 September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-03/) a position statement from the field's own advocates —
Ronco, De Backer, Teboul, Taccone — **conceded that fifteen years of adoption rests on pathophysiological
reasoning and expert opinion, while naming the precise mechanism of action as uncertain.** **Today the
pathophysiological reasoning was checked against the biomarkers and did not appear in them.**

**A discrepancy between the two meta-analyses worth stating rather than smoothing over: 12 trials and 713
patients in August, 6 trials and 798 patients today.** **Fewer trials, more patients — mean trial size 59
then and 133 now.** That is an inclusion-criteria difference, not an error: today's review restricts to
elective cardiac surgery with a prespecified AKI primary outcome, and the August review was broader. **But a
reader comparing the two needs to know that they are not nested, and neither abstract says so.**

**The trial sequential analysis is the model of how this should be reported, and it is worth setting directly
against yesterday's.** **[Yesterday's vasopressor meta-analysis](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-22/) put
"Improve Septic Shock Outcome" in its title while its own TSA reported a lack of firm evidence.** **This one
states a null and then states that the null is not yet reliable either: 48% of the required information size,
and the Z-curve crossed neither the monitoring boundary nor the futility boundary.** **That is the complete and
honest position — no benefit demonstrated, and futility not demonstrated either — and the accrued figure is
internally consistent, because 48% of 1,491 is 716 against the 723 patients who contributed the primary
outcome.** **Third trial sequential analysis in the last four briefs, and the first to be used to qualify the
authors' own conclusion in both directions.**

**Two limits on how far the negative travels.** **I² of 42% is moderate heterogeneity across only four
contributing trials**, and the confidence interval on a relative risk of 0.93 runs to 1.21, implying a standard
error near 0.14 — **so a 25% relative reduction in AKI is not excluded.** **And 75 of the 798 patients were in
trials that did not report the primary outcome at all**, which is a reporting gap rather than attrition but
narrows the base further. **The right conclusion is the authors': not shown, not refuted, and half the
evidence still missing** — with the biomarker result the one finding that does not need more patients to be
interesting.

## 2. Less atelectasis at high inspired oxygen, which is the wrong direction, on three patients per arm

**Journal · Published:** *Anaesthesia Reports*, **1 July 2026** — **sixteen weeks old**, newly readable and
stated plainly as not new. 0 citations. **Randomised controlled feasibility trial. Twenty-eight adults**
having major abdominal surgery for cancer resection, allocated to **conservative FIO₂ 0.3 (n = 8), typical 0.55
(n = 10) or recommended 0.8 (n = 10)** throughout anaesthesia. Outcomes to **7 days**.

> The World Health Organisation recommends that all anaesthetised patients receive FIO2 0.8 during surgery to
> reduce the risk of surgical site infections **but did not consider the effect this might have on other
> clinical outcomes. A Cochrane systematic review concluded that insufficient evidence exists** … **and that
> doing so might increase mortality.** …
>
> **Higher FIO2 levels were associated with lower rates of atelectasis — 6 (75%) versus 8 (80%) versus 3 (30%)
> for FIO2 = 0.30 versus 0.55 versus 0.80, respectively; p = 0.045.** Postoperative cognitive recovery scores
> (**p = 0.277**), Postoperative Morbidity Survey infection scores (**5 (71%) versus 5 (56%) versus 3 (33%),
> p = 0.307**) and intensive care unit length of stay (**p = 0.870**) did not differ between groups.
> **Changing the administered intra-operative oxygen concentration may alter postoperative clinical outcomes,
> and adequately powered clinical studies are urgently needed.**

**Interpretation.** **The headline finding points the opposite way to the physiology, and that is the first
thing to say.** **High inspired oxygen causes absorption atelectasis — nitrogen is washed out, oxygen is taken
up faster than it is delivered, and poorly ventilated alveoli collapse.** **This trial found the least
atelectasis in the FIO₂ 0.8 arm: 3 of 10, against 6 of 8 and 8 of 10 at lower concentrations.** **Either a
real effect is operating that outweighs absorption atelectasis — higher oxygen reserve meaning fewer
desaturation events and less recruitment loss during airway manipulation is the obvious candidate — or this is
small-number noise. The counts are 3, 6 and 8 patients.**

**A p of 0.045 from 28 patients across three arms, as one of four reported comparisons, should not be read as
a finding, and the paper does not ask you to.** **It is titled a feasibility trial, its stated purpose was to
establish whether intra-operative FIO₂ can be controlled and systemic redox state measured, and its conclusion
asks for adequately powered studies.** **Taken at that level it is useful: it says the question is answerable
and the direction may surprise.**

**A denominator inconsistency the abstract does not explain.** **The atelectasis arms are 8, 10 and 10 —
twenty-eight patients. The infection percentages imply denominators of 7, 9 and 9 — twenty-five.** **Three
patients are missing from the morbidity outcome and no reason is given.** At this scale three patients is more
than a tenth of the trial.

**Where it sits in this archive's oxygen arc, with the distinctions kept straight.** On
[17 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-17/) a meta-analysis of closed-loop oxygen control showed 21
percentage points more time inside a target saturation range and measured no clinical outcome at all. On
[20 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-20/) eleven trials and 4,136 patients found that **which
oxygenation target you choose after cardiac arrest changes neither mortality nor neurological outcome.**
**Today's trial is a different question again and must not be merged with either: intraoperative inspired
oxygen concentration in elective abdominal surgery, not ICU oxygenation targets after cardiac arrest.** **What
the three share is that the only outcome anyone has reliably moved is a process or radiological measure, and
every clinical endpoint has come back flat.**

**One genuine link to yesterday's reading, though.** **The [EIT-guided PEEP
trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-21/) reported a 72.4% control-arm rate for a pulmonary-complication
composite that includes atelectasis, and this archive flagged that the composite was probably dominated by its
radiological component.** **PULSE Ox reports atelectasis alone, at 30–80% depending on oxygen concentration,
in ordinary abdominal surgery.** **That is the independent confirmation that radiological atelectasis after
major surgery is close to a majority finding, and therefore that a composite containing it will be driven by
it.**

**A record note, and it is the third instance of the same defect in four days.** **This paper has two Europe
PMC records: one open-access with no abstract, no DOI and a publication date of 1 January 2026, and one with
the full abstract, the real DOI and the real date.** **In the sweeps of 21 and 22 October only the
abstract-free record surfaced, so the item was logged as unreadable for two days and became readable today
without anyone doing anything.** **The sibling-record rule has now paid three times, and the lesson is
sharpening: a defective record is often a temporary state of the index rather than a permanent property of the
deposit.**

## 3. Satisfaction up thirteen-fold, measured the next day in patients the drug had sedated, with recovery unchanged

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Anesthesia*, **22 October 2026** — **one day old**, 0 citations.
**Prospective, triple-blind, placebo-controlled randomised trial** (UMIN000025489). **Eighty-three women**
aged 20–45 having elective caesarean under combined spinal-epidural anaesthesia, randomised 1:1 to
**dexmedetomidine after cord clamping — 1 µg/kg over 10 minutes then 0.4 µg/kg/hr to peritoneal closure** — or
equivalent-volume saline. **81 contributed to the available-case analysis.** **Primary outcome: satisfaction
with intraoperative sedation and analgesia, on a 7-point verbal rating scale, assessed on postoperative
day 1.**

> **Satisfaction scores were higher with dexmedetomidine than saline (Hodges-Lehmann location shift, 1.0
> point; 95% CI 1.0 to 2.0; P < 0.001). In ordinal logistic regression, dexmedetomidine was associated with
> higher satisfaction scores (common odds ratio, 13.4; 95% CI 4.6 to 39.0). Dexmedetomidine reduced
> intraoperative pain and intraoperative nausea/vomiting, but did not improve 24-h Quality of Recovery-40
> scores and modestly prolonged motor recovery.** … **These findings support further evaluation of
> dexmedetomidine as a selective adjunct rather than for routine sedation.**

**Interpretation.** **The problem is the primary outcome, and it is structural rather than a flaw in
execution.** **The intervention is a sedative. The outcome is the patient's recollection, a day later, of how
the operation felt.** **A sedated patient's memory of an operation is not the same object as an unsedated
patient's memory of the same operation**, so a higher satisfaction score is consistent with the experience
having been better and equally consistent with its having been less completely encoded. **The trial cannot
separate those, and neither can any trial that measures recalled experience of a drug that alters recall.**

**The triple-blinding does not rescue it, for a reason worth being explicit about.** **Dexmedetomidine produces
unmistakable sedation. A patient cannot be blinded to the fact that she became drowsy during her caesarean,
whatever the syringe was labelled.** **Blinding the allocation is not the same as blinding the effect, and a
common odds ratio of 13.4 — with an interval from 4.6 to 39.0 — is an implausibly large clinical effect and an
entirely ordinary size for an unblinding effect on a self-reported scale.**

**What settles the reading is the secondary outcome the authors report against themselves: the 24-hour
Quality of Recovery-40 score did not improve.** **So satisfaction with the operation rose thirteen-fold and
measured quality of recovery the following day was unchanged.** **The honest summary is that the
dexmedetomidine group remembered the operation more favourably and recovered no better** — which may still be
worth having, because a caesarean is an experience as well as a procedure, and it is a different claim from
the one a reader skimming the odds ratio would take away.

**Two technical notes, one small and one worth copying.** **The Hodges-Lehmann location shift is reported as
1.0 with a 95% interval of 1.0 to 2.0 — the point estimate sits exactly on its own lower bound**, which is a
discreteness artefact of that estimator on a 7-point scale rather than an error, and it is a reminder that an
interval on an ordinal scale does not behave like one on a continuous measure. **And the costs are reported:
motor recovery was modestly prolonged**, which for a mother who wants to hold and feed her baby is the
relevant trade and is named rather than buried.

**The conclusion deserves credit and this archive has been noting the opposite all week.** **"Support further
evaluation of dexmedetomidine as a selective adjunct rather than for routine sedation"** — a conclusion that
narrows its own claim, labels the pain and nausea findings exploratory, and declines to recommend routine use
off a single-centre trial of 83 women. **Set beside yesterday's "Improve Septic Shock Outcome", it is the
difference between writing for citation and writing for a reader.**

## 4. The observer-rated scales moved, the machines did not, and a p of 0.24 is reported as a finding

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care Medicine*, **21 October 2026** — **two days old**, 0 citations.
**Randomised controlled trial, two parallel groups, 100 adults** in a general ICU ventilated for at least
48 hours. Intervention: **daily individualised music therapy averaging 25 minutes** — music relaxation, music
listening and clinical improvisation — **added to standard care during weaning.** **Primary outcome: weaning
duration.**

> **No significant difference in weaning duration was observed between groups (p = 0.97).** Patients in the MG
> group showed **significant improvements in agitation and sedation levels (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale)
> on days 1 and 3 (p = 0.048, 0.005, and 0.024) and a lower percentage required propofol (p = 0.028).** No
> significant difference in perceived pain. **The MG obtained lower Perceived Stress Scale-14 scores
> (p = 0.24).** Additionally, **MG significantly reduced tracheostomy rates (14%; p = 0.025).** Median total
> IMV and ICU stay duration were shorter in the MG group, but not significantly (p = 0.206 and 0.203).
> **Heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and oxygenation showed no significant difference before and
> after music therapy.**

**Interpretation.** **The primary outcome is as flat as a primary outcome gets: p = 0.97 on weaning duration.**
**Everything else in this abstract is secondary, and there are at least eight secondary comparisons quoted, of
which five fall below 0.05.** With a null primary and that many secondaries, **the question is not whether
something crossed a threshold but whether the pattern of what crossed it means anything.** **It does, and the
pattern points the wrong way.**

**Sort the secondary outcomes by who measured them.** **The ones that moved are the Richmond
Agitation-Sedation Scale (an observer rating), propofol use (a clinician's prescribing decision) and
tracheostomy rate (a clinician's decision).** **The ones that did not move are perceived pain, perceived
stress at p = 0.24, ventilation and ICU duration, and — decisively — heart rate, respiratory rate, blood
pressure and oxygenation, which showed no change before and after the sessions.** **Music therapy cannot be
blinded from the treating team: a therapist visits the bedside for twenty-five minutes a day and everyone
sees it.** **So the outcomes that improved are exactly the ones an unblinded clinician scores or decides, and
the outcomes that did not are the ones a monitor records.** **That is the signature of unblinding, not of a
physiological effect, and the physiological null is the paper's own evidence for it.**

**The tracheostomy finding is the most clinically important claim and the most exposed to this.** **A 14%
reduction in tracheostomy at p = 0.025 would matter a great deal if real** — tracheostomy is a procedure, a
complication risk and a long-term airway. **But the decision to perform one is a judgement made by a team
that knows which patients have a music therapist attending daily, in a trial whose primary outcome — the
weaning duration that drives that decision — showed no difference whatsoever.** **A shorter weaning course
would explain fewer tracheostomies. There was no shorter weaning course.**

**And one straightforward reporting error, stated plainly because it is in the abstract and will be quoted.**
**"The MG obtained lower Perceived Stress Scale-14 scores (p = 0.24)" is written in the same declarative form
as the significant results beside it, and p = 0.24 supports no such statement.** **The conclusion then carries
it forward: "It may potentially decrease perceived stress."** **That is a non-finding promoted to a
possibility by grammar alone**, and it sits two sentences from a correctly hedged non-significant result for
ICU stay. **The same abstract both does and does not know how to report a p-value above 0.05.**

**None of this makes music therapy in the ICU a bad idea, and it is worth saying so.** **A twenty-five-minute
individualised session for a patient being weaned from a ventilator is cheap, carries no plausible harm, and
the humanisation of critical care the authors name as an aim is a legitimate goal that does not need a
p-value.** **What the trial does not support is the claim that it shortens weaning, reduces tracheostomy or
lowers stress.** **Its own primary outcome and its own monitors say otherwise.**
