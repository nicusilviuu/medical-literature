## The 16 October hypothesis fails its own test

**Yesterday's guidelines watch found four EACTS expert consensus documents this archive had missed and
reported that all five EACTS documents from that week — including the one the October audit did find —
share a first index date of 16 October 2026, the rollback day.** It called that date "the most-implicated
date in this archive's history", said plainly that it was not proof, and named the test: **re-run the
16 October sweep as recorded and see what returns.**

**The test ran today. It does not support the claim.**

| The 16 October bucket | |
| --- | --- |
| Records in the bucket | **35,391** |
| In-scope guidance records, swept and collected | **60 of 60, shortfall zero** |
| Already in this archive | **7** — including **all five EACTS documents** |
| Secondary (commentary, correction, letter) | 6 |
| Remainder | 47 |

**The 47 is where the hypothesis dies.** Read as a list it is wild boar injuries in wilderness
environments, periprosthetic joint infection prevention, a Bangladeshi haemodialysis diet audit, sublingual
immunotherapy, spinal ependymoma, return-to-sport after cervical spine injury, radiotherapy capacity in
low-income settings, electroacupuncture for back pain, and a cluster of guideline-methodology papers.
**They are in the list because the scope filter matches words like "analgesia" and "critical care" anywhere
in a record, not because this archive should have reported them.** **There is no concentration of missed
in-scope documents on 16 October beyond the EACTS set that prompted the suspicion.**

**So the better explanation is duller and it is a general fact about the index.** **A journal's issue batch
takes one index date: *European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery* and *Interdisciplinary CardioVascular
and Thoracic Surgery* issues dated 1 and 7 October were all first indexed on 16 October together.** **A
single index date can therefore carry one society's whole month of output, which makes five documents on one
date a publishing coincidence rather than a loss event.** **And missing four of them has an explanation
already established yesterday that needs no date: two of the four carry no guidance publication type at all,
and the scope filter this archive runs would not have caught them.**

**The hypothesis is withdrawn.** **What survives is the reason it was testable: a claim about a specific date
with a specific query attached to it.** **Three claims this week have been put to a stated test — the burst
period (falsified the next day), the PubMed Central bucket alignment (not reproduced), and now this
one — and all three failed.** **That is the instrument working. A claim that cannot name its own test should
not be published.**

## 1. A 0.6-point difference reaches significance and a 9.9-point difference does not

**[Balanced Crystalloids Versus Normal Saline for Fluid Resuscitation in Pediatric Septic
Shock](https://doi.org/10.1097/PEC.0000000000003746)** · *Pediatric Emergency Care* · **published
16 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Six randomised trials, 10,308 children aged 0 to 18 with sepsis or septic shock. Searches to May 2026,
RoB 2, random-effects, leave-one-out and a pre-planned sensitivity analysis excluding the largest trial.
Risk ratios are reported with saline as the reference.**

> Pooled mortality showed no statistical significance between groups (3.8% with balanced crystalloids vs.
> 4.0% with NS; RR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.67-1.27; P = 0.63). **Acute kidney injury incidence also did not differ
> significantly between groups (19.7% vs. 29.6%; RR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.46-1.27; P = 0.29). The need for renal
> replacement therapy (RRT) was lower with balanced crystalloids (1.3% vs. 1.9%; RR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.48-0.92;
> P = 0.01).** Hyperchloremia incidence was lower with balanced crystalloids (32.3% vs. 49.9%; RR, 0.65; 95%
> CI, 0.61-0.69; P < 0.00001). After excluding the largest trial (PRoMPT BOLUS), mortality remained
> nonsignificant (RR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.45-1.50), and the reductions in RRT (RR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.39-0.87) and
> hyperchloremia (RR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.59-0.82) persisted.

**Put the two kidney results side by side, because the comparison is the most instructive thing on this
page.** **Acute kidney injury differs by 9.9 percentage points and does not reach significance. Dialysis
differs by 0.6 percentage points and does.** **Dialysis is sixteen times rarer, so it should have had less
power, not more.**

**The explanation is in the interval widths and it is a measurement problem, not a statistical one.** **The
kidney injury interval spans a log width of 1.016; the dialysis interval spans 0.651 — so the estimate for
the outcome that is 15.4 times more common is 1.56 times LESS precise on the relative scale.** **That can
only mean the trials disagree about kidney injury far more than they disagree about dialysis** — and the
obvious reason is that acute kidney injury is a definition, applied differently across six trials, while
renal replacement therapy is an event that either happened or did not. **This week's dominant theme, arriving
at an outcome hierarchy: the harder, unambiguous endpoint gave the cleaner answer, and the softer, commoner
one gave the wider interval.**

**And it is the exact converse of Saturday's dobutamine trial**, where a creatinine-defined kidney injury
endpoint produced a strong positive result in 584 open-label patients, with no dialysis or mortality
endpoint reported at all. **Read together: when both are measured, the soft endpoint is the noisy one.**

**One result should not be read as clinical at all.** **Hyperchloraemia, 32.3% against 49.9%, risk ratio 0.65
with an interval from 0.61 to 0.69 and a z of −13.7.** An interval that tight on 10,308 patients is what you
get when the outcome is a biochemical consequence of the assigned intervention. **It confirms that the
children received the fluid they were randomised to. It is an adherence check wearing the clothes of an
outcome.**

**My reading.** **Use a balanced crystalloid: it halves hyperchloraemia, it reduces dialysis by a third, and
it does not change mortality in 10,308 children.** **The absolute dialysis difference is 0.6 points, so the
number needed to treat is about 170** — a real benefit on a hard outcome, honestly small. **The kidney
injury result should not be quoted in either direction until the trials agree on what kidney injury is.**

## 2. Nine milligrams of morphine, two pain differences nobody would notice, and an interval that cannot be right

**[Quadratus lumborum versus transversus abdominis plane block for postoperative analgesia in laparoscopic
colorectal surgery](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10151-026-03405-6)** · *Techniques in Coloproctology* ·
**published 15 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Four randomised trials, 419 patients. Searches to 27 July 2025, RoB 2, random-effects, GRADE.** The
rationale is anatomical and sound: a quadratus lumborum block can spread to the thoracolumbar fascia and
paravertebral space, so it may cover visceral pain that a transversus abdominis plane block does not.

> Compared with the TAP block, the QL block led to lower opioid use within 24 h (MD -8.92 mg morphine
> equivalents; 95% CI -11.57 to -6.28) and reduced pain scores both at rest MD **-0.25 (-0.37 to -0.21)** and
> during movement MD -0.40 [-0.52 to -0.29]. … The rates of PONV (OR 0.54; 95% CI 0.25-1.19) and dizziness
> (OR 0.60; 95% CI 0.22-1.63) were not significantly different … **The evidence was rated high for PONV and
> operative time, and moderate for opioid consumption, pain at rest, pain during movement, and dizziness,
> reflecting downgrades for imprecision and … substantial-to-high heterogeneity (I2 85% and 93%,
> respectively).**

**The opioid result is the real one and it is worth having: 8.92 mg of morphine equivalents saved in the
first 24 hours, with a symmetric interval from 11.57 to 6.28.** In an enhanced-recovery pathway that is a
meaningful difference.

**The two pain results are statistically significant and clinically invisible.** **A reduction of 0.25 points
at rest and 0.40 points on movement, on a pain scale whose minimal clinically important difference is
conventionally around one to one and a half points.** **This archive reported exactly this structure three
days ago in a pregabalin review — significance below the threshold the authors themselves cite — and here it
appears again in a different analgesic literature in the same week.** **RULE, now twice-demonstrated: in a
regional anaesthesia meta-analysis, read the pain difference against the minimal clinically important
difference before reading its p-value.**

**One confidence interval cannot be right, and it should be flagged rather than resolved.** **A mean
difference is symmetric by construction. "MD −0.25 (−0.37 to −0.21)" has arms of 0.120 below and 0.040
above — a three-to-one asymmetry.** The pain-on-movement interval, by contrast, is 0.120 and 0.110, which is
symmetric to rounding. **So one of the three numbers in the pain-at-rest result is wrong, and the abstract
gives no way to tell which.** **Reported as an internal inconsistency; not adjudicated.**

**And the GRADE pattern is the opposite of reassuring.** **Certainty was rated HIGH for the two outcomes
with no difference — postoperative nausea and vomiting, and operative time — and only MODERATE for every
outcome that moved.** **Heterogeneity of 85% and 93% across four trials is the stated reason**, and this
archive's rule applies with force: **an I² of 85% on four trials means the trials are measuring different
things, and the pooled estimate is an average of disagreement.**

**My reading.** **A quadratus lumborum block probably spares opioid and probably does not make pain
meaningfully different, on four small trials that disagree with each other.** The authors' conclusion is
appropriately hedged — larger multicentre trials are needed "before routine preference over the TAP block
can be firmly recommended" — and the honest summary is that the anatomical rationale is better than the
evidence.

## 3. Colchicine does not prevent perioperative kidney injury, in 2,947 masked patients

**[Colchicine and Perioperative Acute Kidney Injury: A Substudy of the COP-AF Randomized Clinical
Trial](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.ajkd.2026.06.019)** · *American Journal of Kidney Diseases* ·
**published 30 October 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Prespecified substudy of a placebo-controlled randomised trial. Patients aged 55 or over having major
non-cardiac thoracic surgery, 45 centres in 11 countries, 2018 to 2023. 0.5 mg oral colchicine twice daily
for ten days against placebo. Patients, providers, data collectors and outcome adjudicators all masked.**
2,947 of 3,209 enrolled patients included — 262 excluded, 8.2% — in arms of 1,476 and 1,471, which sum
exactly.

> Perioperative AKI occurred in 67 participants (4.7%) in the colchicine group and in 63 participants (4.4%)
> in the placebo group (risk ratio [RR], 1.07 [95% CI, 0.76-1.50], P = 0.72; risk difference [RD], 0.3% [95%
> CI, -1.2% to 1.8%]). **The RD of AKI in patients with CKD treated with colchicine versus placebo was
> -0.9%, and it was 0.5% in those without CKD (interaction P = 0.62).** Limitations Limited statistical power
> to detect small clinically important effects on AKI treated with dialysis.

**This is a clean, large, masked null and it is the counterpart this archive wanted for Saturday's dobutamine
trial.** **Both use a creatinine-defined perioperative kidney injury endpoint. The dobutamine trial was
open-label, 584 patients, and reported a risk ratio of 0.64. This one is masked, 2,947 patients, and reports
1.07 with an interval from 0.76 to 1.50.** **The larger and better-blinded trial found nothing on the same
endpoint class** — which does not refute the dobutamine result, and does make the case for reading
creatinine endpoints with the archive's standing scepticism rather than against it.

**Two things the paper does right and should be credited for.** **The interaction by pre-existing chronic
kidney disease is pre-specified, reported, and null (P = 0.62)** — the instrument this archive has spent the
week asking for, used correctly on a question where a subgroup story would have been easy to tell. **And the
limitation is stated in the right currency: limited power for kidney injury treated with dialysis**, which
is the hard endpoint, and which the first item on today's page shows is where the signal usually lives.

**One arithmetic note.** **67 of 1,476 is 4.54%, not the stated 4.7%; 63 of 1,471 is 4.28%, not 4.4%.** The
stated percentages imply denominators near 1,425 and 1,431 — about fifty and forty patients fewer than the
randomised arms. **So the outcome denominators are not the arm sizes, and the abstract does not say what
they are.** **That is the third instance of the same reporting habit in this archive in one month** — after
COVALENT on 16 October and SAVE-ICU on Saturday. **None of the three changes its result; all three mean the
same thing: recompute the percentage from the arm size, and when it disagrees, the denominator is doing
something unstated.**

**My reading.** **Colchicine, given for ten days around major thoracic surgery to prevent atrial
fibrillation, does not protect the kidney.** The question was worth asking — perioperative inflammation is a
plausible mechanism and the drug was already being given — and the answer is firmly negative in the only
trial likely to be done.

## 4. Emergence delirium affects a third of older adults, and pain is the one reproducible lever

**[Emergence Delirium and Agitation in Older Adults After General
Anesthesia](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jopan.2026.10.003)** · *Journal of PeriAnesthesia Nursing* ·
**published 14 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Twelve reports representing eight independent cohorts; the main incidence analysis uses seven cohorts,
3,601 patients and 1,161 events. Searches to 30 April 2026, logit-transformed random-effects pooling, with
heterogeneous or single-study factors handled by evidence mapping rather than pooling.**

> …yielding a pooled incidence of 29.7% (95% CI: 23.4%-36.8%). Increasing age (OR = 1.07 per year, 95% CI:
> 1.04-1.10) and higher early recovery pain score were the most reproducible risk signals. **In the NRS-only
> sensitivity analysis, each one-point increase in pain score was associated with a 29% higher risk of ED/EA
> (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.21-1.37), with no observed heterogeneity.** Nutritional risk, hypothermia,
> catheter-related discomfort, urinary catheterization, preoperative anxiety, and selected anesthetic or
> non-pharmacological interventions emerged as clinically relevant but insufficiently homogeneous candidate
> factors.

**One in three older adults, which is the number to carry.** The crude event rate is 1,161 of 3,601, or
32.2%; the pooled estimate of 29.7% is lower, which is what logit-transformed random-effects pooling does to
a set of cohorts with different rates, and the two figures together bracket the answer.

**The pain finding is the only actionable one and it is unusually clean.** **An odds ratio of 1.29 per point
of pain score, interval 1.21 to 1.37, with no observed heterogeneity across the contributing studies.**
**Zero heterogeneity is normally a warning in a small pool — this archive's standing rule — but here it is
on a dose-response relationship within a single instrument, the numerical rating scale, which is exactly the
situation where studies should agree.** **Age at 1.07 per year is real and not modifiable. Pain at early
recovery is modifiable, and the effect size means a patient arriving in recovery at 6 out of 10 rather than
3 carries roughly twice the odds.**

**What the paper does with the rest is the part to praise.** **Nutritional risk, hypothermia,
catheter-related discomfort, urinary catheterisation and preoperative anxiety were not pooled, because they
were too heterogeneous or rested on one study each — so they are presented as an evidence map of candidate
factors rather than as effect estimates.** **Declining to pool is a decision most reviews get wrong in the
other direction**, and the result is a paper that reports two numbers it can stand behind and a list it
cannot.

**Two limits.** **Eight independent cohorts is a small base for a condition this common**, and the
definitions remain fragmented — the authors say so in their first sentence, that the evidence "remains
fragmented across different definitions". **This is the fifth appearance of delirium as an endpoint in this
archive in a week, and the fourth document in that week to name its own definitional problem.**

## 5. Pregabalin does not reduce persistent pain after spine surgery

**[Perioperative Pregabalin for Persistent Postsurgical Pain After Lumbar Spine Surgery In
Adults](https://doi.org/10.1093/pm/pnag149)** · *Pain Medicine* · **published 13 November 2026** ·
paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Five randomised placebo-controlled trials, 335 participants, all of pregabalin despite an eligibility
criterion written for gabapentinoids. PROSPERO CRD420261401910, random-effects with restricted maximum
likelihood, leave-one-out robustness, RoB 2 and GRADE.**

> Pregabalin did not reduce pain intensity at 3 months (4 trials, 275 participants; standardized mean
> difference [SMD] 0.03, 95% confidence interval [CI] -0.41 to 0.47; I² = 69%; low certainty) or at the
> latest follow-up (SMD -0.13, 95% CI -0.59 to 0.32; I² = 73%). **Functional disability was lower with
> pregabalin (2 trials, 143 participants; SMD -0.51, 95% CI -0.86 to -0.16), but the differences were below
> minimal important change thresholds (very low certainty).** … In an exploratory analysis of four trials, a
> favorable functional outcome was more frequent with pregabalin (risk ratio 1.44, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.86; very
> low certainty). Adverse events were inconsistently reported.

**The primary outcome is a clean null on four trials and 275 patients, and the interval is wide enough to
include a moderate benefit in either direction.** Both pain intervals are exactly symmetric, so the
reconstruction is straightforward and nothing is hidden.

**The functional disability result is the one that will be quoted, and the authors disqualify it
themselves.** **A standardised mean difference of −0.51 with an interval excluding zero — and "the
differences were below minimal important change thresholds".** **A paper that reports a significant result
and says in the same sentence that it is too small to matter is doing the reader's work**, and it is the
second such finding on this page: **the quadratus lumborum review above reports pain differences of 0.25 and
0.40 points without making that comparison at all.** **Same week, same field, same arithmetic — one review
states the threshold and one does not.**

**Three cautions on the numbers.** **Functional disability rests on two trials and 143 participants.** **The
exploratory favourable-outcome risk ratio of 1.44 is graded very low certainty and comes from a dichotomised
version of the same underlying scale**, so it is not independent evidence. **And heterogeneity of 69% and
73% on four trials is high and imprecisely estimated at that number of studies.**

**My reading.** **Do not give perioperative pregabalin to prevent persistent pain after lumbar spine
surgery: the primary outcome is null at low certainty, and the only positive result is below the threshold
its own authors cite.** The authors' request is the right one — adequately powered trials in patients at
high risk of persistent pain, with standardised outcomes and systematic reporting of harms, the last of
which five trials managed inconsistently.
