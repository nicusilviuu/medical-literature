Items 1 and 2 are a pair, and the pair is the point: **a narrative review published three days ago, and
the trial that made the review possible to write.** The review is current; the trial is seventeen months
old and has been cited 38 times. Neither is interpretable alone.

## 1. Volatile anaesthetic sedation in ARDS — the question, and where it now stands

**Journal · Published:** *Intensive Care Medicine*, **28 September 2026** (**new this week**).
**Narrative review**, not original research — indexed as `Review`.
O'Gara, Boncyk, Müller Wirtz, Molano Franco, Kotani, Landoni, Jerath, L'Her, Bellgardt, Becher,
Beck-Schimmer, Beitler, Constantin, Hughes et al.

> Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) arises from a heterogenous set of inciting injuries… Over
> decades, therapeutic efforts have focused on targeting the inflammatory response and providing
> excellent supportive care. **Volatile anesthetics have been proposed as agents that could achieve both
> of these aims** by protecting against inflammatory lung injury, allowing effective sedation, and
> through respiratory effects including bronchodilation and maintenance of respiratory drive. **Promising
> preliminary data demonstrated reductions in inflammatory mediators and improvements in oxygenation**
> with volatile anesthetics for patients with ARDS. **However, a recent multicenter randomized controlled
> trial found that sevoflurane sedation for patients with moderate-to-severe ARDS reduced ventilator free
> days at day 28 and increased mortality compared to propofol sedation.** Ongoing and recently completed
> clinical trials may provide additional context for patients with respiratory failure including ARDS,
> although **none are being conducted solely in ARDS patients.** Priorities for future trials… include
> **reconsideration of the dose, agent selection, and duration of volatile sedation, and potential
> heterogeneity of treatment effect across ARDS sub-phenotypes.** **Pending further high-quality data
> suggesting benefit, routine clinical use of volatile ICU sedation in ARDS is currently not recommended
> outside of clinical trials.**

**Interpretation.** This is a review rather than new evidence, and it is included because of what it
does: it states a negative conclusion plainly, in a field where the authors' own earlier work pointed the
other way. The author list includes investigators who ran the positive preliminary studies.

The mechanistic case was genuinely attractive, and worth restating because it explains why the field
spent a decade on this. Volatile agents promised to be **sedative and anti-inflammatory at once** — one
intervention addressing both halves of ARDS management — with bronchodilation and preserved respiratory
drive as bonuses. The early data delivered exactly what that story predicted: **less inflammation, better
oxygenation.** Then the trial measured whether patients lived, and the answer went the other way.

**Note what the review does not do.** It does not retreat to "more research is needed" as a way of
keeping the option open; it says routine use **is not recommended outside trials.** But it also does not
declare the mechanism dead, and the four specified priorities — dose, agent, duration, sub-phenotype —
are the honest list of what the trial did not test rather than an excuse for the result. **The one I
would weight most is duration:** the trial sedated for up to seven days, which is a very different
exposure from a few hours in theatre.

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08613-0)

## 2. SESAR — the trial the review turns on

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 May 2025** — **seventeen months old, and the most cited paper in
today's brief (38 citations).** Phase 3 randomised, open-label, assessor-blinded, 37 French ICUs. In
PubMed Central. Included because item 1 rests on it and a secondhand summary of a mortality result is
not good enough.
Jabaudon, Quenot, Badie, Audard, Jaber, Rieu, Varillon, Monsel, Thouy, Lorber, Cousson et al.

> **Objective** To determine the efficacy and safety of inhaled sevoflurane compared with intravenous
> propofol for sedation in patients with ARDS.
>
> **Design** … conducted from May 2020 to October 2023 with 90-day follow-up. Adults with **early
> moderate to severe ARDS** (Pao₂:Fio₂ <150 mm Hg with PEEP ≥8 cm H₂O) were enrolled in **37 French
> intensive care units.**
>
> **Interventions** … inhaled sedation with sevoflurane or … intravenous sedation with propofol **for up
> to 7 days.**
>
> **Results** Of **687 patients** enrolled (mean age 65 [SD 12]; 30% female), 346 were randomized to
> sevoflurane and 341 to propofol. The median total duration of sedation was 7 days (IQR, 4 to 7) in both
> groups. The number of **ventilator-free days through day 28** was 0.0 days (IQR, 0.0 to 11.9) in the
> sevoflurane group and 0.0 days (IQR, 0.0 to 18.7) in the propofol group (**median difference, −2.1
> [95% CI, −3.6 to −0.7]**; standardized hazard ratio, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.50 to 0.97]). The **90-day
> survival rates were 47.1% and 55.7%** in the sevoflurane and propofol groups, respectively (**hazard
> ratio, 1.31 [95% CI, 1.05 to 1.62]**). Among 4 secondary outcomes, sevoflurane was associated with
> **higher 7-day mortality (19.4% vs 13.5%**; relative risk, 1.44 [95% CI, 1.02 to 2.03]) and **fewer
> intensive care unit-free days.**
>
> **Conclusions** … inhaled sedation with sevoflurane resulted in **fewer ventilator-free days at day 28
> and lower 90-day survival** than sedation with propofol.

Trial registration NCT04235608.

**Interpretation.** **An 8.6 percentage point absolute difference in 90-day survival, against the
intervention, with a confidence interval excluding no effect.** This is the rare trial that does not need
careful interpretation to be actionable.

Three features make it more than a null-plus-noise result. **The harm appears early** — 7-day mortality
19.4% versus 13.5% — so this is not a late divergence that could be attributed to post-ICU care.
**Three endpoints move together**: ventilator-free days, ICU-free days and survival all favour propofol,
which is harder to explain as chance than a single endpoint would be. And **the ventilator-free-days
medians are both 0.0**, which is worth pausing on: in a population this sick, more than half of patients
in both arms were dead or still ventilated at day 28, and the whole signal lives in the upper half of the
distributions (IQR to 11.9 versus to 18.7). The median difference of −2.1 is a shift in that upper tail,
not a typical patient's experience.

**Where the earlier evidence went wrong is instructive, and I can show it exactly**, because the pilot is
retrievable: the 2017 single-centre RCT in *AJRCCM* (10.1164/rccm.201604-0686oc, now cited 137 times)
randomised **50** patients to sevoflurane or midazolam and found **PaO₂/FiO₂ 205 ± 56 versus 166 ± 59 on
day 2 (P=0.04)**, with reduced cytokines and soluble RAGE and no serious adverse events. Every one of
those endpoints is a surrogate — oxygenation, inflammatory mediators, a marker of epithelial injury — and
every one moved in the right direction. **Then 687 patients showed the opposite on survival.** The
comparator also changed between the two trials, from midazolam to propofol, which matters: beating
midazolam on oxygenation is a lower bar than beating propofol on mortality.

**This is a textbook case for the archive's standing caution about surrogate endpoints**, and a sharper
one than most, because the surrogates were not weakly positive — they were convincingly positive, across
several mechanistically independent measures, in the direction preclinical work predicted.

**One more reason this lands today.** Yesterday's brief reported that
[**anaesthetic greenhouse gas emissions come overwhelmingly from the volatile agent itself**, with
sevoflurane two orders of magnitude above carrier gas and absorbent](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/).
Volatile ICU sedation is volatile agent use for **days rather than hours** — the largest per-patient
exposure anaesthesia could produce. So the environmental and the clinical arguments about volatile
sedation in ARDS now point in the same direction, which is unusual enough to name. **It should not be
mistaken for support, though: the clinical result stands entirely on its own, and would be just as
decisive if sevoflurane had no warming potential at all.** Climate arguments are a poor way to settle
clinical questions, and this one has been settled on survival.

Worth contrasting with theatre practice. [The JAMA pragmatic trial reported here on 24
September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-24/) randomised **2,508 patients** to TIVA or volatile
anaesthesia for surgery and found **no difference** in days alive and at home. **Volatile versus
intravenous appears to be a null question for hours in an operating theatre and a harm question for days
in an intensive care unit.** Same drug class, same comparison, opposite conclusions — which is the
clearest argument available that dose and duration, not agent class, are what matter.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2025.3169)

## 3. Paravertebral versus erector spinae plane block for minimally invasive thoracic surgery

**Journal · Published:** *Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine*, **30 September 2026** (**new this week**).
Retrospective single-centre hospital registry analysis, Beth Israel Deaconess, 2020–2024.
Polshin, Braeuer, Tenge, Hentges, Chen, Paschold, Marcus, Zucco, Rakalin, Sahli, Gangadharan, Wilson,
Wachtendorf, Schaefer

> Minimally invasive thoracic surgery (MITS) has become the primary approach for the vast majority of
> thoracic surgeries… Prophylactic thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) is a common approach to facilitate
> analgesia after MITS. **Recently, erector spinae plane block (ESPB) has been widely employed as a
> technically easier alternative. It is still unclear which technique is associated with lower
> postoperative opioid administration and faster recovery.**… The primary outcome was opioid consumption
> in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU), assessed by cumulative oral morphine equivalents (OME)…
> Multivariable negative binomial regression… adjusting for patient and procedural characteristics.
>
> A total of **404 patients** were included (**283 ESPB and 121 TPVB**), receiving a median (IQR) of 25.5
> mg OME (15.0–42.5) in the PACU. After adjustment, patients receiving TPVB required **9.4 mg less OME
> (95% CI −15.7 to −3.0, p<0.01)**… predicted time to first opioid administration **11.4 min shorter**
> for TPVB (95% CI 1.3 to 21.5, p=0.03)… **118 min shorter LOS in the PACU** (95% CI −216 to −20,
> p=0.02) and **1.2 fewer days in the hospital** (95% CI −1.6 to −0.7, p<0.001)… **These findings provide
> a rationale for prospective trials** to investigate factors contributing to enhanced postoperative
> recovery with TPVB as compared with ESPB.

**Interpretation.** The direction matches what this archive has already reported, and the authors are
appropriately modest about what a registry can show — they ask for prospective trials rather than
claiming to have replaced them.

**The thread, with dates stated plainly:**

| Study | Design | Setting | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [BJA, 24 Nov 2025](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-27/) | Noninferiority RCT, n=74 | Median sternotomy | ESPB **noninferiority not demonstrated** |
| *JAMA Surgery*, 1 Dec 2023 | Noninferiority RCT, n=196 | MITS | Surgeon-placed PVB **noninferior** to anaesthetist's ultrasound-guided PVB |
| RAPM, today | Retrospective, n=404 | MITS | TPVB **better** on four outcomes |

Three papers, none of which establishes TPVB superiority on its own, all pointing the same way. The RCT
was underpowered for its own margin; this study is observational.

**The reason to be cautious here is a specific number, not a general worry about observational data.** A
single-shot regional block reduced PACU opioid consumption by **9.4 mg of oral morphine equivalent** —
real but modest — and is reported in the same analysis as **1.2 fewer days in hospital.** Those two
findings are hard to reconcile through any analgesic mechanism. Length of stay after thoracic surgery is
driven by chest drains, air leaks, complications and discharge logistics; **9 mg of morphine equivalent
in the recovery room does not plausibly move it by a day.** The far likelier explanation is confounding
the adjustment could not reach — and the arm sizes point at where. **283 ESPB versus 121 TPVB** over
2020–2024 suggests ESPB became the default during the study period, so block choice is entangled with
calendar time, with the individual proceduralist, and probably with case complexity. **Read the opioid
and time-to-first-opioid findings as the credible ones and treat the length-of-stay result as a flag for
residual confounding rather than a benefit to quote.**

**What the 2023 trial adds is the undercutting of ESPB's main justification.** ESPB spread because TPVB
is technically harder and operator-dependent — the premise stated in today's abstract. But that trial
(10.1001/jamasurg.2023.5228, retrieved in the same search as the paper above, 14 citations) randomised
196 patients and found a PVB **placed by the surgeon under thoracoscopic vision** was noninferior to one
placed by an anaesthetist with ultrasound, on 48-hour opioid consumption. **If a surgeon can place an
adequate paravertebral block through the camera, "too difficult" is a weaker argument for preferring the
easier block than it was.** That is my inference from putting the two together, not a claim either paper
makes.

Note also that this evidence continues to sit awkwardly against the **2026 ASA regional analgesia
guideline**, which [gave fascial plane blocks a strong first-line
recommendation](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-22/) in open cardiothoracic surgery. Nothing here
overturns a guideline built on pooled effect sizes; it does mean the head-to-head literature keeps
failing to reproduce the ranking.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2026-108183)

## 4. Propofol on a human cortical circuit grown in a dish

**Journal · Published:** *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, **30 September 2026** (**new this week**).
Laboratory study in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived assembloids.

> The mechanisms by which anaesthetics alter neurophysiological states remain poorly understood. **Although
> some hypotheses emphasise thalamic suppression, others propose that cortical (Cx) circuits alone,
> comprising interconnected excitatory and inhibitory neurones expressing GABA-A receptors, are sufficient
> for anaesthetic-induced neural dynamics.** We generated human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived
> Cx-ganglionic eminence (GE) assembloids… We recorded local field potentials and microelectrode array
> activity from assembloids exposed to **96 μM propofol**, analysing changes in extracellular field delta
> power (0.1–4 Hz) and **Lempel-Ziv complexity**, and neuronal firing rates. Control experiments included
> propofol application to **excitatory Cx-only organoids** and co-application of propofol with the
> competitive GABA-A receptor antagonist **bicuculline**…
>
> Propofol exposure **significantly increased relative delta power (P=0.0078), reduced signal complexity
> (P=0.0117)** of local field potentials, and **suppressed neuronal firing rates (P=0.0156). These effects
> were absent in excitatory Cx-only organoids and were abolished by GABA-A receptor antagonism with
> bicuculline**, confirming the requirement for both interneurones and GABAergic signalling…
>
> Human Cx-GE assembloids recapitulate key electrophysiological features of anaesthetic states,
> **demonstrating that a minimal Cx circuit containing excitatory and inhibitory neurones with functional
> GABA-A receptors is sufficient to generate anaesthetic-induced alterations in neural dynamics.**

**Interpretation.** This is a long way from the bedside and it is here because it answers a question
clinicians actually argue about, with an experiment that could not previously be done.

**The two electrophysiological signatures anaesthetists rely on — rising delta power and falling signal
complexity — were reproduced in a pea-sized human tissue construct with no thalamus, no brainstem and no
sensory input.** Depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring is built on those signatures, and the Lempel-Ziv
complexity measure used here is closely related to the complexity indices used in consciousness research.
That they emerge from cortex alone is a real constraint on theory: **thalamic suppression cannot be
necessary for these dynamics, whatever else it contributes in an intact brain.**

**The control experiments are what make it convincing**, and they are the reason this is worth reporting
rather than filing. The effects **vanished in excitatory-only organoids** and were **abolished by
bicuculline** — so the result depends on inhibitory interneurones and on GABA-A signalling specifically,
rather than being any nonspecific drug effect on tissue. A positive finding alone would have been weak;
two negative controls in the predicted directions make the mechanism claim stand up.

**Three limits, stated plainly.** *Sufficient is not necessary and not equivalent* — showing cortex can
generate these dynamics says nothing about what produces unconsciousness in a patient, where thalamocortical
loops are intact and the endpoint is behavioural, not spectral. **96 μM is a single concentration**, so
there is no dose-response here. And **assembloids are immature tissue** without the myelination, glial
maturity or long-range connectivity of an adult brain, which is a particular caveat for the paper's own
suggestion that the model could illuminate **perioperative neurocognitive disorders** — a condition of
mostly older brains studied in a developmentally early one.

The claimed applications are reasonable as aspirations and should be read that way: **drug screening, and
individual differences in anaesthetic sensitivity including genetic risk for unintentional awareness with
recall.** Awareness is rare enough that mechanistic work on susceptibility has been nearly impossible; a
tractable human-derived platform is a plausible route in. **None of that has been demonstrated yet** —
the paper demonstrates that the model behaves like cortex under propofol, which is the necessary first
step and not the same thing.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2026.07.062)
