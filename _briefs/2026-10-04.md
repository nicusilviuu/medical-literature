## 1. The Brain Injury Guidelines: 98% sensitivity, 13% specificity

**Journal · Published:** *CJEM*, **2 October 2026** — two days old. Systematic review and meta-analysis,
PRISMA, PROSPERO CRD42023481186. **Routed here from [yesterday's guidelines
watch](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-03/)**, where it was identified as a research article rather
than a guideline despite its title.
Zelt, Graham, Altuntur, Davidson, Saluja

> **Objectives** The Brain Injury Guidelines (BIG) serve to predict the severity of traumatic brain injuries
> resulting in radiologic findings. **The use of BIG promises significant time and resource savings, over
> routine neurosurgical consultation and repeat imaging.** After more than a decade of validation, studies
> have demonstrated BIG to reliably predict adverse outcomes…
>
> **Methods** … **Seven databases** were searched, articles from inception to October 2025… Pooled
> sensitivities and specificities calculated using **random effects meta-analysis.** The main outcomes were
> **neurologic deterioration, neurosurgical intervention and death.** Bias was assessed using a **modified
> PROBAST tool.**
>
> **Results** From the **4,733 studies screened, 47 articles** were identified for inclusion, **of which 23
> studied the original BIG and were suitable for meta-analysis.** … The pooled sensitivity and specificity
> for **BIG1** to rule out neurological deterioration, neurosurgical intervention and death were **98.3%
> (95%CI 95.7–99.4) and 12.7% (95%CI 1.5–58.7)**, **98.7% (97.4–99.4) and 14.4% (3.3–45.8)**, and **98.2%
> (96.6–99.1) and 12.8% (3.0–41.0)** respectively.
>
> **Conclusions BIG is highly sensitive** … **may be used to identify patients appropriate for discharge,
> without further immediate investigations or consultation** … **BIG specificity is low, however**, further
> study of BIG variants may offer solutions.

**Interpretation.** **Put the two numbers side by side and the tool changes character.** Sensitivity of
98.2–98.7% with specificity of 12.7–14.4% describes a rule that **almost never misses an adverse outcome and
almost never tells you a patient is safe.** Roughly seven of eight patients who will not deteriorate are
nonetheless flagged by it.

**That is not a criticism — it is a description of what the rule is for, and it is different from what the
first line of the abstract says it is for.** A rule-out instrument with near-perfect sensitivity is exactly
what you want for the decision "can this patient go home without a neurosurgical opinion," because the cost
of a miss is catastrophic and the cost of a false alarm is an opinion. **But the stated promise is "significant
time and resource savings," and a test that flags 86–87% of non-deteriorating patients does not save much
consultation.** Both things can be true: the rule is safe to discharge on, and it will not reduce
consultations as much as advertised.

**The specificity confidence intervals are the part that should stop a reader.** For neurological
deterioration, pooled specificity is **12.7% with an interval of 1.5% to 58.7%.** That spans "flags
essentially everyone" to "a genuinely discriminating test." **An interval that wide across 23 studies is not
an estimate; it is a statement that the studies disagree profoundly**, almost certainly because BIG's
specificity depends on the case mix walking through each door. The authors do not hide it — "BIG specificity
is low" is in the conclusion — but the uncertainty is larger than "low" conveys.

**Two structural points worth stating.** Of **47 included articles only 23 were poolable**, and of the
remainder **just 2 studied BIG variants, with insufficient data to meta-analyse** — so the paper's own
proposed solution, that variants may fix the specificity, rests on two unpoolable studies. And **outcomes
were pooled as rule-outs for three different events** — deterioration, intervention, death — which are
nested rather than independent, so the three sensitivity figures are not three separate confirmations.

**Read against [yesterday's SFNC traumatic brain injury
guidelines](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-03/) the pair is complementary and worth holding together:
the French document says when to operate; this one says when not to pick up the phone.** Neither addresses
intracranial pressure management, which remains the uncovered middle of neurotrauma in this archive.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43678-026-01218-y)

## 2. TISA-818 in ARDS — a phase 2 trial that needs reading carefully

**Journal · Published:** *Chest*, **3 October 2026** — **published yesterday.** Randomised, double-blind,
placebo-controlled, multicentre phase 2 trial; **the first trial in China to use the 2023 global ARDS
definition**, by the authors' account.
Huang, Huang, Jiao, Sun, Han, Jiang, Zhang, Luo, Su, Gu, He, Jiao, Tang, Jiang, Wang, Yang et al.

> **The 2023 global definition includes non-intubated patients, creating an opportunity to evaluate therapies
> earlier in the disease course.** … **Fifty-eight patients were randomized 1:1:1** to intravenous TISA-818
> **6 mg twice daily, 12 mg once daily, or matched placebo for 14 days. The primary endpoint was safety.**
> Key secondary endpoints included respiratory support-free days (RSFDs) through day 28…
>
> TISA-818 demonstrated an acceptable safety profile… **Treatment-related AEs were mild-to-moderate and
> occurred in 5.3%, 36.8%, and 21.1%** of patients in the placebo, 6 mg BID, and 12 mg QD groups. **Day-28
> mortality was 5.3%, 10.5%, and 26.3%, whereas day-60 mortality was 26.3%, 21.1%, and 31.6%** for placebo,
> 6 mg BID, and 12 mg QD. **No drug-related serious AEs or deaths occurred.**
>
> **In the prespecified non-intubated subgroup (n=27, 9 per arm), exploratory analyses numerically favored
> 6 mg BID over placebo, with more RSFDs (difference, 6 days; 95% CI, −3 to 15)** and consistent numerical
> improvements across all secondary outcomes.
>
> **Day-28 mortality was numerically imbalanced but not sustained, likely reflecting small-sample
> variability; cautious interpretation is warranted. Consistent improvements across all efficacy endpoints,
> particularly with 6 mg BID in non-intubated patients, support further investigation in adequately powered
> trials.**

**Interpretation.** **The authors flag the problem themselves, which earns them credit, and the conclusion
then sets it aside, which does not.**

**Day-28 mortality was 5.3% on placebo, 10.5% on the lower dose and 26.3% on the higher dose** — a
monotonic rise with exposure. The abstract reports only percentages; **reconstructing from them, the
denominators are 19 per arm, so these are 1, 2 and 5 deaths** — and that reconstruction is mine, not the
paper's, because 58 randomised across three arms cannot divide evenly and the abstract does not give counts.
**Five deaths against one is a difference of four patients.** The authors are right that this is fragile.

**Their argument that it was "not sustained" is supported by the day-60 figures and is worth taking
seriously:** placebo mortality rose from 5.3% to 26.3% while the high-dose arm went 26.3% to 31.6% — so by
day 60 the gap had largely closed. **That is a real defence, and it means the day-28 signal was substantially
a difference in timing of death rather than in number.**

**What does not survive is the conclusion's framing.** "Consistent improvements across all efficacy endpoints"
rests on the **non-intubated subgroup of 27 patients, nine per arm**, where the headline respiratory
support-free day difference is **6 days with an interval of −3 to 15** — an interval comfortably containing
zero and containing harm. **Nine patients per arm cannot establish consistency across anything.** With that
many endpoints and that few patients, all moving in one direction is unremarkable; it is what noise looks
like when the endpoints are correlated, which RSFDs, day-14 support-free status, clinical improvement rate and
length of stay all are. **They are largely the same measurement reported four ways.**

**And the safety claim deserves one more look, since safety was the primary endpoint.** Treatment-related
adverse events ran at **5.3% on placebo against 36.8% on the 6 mg twice-daily dose** — reconstructing again,
**1 patient versus 7.** The paper calls this "comparable rates of treatment-emergent adverse events" and an
"acceptable safety profile," and the distinction it is relying on is between *treatment-emergent* (any event)
and *treatment-related* (judged attributable). **Those are different denominators doing different work in the
same sentence**, and the seven-fold difference in the attributable column is the one a reader should carry
forward into the phase 3.

**The genuinely novel thing here has nothing to do with the drug.** It is the first trial this archive has
seen to use the **2023 global ARDS definition**, which admits non-intubated patients — and the paper's own
most interesting result is structural: **the prespecified non-intubated subgroup is where the signal was**,
which is the hypothesis the new definition exists to permit. **That is worth watching regardless of whether
TISA-818 survives.** It also places this alongside the lung-ultrasound-in-ARDS paper under the 2023
definition logged in [the 1 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-01/) and still unwritten.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.09.095)

## 3. Twenty-seven years of analgosedation protocols, and none in ECMO patients

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Critical Care*, **3 October 2026** — **published yesterday.** Scoping
review, five databases, **December 1999 to September 2026.**
Remmington, McKenzie, Camporota, Lee, Milic, Barker, Harper, Rose

> Our objectives were to assess structure and content of analgosedation protocols … **including those
> receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)**, against current international sedation guidelines,
> and to explore reported **barriers and facilitators** to implementation… coded using the **Theoretical
> Domains Framework.**
>
> We screened **18,457 citations with 81 studies** meeting inclusion criteria. Of these, **35 (43%) were
> observational studies, 27 (33%) non-randomised interventional studies, and 19 (23%) randomised controlled
> trials. Few studies published between 2019 and 2026 included propofol or dexmedetomidine, multi-modal
> analgesia and delirium assessment in analgosedation protocols, and no studies in ECMO patients.**
>
> The most frequent protocol content was **use of a sedation score (77%)**, recommending opioid and sedative
> drugs (74%), and setting a **daily target sedation score (65%)**. The most reported barrier was **a
> protocol insufficiently detailed to cover the breadth of patient needs, reported in 12 (63%) studies.** The
> most frequently reported facilitator was **availability of the protocol in an easily accessible format,
> reported in four (31%) studies.** … **Healthcare staff fear of adverse effects is a potential barrier.**

**Interpretation.** **The null finding is the finding: across 18,457 citations and 27 years, not one study
examined an analgosedation protocol in ECMO patients** — a population that is deeply sedated, often
paralysed, has radically altered drug pharmacokinetics from circuit sequestration, and is managed in exactly
the units most likely to have a protocol. **ECMO was named in the objectives, which means the authors went
looking and found nothing.** That is a cleanly identified gap and this archive has nowhere else recorded it.

**The second finding is nearly as pointed: few protocols published since 2019 included propofol or
dexmedetomidine, multi-modal analgesia, or delirium assessment** — the three things international sedation
guidelines have been recommending for years. **So the protocols in the literature lag their own guidelines.**
Set against [yesterday's sedation block](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-03/), where A2B found no
extubation advantage for either α2 agonist and a rat study found dexmedetomidine's recovery profile the worst
of four agents, there is a certain irony in protocols being criticised for omitting dexmedetomidine. **The
honest reading is that the protocols are out of step with guidelines, and the guidelines may be out of step
with the trials.**

**One reading trap in the numbers, and it matters because these are the headline percentages.** "12 (63%)
studies" and "four (31%) studies" cannot both be fractions of 81. Reconstructing, **the barrier denominator
is 19 and the facilitator denominator is 13** — i.e. only 19 of the 81 studies reported barriers at all, and
only 13 reported facilitators. **The abstract never states those denominators.** So "the most frequently
reported facilitator" was reported by **four studies out of eighty-one**, which is a much weaker statement
than "31%" suggests. **Percentages whose denominators are not stated should be converted back to counts
before being quoted**, and the counts here are small.

**What the review cannot do, by design, is tell you whether protocols work.** A scoping review maps the
literature; 43% of the included studies are observational and only 19 are randomised. **The useful output is
the gap list, not an effectiveness claim**, and the authors frame it that way.

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155766)

## 4. What aviation, the military and team sport know about deciding under time pressure

**Journal · Published:** *CJEM*, **3 October 2026** — **published yesterday.** **Narrative review** — the
authors label it as such — of 25 studies drawn from across healthcare, emergency response, crisis management,
aviation, sport, business and military contexts.
Raymond, Profeit, Hall, Ly, Cheung

> **Team decision-making is central to effective practice in high-acuity healthcare environments, yet most
> decision-making research in medicine remains focused on individuals rather than teams.** … A systematic
> search of MEDLINE and Scopus … identified **595 unique records**; after review by the first and second
> authors, **25 studies met inclusion criteria** and focused explicitly on team-level decisions made under
> conditions of time pressure and high stakes. … **These studies were then analyzed by the first author and
> themes were identified.**
>
> Content analysis revealed **five factors: role clarity, hierarchy, information sharing, team cognition, and
> team training.** … **clear and mutually understood roles** facilitate coordination, whereas **role
> ambiguity resulted in conflict, redundancy, or unsafe task prioritization. Flexible, psychologically safe
> hierarchies, rather than rigid structures, enabled inclusive communication and reduced deference-based
> errors. Effective information sharing required both sufficiency and restraint**, emphasising relevance,
> timeliness and shared situational awareness. **Team cognition, including the development of shared mental
> models, was foundational.** … **team training approaches that emphasize adaptability, cross-role
> understanding, pre-briefing, and debriefing cultivated resilience and improved decision accuracy.**

**Interpretation.** This belongs in a literature brief for anaesthesia and critical care because **it arrives
one working week after a national airway guideline shipped the exact artefacts it identifies.**

[The SFAR adult airway recommendations reported on 30 September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/)
devoted one of their three fields to human factors, with a human-factors society as formal co-author, and
shipped **cognitive aids for conducting a briefing and a debriefing** alongside the intubation algorithms.
**Here is an independent cross-domain review concluding that pre-briefing and debriefing are among the
training approaches that actually improve decision accuracy under pressure.** A guideline produced the tool;
a review of aviation, military and sport evidence identifies the mechanism. **Those two documents did not
cite each other and arrived five days apart.**

**Two of the five factors cut against how acute teams are usually run.** *Flexible, psychologically safe
hierarchies rather than rigid structures, reducing deference-based errors* is an argument against the clear
command structure that resuscitation teaching tends to emphasise — or more precisely, an argument that command
clarity and permission to speak up are different variables and only the first is routinely taught. And
*information sharing required both sufficiency and restraint* names something rarely said aloud: **in a
time-pressured resuscitation, more communication is not better, and a team member reporting everything they
know is a hazard.** That is the opposite of the instinct most handover training instils.

**The method is the weak point and should be stated plainly, not least because of what the paper is about.**
This is a **narrative review**, not a systematic one — the authors say so. **Of 595 records, 25 were included,
and the thematic analysis was performed by a single author**, which in a paper arguing for the value of
multiple perspectives and shared cognition is an irony worth noting rather than glossing. The evidence base
spans domains whose stakes, training budgets and team stability differ enormously: **a flight crew and a
night-shift resuscitation team are not the same object**, and transferability is asserted rather than
demonstrated. **Treat the five factors as a well-organised hypothesis set rather than as findings.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43678-026-01285-1)
