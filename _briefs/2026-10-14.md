## 1. VICTORY: vitamin C stopped for harm in severe burns — and the harm is carried by the adjustment

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**, 3 citations. **Phase 3,
double-blind, placebo-controlled, 24 burn centres across five regions**, enrolling August 2020 to September
2025. **238 patients** (mean age 48.9, 79% male, **mean total body surface area 37.0%**), deep second- and/or
third-degree burns over **≥20% TBSA** requiring grafting. **Vitamin C 50 mg/kg every 6 hours for 96 hours**
versus matched placebo. **Stopped early at the first prespecified interim analysis for futility/harm.**

> **Results** … 120 were assigned to vitamin C and 118 to placebo. **The primary composite outcome occurred in
> 49 patients (40.8%) in the vitamin C group and 35 patients (29.7%) in the placebo group (adjusted risk ratio
> 1.28 [95% CI, 0.99-1.65]; P = .06)**, crossing the prespecified futility/harm threshold and prompting early
> trial termination. Time to discharge alive within 90 days was not improved (adjusted subdistribution hazard
> ratio, 0.85 [0.62-1.16]; P = .31). **Twenty-eight-day mortality was higher in the vitamin C group (15.0% vs
> 7.6%; adjusted RR, 1.96 [1.32-2.90]; P = .001), as was hospital mortality (23.3% vs 16.1%; adjusted RR, 1.44
> [1.03-2.00]; P = .03).**
>
> **Conclusions** … high-dose intravenous vitamin C **did not reduce** 28-day mortality and persistent organ
> dysfunction **and is possibly harmful.**

**Interpretation.** **Take the percentages back to counts, which is this archive's standing habit, and the
trial's structure becomes visible.** 15.0% of 120 is exactly **18 deaths**; 7.6% of 118 is **9**. 23.3% of 120
is **28**; 16.1% of 118 is **19**. No other integers round to those percentages, so the counts are certain.

| Endpoint | Counts | Unadjusted RR (95% CI) | **Reported adjusted RR (95% CI)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary composite | 49/120 vs 35/118 | 1.38 (0.97–1.96) | **1.28 (0.99–1.65)**, P = .06 |
| **28-day mortality** | **18/120 vs 9/118** | **1.97 (0.92–4.20)** | **1.96 (1.32–2.90)**, P = .001 |
| Hospital mortality | 28/120 vs 19/118 | 1.45 (0.86–2.45) | **1.44 (1.03–2.00)**, P = .03 |

**Three things fall out of that table, and the first is the important one.** **On all three endpoints the
adjusted point estimate is essentially identical to the unadjusted one — 1.96 against 1.97, 1.44 against
1.45 — so the covariate model is not moving the estimate. What it is doing is narrowing the interval**, and on
both mortality endpoints that narrowing is what carries them across significance. **Eighteen deaths against
nine is not a significant difference on its own: unadjusted it is 1.97 with an interval from 0.92 to 4.20,
which includes no effect.** The same holds for hospital mortality, 0.86 to 2.45 unadjusted.

**This is not a criticism and I am not claiming an error.** Covariate adjustment legitimately buys precision
when the covariates are strongly prognostic, and in major burns — age, burn size, inhalation injury — they are
about as prognostic as covariates get, which is exactly why prespecifying the adjustment is correct practice.
**The point is what a reader should carry away: the harm conclusion is a model-based result, not an arithmetic
one**, and anyone repeating "mortality doubled" should know it rests on 18 events against 9 in a trial halted
at 238 patients.

**Second, the primary outcome did not reach significance.** P = .06, interval 0.99 to 1.65, and the
investigators say so plainly. **The composite diluted the signal**: pooling death with ventilator, dialysis
and vasopressor dependence at day 28 adds events that vitamin C did not move, and a mortality difference gets
averaged against them. **This is the mirror image of the usual complaint about composites** — normally they
manufacture significance by adding soft events; here they hid a hard one. **A composite is not neutral in
either direction, and which way it leans depends on where the effect sits.**

**Third, the trial's own language is calibrated and worth copying.** "Did not reduce … and is **possibly**
harmful" is the correct strength of claim for this evidence. **A trial stopped at a futility/harm boundary has
done its job by stopping**; it has not established the size of the harm, and the investigators do not pretend
otherwise. **The practical conclusion is unambiguous even so: there is no longer a case for giving high-dose
intravenous vitamin C in severe burns, and there is a signal pointing the other way.** High-dose vitamin C in
critical illness has accumulated a run of neutral and unfavourable trials; this is the burn population's
answer, and it is not favourable. **I am not naming the sepsis trials from memory here** — that is a rule this
archive wrote for itself after getting trial names wrong on 9 October.

## 2. Capillary refill time crosses its own threshold in both directions, in one episode

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **31 August 2026** — **six weeks old**, 0 citations, open access.
**Double-blind, randomised, crossover: 25 healthy volunteers**, intravenous *E. coli* lipopolysaccharide
**0.8 ng/kg** or saline placebo. **Quantitative capillary refill time (qCRT)** by polarised reflectance
imaging — which removes the observer — at baseline and **1.5, 3.5 and 5 hours**, analysed with censored
(Tobit) mixed-effects models.

> **Background** The capillary refill test is a widely used bedside marker of tissue perfusion, but its
> reliability during acute inflammation is not well characterised. **The guiding 3-s threshold rests on limited
> physiological validation and on visual assessment with high observer variability.**
>
> **Results Finger qCRT followed a biphasic course that crossed the 3-s clinical threshold in both directions
> within a single inflammatory episode.** From a baseline near 3.5 s, it was **prolonged to 8.31 s at 1.5 h,
> with 14 of 25 reaching the 10-s ceiling, then shortened to 1.27 s at 5 h during persisting systemic
> inflammation.** Estimated marginal contrasts against placebo were **4.13 s and −3.10 s** (both p < 0.0001).
> … **Skin temperature was the strongest qCRT covariate, with each standard-deviation increase associated with
> a 1.62 s decreased qCRT.** … Resting values were highly variable, **between-subject differences explaining a
> minority of total variance (intraclass correlation 0.30).**
>
> **Conclusions** … **a single value against a fixed threshold is unreliable, whereas change over time within a
> participant is informative.** These findings in healthy participants **are not directly generalizable to
> septic shock** and require confirmation in prospective patient studies.

**Interpretation.** **This is the cleanest demonstration this archive has seen of a monitored number that is
not monotonic in the thing it is supposed to track.** Inflammation was present throughout and rising; capillary
refill went **up, then down past where it started**, ending at 1.27 s — a value that at the bedside reads as
excellent peripheral perfusion — **while the subject was still systemically inflamed.** A clinician reading a
single qCRT at five hours would conclude the opposite of the truth, and would be using the threshold exactly as
taught.

**The intraclass correlation of 0.30 is the quieter finding and may be the more damaging one.** It means
**most of the variation in a resting capillary refill time is within people rather than between them.** A
threshold is a between-person instrument: it asks whether *this* patient's value is abnormal relative to
patients in general. **If between-person differences are a minority of the variance, a fixed cut-off is
measuring mostly noise** — and the authors draw the right conclusion, which is that the trajectory in one
patient carries the information and the single reading does not. **That is a different clinical instruction
from the one in current use.**

**Then skin temperature: 1.62 s per standard deviation.** The 3-second threshold spans roughly two standard
deviations of skin temperature. **A cold finger and a warm finger are on opposite sides of the cut-off before
perfusion is considered at all**, and the authors note the late shortening was attenuated after adjustment for
thermoregulatory and haemodynamic covariates — so part of the dramatic fall is the periphery warming up as the
febrile response develops, not perfusion improving.

**Set against this archive's monitoring thread, this is a fourth distinct failure mode.** The thread held
three: the measurement changes (videolaryngoscopy), the calibration changes (remimazolam and BIS), and the
number is not on the causal path (AlphaMax). **This one is new: the number reverses direction while the disease
it indexes continues.** That is worse than drift, because the error is not an attenuated signal but an inverted
one.

**The limitation is stated by the authors and belongs in any use of this paper: 25 healthy volunteers given
endotoxin are not patients in septic shock**, and no mediation analysis was done. **What the study does
establish is that the 3-second threshold has no physiological warrant to be a threshold** — which it never
really had, as the background section concedes.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06247-8) · *Critical Care*, NCT06618716

## 3. Seventy trials on tight glucose control: the number moves, infection moves, mortality does not

**Journal · Published:** *Nursing in Critical Care*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**, 0 citations.
**Systematic review and meta-analysis to 30 October 2024**, PRISMA, Cochrane RoB 2, random effects, **trial
sequential analysis on the primary outcome.** **70 randomised trials, 36,502 patients** — 64 trials and 32,491
patients (89%) in adults, 6 trials and 4,011 patients (11%) in children. **Held in this archive's queue for
four days and published here now.**

> **Results** … **The RR of all-cause mortality after intensive and liberal glucose control was 0.99 (95% CI,
> 0.93-1.05) in adults.** Comparable findings were observed … in children. **Intensive glucose control had a
> statistically significantly higher risk of severe hypoglycaemia in both children (RR 5.70; 2.60-12.51) and
> adults (RR 3.55; 2.49-5.07).** However, intensive glucose control had a **statistically lower risk of
> infection in both children (RR 0.83; 0.70-0.98) and adults (RR 0.78; 0.63-0.97).** In the subgroup analysis
> of adults, **a lower risk of infection was observed in all surgical groups, but not in the medical group.**
> There was no statistically significant difference in other complications, including sepsis, acute renal
> injury, new need for dialysis and need for blood transfusion.
>
> **Relevance to clinical practice** … **routine intensive glucose control does not improve survival and should
> be applied cautiously due to increased hypoglycaemia risk**, although it may reduce infection risk,
> particularly in surgical ICU patients.

**Interpretation.** **The mortality result is as close to a settled null as critical care gets: RR 0.99 with an
interval of 0.93 to 1.05 across 36,502 patients, with a trial sequential analysis behind it.** That interval
excludes anything clinically meaningful in either direction. **Twenty-five years of argument, and the answer is
that it does not matter for survival.**

**But this is the paper this archive's monitoring thread has been missing, and it arrived from the queue rather
than from a search.** The thread asks for a trial that **moved a monitored variable and changed an outcome** —
the positive case it entirely lacked. **Here it is, twice over, in opposite directions.** Driving glucose down
**reduced infection** (adults RR 0.78, 0.63–0.97) and **tripled severe hypoglycaemia** (RR 3.55, 2.49–5.07),
with a **5.7-fold** increase in children. **So targeting the number does change what happens to patients. It
simply does not change the outcome the targeting was justified by.**

**That is a more interesting result than a flat null, and it reframes the thread.** The failure mode is not
"the number is inert." It is that **a monitored variable can sit on a causal path to several outcomes at once,
pointing in opposite directions, and net to nothing on mortality.** Tight control buys fewer infections and
pays in hypoglycaemia, and the two cancel on survival. **A protocol that targets the number alone cannot
express that trade-off**, which is why the honest recommendation is the cautious, patient-specific one the
authors give.

**The surgical-versus-medical split is where the clinical content is.** The infection benefit appears in **all
surgical subgroups and not in the medical group.** That is a plausible biological story — wound and
surgical-site infection is glucose-sensitive in a way ventilator-associated pneumonia in a medical patient is
not — and it is a specificity finding, which is one of the two controls this archive uses for an interaction.
**It is a subgroup analysis in a meta-analysis of 70 trials, so it is hypothesis-generating and not
practice-changing**, but it points somewhere concrete: the patients plausibly helped are surgical, and they are
also the ones in whom hypoglycaemia is most monitorable.

**Two cautions on the document itself.** The paediatric arm is **6 trials and 4,011 patients**, 11% of the
total, and the authors flag it as limited — the 5.70 hypoglycaemia risk ratio has an interval from 2.60 to
12.51, which is a wide thing to quote. **And the search closed on 30 October 2024**, nearly two years before
publication, so anything since is not in it.

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/nicc.70611) · *Nursing in Critical Care*

## 4. STOP-Vanc: a swab that was right 98.9% of the time and changed nothing

**Journal · Published:** *Clinical Infectious Diseases*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**, 0 citations,
open access. **Pragmatic, prospective, single-centre, non-blinded randomised trial**, **277 adult ICU patients**
with suspected community-acquired pneumonia, randomised 1:1 to usual care **with or without MRSA nares PCR**
after ICU admission. **Primary outcome: vancomycin-free hours alive** in the first 7 days, by a longitudinal
proportional-odds state-transition model adjusted for baseline covariates.

> **Results** … **MRSA PCR nasal swab testing had a negative predictive value of 98.9% in the intervention
> arm.** The primary endpoint, vancomycin-free hours alive, was **105.7 in the control arm and 109.7 in the
> intervention arm (adjusted difference, 4 hours; 95% CI, −9.5-18.2; P = .458).**
>
> **Conclusions Despite MRSA PCR nasal swab testing demonstrating a high NPV in this critically ill
> population, MRSA PCR nasal swab testing did not decrease the duration of vancomycin use or 30-day
> mortality** … **Additional clinician education and antimicrobial stewardship interventions might be needed.**

**Interpretation.** **The test worked and the trial failed, and those are not in tension — they are the
finding.** A negative predictive value of 98.9% means a negative swab all but excludes MRSA pneumonia in this
population. **The information was available, accurate and in the chart. Vancomycin-free hours moved by four out
of about 108, with an interval from −9.5 to 18.2.** Nobody acted on it.

**This is the sharpest possible statement of a problem the monitoring thread keeps circling: a measurement is
not an intervention.** Diagnostic accuracy and clinical effect are separate quantities, and a test can be
excellent at the first while having no purchase on the second. **The causal chain from swab to shorter
antibiotic course runs through a clinician's decision to stop a drug in a patient who is critically ill and
might deteriorate** — and that link, not the test's performance, is the rate-limiting step. **The authors say
so in their conclusion, which is the right place to put it.**

**What makes this trial unusually clean is that it rules out the usual excuses.** The test was not inaccurate.
The result was not unavailable. The population was not low-risk in a way that made the question academic — these
were ICU patients with suspected pneumonia, the exact group empiric vancomycin is aimed at. **So the residual
explanation is behavioural, and it is the explanation the trial leaves standing.** Note also what the primary
endpoint is: **vancomycin-free hours *alive*,** which handles death honestly rather than letting a dead patient
count as antibiotic-free — the same design care this archive noted in the
[EQ-5D-scores-death-as-zero case on 12 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-12/).

**The practical reading for a stewardship programme is uncomfortable and useful: adding this test to an ICU
will not by itself reduce vancomycin days.** It needs to be attached to something that acts — a pharmacist
prompt, a stop order, a daily review — and the trial is evidence that availability of the result is not that
something. **Caveat stated plainly: single centre, non-blinded, 277 patients**, so a differently organised unit
might respond differently. **But the direction of the finding is not a power problem** — the point estimate is
four hours.
