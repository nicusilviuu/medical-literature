## 1. What 1,493 French emergency clinicians actually do

**Journal · Published:** *International Journal for Quality in Health Care*, **1 January 2026** — nine
months old. National survey, 32-question online questionnaire, **distributed by SFAR and SFMU**, open
**1 October to 1 December 2023.** Routed here from [yesterday's guidelines
watch](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-05/), where it surfaced in the SFMU seam audit.
Moyer, Zappella, Ramonda, Abback, Ouatiki, Devauchelle, Cesareo, Clavier, and the ACUTE SFAR group

> **Background** Effective communication and team organization are critical to patient safety during
> emergencies. **Although national guidelines in France highlight the importance of human factors, their
> implementation in clinical practice remains unclear.**
>
> **Methods** A nationwide survey … distributed through social media platforms by the French Society of
> Anesthesiology and Intensive Care and the French Society of Emergency Medicine…
>
> **Results** A total of **1493 respondents** participated, of which 856 (58%) were female. Physicians
> represented 42% (n = 620). **Most respondents (61%) reported being unfamiliar with the national guidelines
> on human factors in critical care. Utilization of structured communication tools during critical
> situations — such as closed-loop communication (31%), time-outs (16%), and debriefings (35%) — was
> limited, particularly among paramedical staff.** Although respondents widely acknowledged the importance
> of communication, **90% reported receiving no or less than one theoretical training session per year, and
> 81% had minimal exposure to simulation-based communication training.**
>
> **Conclusion** … **Targeted interventions, particularly simulation-based training, are needed.**

**Interpretation.** **Sixteen per cent for time-outs is the number to sit with.** The time-out is the
simplest, cheapest and most widely endorsed structured communication tool in acute medicine, and five in six
of these clinicians do not use one in a critical situation.

**And the self-selection runs the wrong way for comfort.** This was a voluntary survey distributed over
social media by two professional societies — **so the respondents are people engaged enough with their
societies to answer a questionnaire about human factors. If anything, 61% unfamiliar with the national
guidelines is an optimistic estimate of the profession**, not a pessimistic one. A convenience sample
usually weakens a prevalence claim; here it biases it in the favourable direction, which makes the finding
harder to dismiss rather than easier.

**One point of fairness about timing, because it would be easy to misuse this paper.** The survey ran
**October to December 2023**, nearly three years before [the SFAR airway recommendations reported here on 30
September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) devoted a third of their document to human factors and
shipped cognitive aids for briefing and debriefing. **So this is not a verdict on that guidance — it is the
baseline that guidance is trying to move**, and it was collected by the same two societies that went on to
write it. That is the useful reading: the societies measured the problem first.

**Set against what this archive has accumulated, the asymmetry is stark.** Four documents of guidance in
eight days — [the SFAR airway human-factors field](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/), [the IONM
timeline checklists](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-03/), [the CJEM cross-domain review identifying
pre-briefing and debriefing](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-04/), and [the Moyamoya ERAS
protocol](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-03/) — against **16% time-out use and 90% receiving less
than one training session a year.** **The gap here is not knowledge, and it is not guidance. It is that
nobody has been trained.**

[Read the survey](https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzag007)

## 2. Does team training change patient outcomes? The one meta-analysis says maybe, and disagrees with itself

**Journal · Published:** *Acta Obstetricia et Gynecologica Scandinavica*, **8 October 2021** — **five years
old, at the outer edge of this archive's recency window, and included because it is the only patient-outcome
synthesis the human-factors thread has.** 25 citations, open access in PubMed Central. PRISMA, GRADE.
Brogaard, Glerup Lauridsen, Løfgren, Krogh, Paltved, Boie, Hvidman

> **Little is known about the optimal simulation-based team training in obstetric emergencies.** … RCTs and
> cohort studies on obstetric teams **in high-resource settings** comparing simulation-based obstetric
> emergency team training with no training on the risk of **Apgar scores less than 7 at 5 min, neonatal
> hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, severe postpartum hemorrhage, blood transfusion of four or more units,
> and delay of emergency cesarean section by more than 30 min.**
>
> **Results** We found **21 studies, four RCTs and 17 cohort studies.** Annual obstetric emergency team
> training **may reduce brachial plexus injury (six cohort studies: odds ratio 0.47, 95% CI 0.33–0.68; one
> RCT: OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.39–4.33, low certainty evidence)** and suggest a positive effect; **but it was not
> significant on Apgar score below 7 at 5 min (three cohort studies: OR 0.77, 0.51–1.19; two RCTs: OR 0.87,
> 0.72–1.05, moderate certainty evidence). The effect was unclear for hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy,
> umbilical prolapse, decision to birth interval in emergency cesarean section, and for severe postpartum
> hemorrhage. Studies with in situ multi-professional simulation-based training demonstrated the best
> effect.**
>
> **Conclusions Emerging evidence suggests an effect … but conflicting results call for controlled trials
> targeted to identify the optimal methodology.**

**Interpretation.** **Read the brachial plexus line carefully, because it contains the whole problem of this
field in one parenthesis: six cohort studies give OR 0.47, and the single randomised trial gives OR 1.30.**
The observational evidence says team training halves the injury rate; the trial point estimate says it
raises it. The trial's interval is wide enough (0.39–4.33) to contain both, so this is not a refutation — but
**the only outcome in the review with a positive signal is the one where the designs disagree in
direction.**

**That is the classic signature of confounding by institutional quality.** Hospitals that run annual
multi-professional emergency drills are hospitals with staffing, funding and a safety culture, and those
things reduce brachial plexus injury whether or not the drill does. **A cohort comparison of trained against
untrained units is substantially a comparison of good units against less good ones.**

**And the better-powered outcome is null in both designs.** Apgar below 7 at five minutes — the most
objective endpoint in the list — gave OR 0.77 across cohorts and **0.87 (0.72–1.05) across two RCTs**, at
**moderate** certainty, the highest certainty grade anywhere in the review. **So the outcome with the best
evidence shows no effect, and the outcome with a signal has conflicting designs.**

**The one finding worth carrying forward is the modifier:** *studies with in situ multi-professional
simulation-based training demonstrated the best effect.* **In situ** means in the real clinical environment
rather than a simulation centre, and **multi-professional** means the actual team rather than one discipline.
Both are what the CJEM review identified independently on 4 October, and both are what item 1 says 81% of
French clinicians have minimal exposure to.

**Where that leaves the thread, stated plainly.** This archive now holds **four documents of human-factors
guidance, one national survey showing the tools are barely used, and one patient-outcome meta-analysis whose
positive signal does not survive randomisation.** The honest summary is that **the case for structured
communication tools rests on mechanism, expert consensus and cross-domain analogy rather than on
demonstrated patient benefit** — and that the trials the 2021 review called for do not appear to have
arrived in five years. **That is not an argument against time-outs. It is an argument that the field has
been writing guidance faster than it has been generating evidence**, and a reader should know which they are
being handed.

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/aogs.14263)

## 3. A 2,485-patient trial where one technique won on both diagnosis and safety

**Journal · Published:** *Chest*, **3 October 2026** — three days old. Prospective multicentre
three-arm randomised controlled trial, June 2020 to November 2023. ChiCTR2100045374.
Bi, Shen, Xu, Tang, Sun, Ye, Zhang, Cong, Zhu, Chen, Tang, Wang

> **Research question** Is US-guided percutaneous lung needle biopsy superior to CT-guided biopsy in
> diagnostic performance and safety, and **does contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) provide further
> advantages?**
>
> **Dual primary outcomes were diagnostic yield and disease-specific diagnostic sensitivity.** Diagnostic
> yield was defined according to **the strict ATS/ACCP definition** as the proportion of biopsies yielding
> clinically actionable diagnoses…
>
> **Results** A total of **2485 participants** were randomized into the US (n=828), CEUS (n=829) and CT
> (n=828) groups. **CEUS-guided biopsy achieved the highest diagnostic yield (CEUS 88.8% [736/829] vs. CT
> 78.9% [653/828] vs. US 76.0% [629/828], P <0.001)**, as well as the highest sensitivity (**86.1% vs.
> 74.3% vs. 68.7%**). For malignant subtypes, **91.8% vs. 83.2% vs. 75.2%**; for specific benign disorders,
> **69.9% [151/216] vs. 46.8% [95/203] vs. 47.1% [90/191]**. Furthermore, **the CEUS group exhibited the
> lowest overall complication rate (CEUS 4.6% [38/828] vs. US 7.5% [60/802] vs. CT 15.8% [127/805],
> P <0.001).**

**Interpretation.** **Trials where one arm wins on both efficacy and safety are rare, and this one wins by a
wide margin on both.** Diagnostic yield 88.8% against 78.9% for CT, and a complication rate of 4.6% against
15.8% — **CT's complication rate is 3.4 times CEUS's.** The usual shape of an imaging-guidance comparison is
a trade: more diagnostic certainty for more risk. Here the trade does not appear.

**The benign-disease result is the most interesting and the least expected.** For specific benign disorders,
CEUS reached **69.9% sensitivity against 46.8% for CT and 47.1% for ultrasound** — ultrasound and CT
essentially tied and CEUS was 23 points better. **Benign lesions are where biopsy most often fails to give
an answer**, and contrast appears to be doing something there that neither plain modality does, plausibly by
distinguishing perfused tissue from necrosis and so showing where to put the needle.

**Four things to hold against it.**

**The complication denominators do not match the randomised arms** — 828, 802 and 805 against 828, 829 and
828. So **27 CEUS and 23 CT participants are missing from the safety analysis**, which the abstract does not
explain. On a key safety outcome that matters, and the direction of any bias cannot be determined from here.

**It is a single-country trial** (Chinese registry, recruitment 2020–2023), and **CEUS is operator-dependent
and contrast-dependent** in a way CT is not. A technique that performs this well in centres that chose to
run a CEUS trial may not transfer to centres where the skill base is thinner — the same caveat the archive
applied to lung ultrasound yesterday, and for the same reason.

**Blinding is not described in the abstract**, and diagnostic yield assessed against the ATS/ACCP definition
requires adjudication. Who adjudicated, and whether they knew the guidance modality, is the question a
reader needs and does not get.

**And the comparison is not quite three equal arms.** The research question as stated asks first whether
ultrasound beats CT and then whether CEUS adds — but **ultrasound was the worst arm on every diagnostic
outcome**, so the guideline-recommended approach lost to the one it was meant to replace, and the paper's
headline is about the third arm. **The ordering of the research question and the ordering of the results do
not match**, which is worth noticing even though nothing is hidden.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.09.089)

## 4. A sepsis biomarker that stops predicting in patients with diabetes

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care Medicine*, **30 September 2026** — six days old. Secondary cohort
analysis of the ACTS trial research materials, academic hospitals in the United States.
Gaulton, Rigsby, Shapiro, Schmidt, Cereda

> **466 adults with sepsis** and respiratory failure or vasopressor-dependent shock with available baseline
> soluble tumour necrosis factor receptor 1 (sTNFR1)… interaction tested **using Cox regression with
> restricted cubic splines**, adjusted for illness severity, age and sex.
>
> **Of 466 patients, 243 (52.1%) had diabetes or ASCVD. Despite similar illness severity and mortality
> between groups, sTNFR1 was higher among patients with diabetes or ASCVD (median 6547 vs. 4883 pg/mL).
> Among patients without diabetes or ASCVD, higher sTNFR1 was associated with 90-day mortality (hazard ratio
> 2.25 per sd; 95% CI, 1.76–2.88). The association was attenuated among those with diabetes or ASCVD, with a
> plateau in mortality risk above approximately 12,000 pg/mL (overall HR 1.04; 95% CI, 0.83–1.30; interaction
> p < 0.001). This interaction was specific to sTNFR1 and was not observed for interleukin-6 or
> angiopoietin-2. No effect modification was present among patients with obesity or hypertension without
> diabetes or ASCVD.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **comorbid conditions may alter the biological relationship between a biomarker and
> outcome.**

**Interpretation.** **The negative controls are what make this credible, and they run in two directions at
once.** The interaction appears for **sTNFR1 but not for interleukin-6 or angiopoietin-2** measured in the
same patients, and it appears for **diabetes or ASCVD but not for obesity or hypertension** in patients
without them. **A spurious interaction has no reason to be specific to one biomarker and one comorbidity
pair**; this one is specific in both dimensions.

**That matters because it completes a four-paper pattern this archive opened yesterday**, and the pattern is
about method rather than about any one marker:

| Paper | Biomarker | What was found | What makes it credible or not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [REMAP-CAP](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-05/) | Ferritin | **No responder identification**; highest tercile had lowest probability of benefit | 1,243 patients; a clean negative |
| [REMAP-CAP](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-05/) | suPAR | **Direction reverses** against non-ICU trials | **Only 145 patients**; interval 0.85–6.06 |
| [ACORN + IV study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-05/) | White cell count | Interaction with antibiotic choice | **Replicated in an independent cohort** |
| **sTNFR1 (today)** | **sTNFR1** | **HR 2.25 without diabetes/ASCVD, 1.04 with** | **Specific to one marker and one comorbidity pair; two negative controls** |

**The lesson across the four is that what separates a believable interaction from a subgroup artefact is a
control, and there are two kinds that work: replication in another cohort, and specificity against markers
or modifiers that should not show the effect.** The suPAR finding has neither, which is why it is the
weakest of the four despite being the most striking.

**The mechanism offered is worth taking seriously rather than treating as decoration.** sTNFR1 was **higher
at baseline in patients with diabetes or ASCVD (6,547 vs 4,883 pg/mL) despite similar illness severity**, and
the risk curve **plateaued above about 12,000 pg/mL** in that group. That is the shape you would expect if
chronic inflammatory disease raises the floor and saturates the signal: the marker is still measuring
something, but in a patient whose baseline is already elevated it no longer discriminates acute severity.
**A biomarker can be ruined as a prognostic tool by a comorbidity without ceasing to be biologically real.**

**Two limits.** This is a **secondary analysis of 466 patients** from a trial run for another purpose, and
**diabetes and ASCVD were defined by Charlson comorbidity categories** — an administrative coding
definition, not a clinical phenotyping exercise, which will misclassify in both directions. And **diabetes
and ASCVD were pooled into one exposure**, so whether the effect belongs to one, the other, or their overlap
is not answerable from the abstract.
