## 1. An area under the curve of 0.80, a specificity of 0.49, and a positive predictive value of 3.7%

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **15 October 2026** — **ten days old**, 0 citations. **Systematic
review and meta-analysis** of clinical prediction models for cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury in
adults having cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, searching PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane and Web of
Science. **Random-effects pooling, with calibration and sensitivity analyses.**

> **The pooled incidence of CSA-AKI was 26.3% (95% CI, 26.0-26.4), whereas CSA-AKI requiring renal replacement
> therapy (CSA-AKI RRT) occurred in 2.2% of patients (95% CI, 2.1-2.2). Among validated CPMs, Thakar 2005
> demonstrated the best predictive performance for CSA-AKI RRT: pooled AUC 0.80 (95% CI, 0.77-0.82),
> sensitivity of 0.87, and a specificity of 0.49 at a threshold of ≥ 3 points. Calibration analyses indicated
> underestimation of risk.** The Mehta 2006 and Wijeysundera 2007 models also demonstrated good
> discrimination. **Discrimination for the prediction of milder stages of CSA-AKI was poorer**, but newer
> models such as the Demirjian 2022 model showed promising performance.

**Interpretation.** **The review gives the three numbers needed to work out what the best model actually does
at the bedside, and does not combine them.** **Prevalence of the outcome 2.2%, sensitivity 0.87, specificity
0.49 at the recommended threshold.** **Those produce a positive predictive value of 3.7% and a negative
predictive value of 99.4%.** **So of every hundred patients the Thakar score flags as high risk for needing
renal replacement, about ninety-six will not need it** — and of those it clears, almost none will.

**That makes it a rule-out instrument, and the abstract's framing points the other way.** **"CPMs may identify
high-risk patients enabling targeted preventive interventions"** is a rule-in use, and a 3.7% positive
predictive value will not support one: an intervention applied to everyone the score flags is being applied to
twenty-six patients who do not need it for every one who does. **Used as a rule-out — identifying the large
majority who can safely skip intensive renal surveillance — the same model is genuinely useful.** **An area
under the curve of 0.80 does not distinguish those two uses, which is why quoting it alone is not enough.**

**And the preventive interventions in question are the ones this archive found wanting two days ago.** **[The
haemoadsorption meta-analysis](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-23/) reported on Friday found no reduction in
cardiac surgery-associated kidney injury and, more fundamentally, no change in the cytokines haemoadsorption
exists to remove.** **So today's review offers risk stratification to target preventive interventions, and the
most-studied preventive intervention in this exact setting did not work.** **Risk stratification is only
valuable where something follows from it, which is the second of the three conditions this archive applies to
any measurement — and here the protocol exists while the thing it triggers does not yet have evidence behind
it.**

**Two numbers deserve scepticism and one is the model vintage.** **The three best-validated models are Thakar
2005, Mehta 2006 and Wijeysundera 2007 — nineteen to twenty-one years old** — while the newest model named,
Demirjian 2022, gets only "promising performance". **A field whose best-validated risk tool predates
off-pump's decline, modern blood conservation and current transfusion thresholds is telling you that
validation effort has not followed development effort.**

**The other is the confidence intervals, which are too narrow to be believed as stated.** **A pooled incidence
of 26.3% with an interval of 26.0 to 26.4 is a width of four tenths of a percentage point; the renal
replacement incidence of 2.2% has an interval of one tenth.** **Pooled incidences across heterogeneous cardiac
surgery cohorts do not have that precision unless the heterogeneity was not propagated into the interval.**
This archive has recorded intervals twenty to thirty times too narrow before, and these have the same
signature. **The point estimates are probably about right; the intervals should be read as descriptive rather
than inferential.**

**The honest finding the authors do report, and it matters more than the discrimination: calibration
underestimates risk.** **For a rule-out tool, under-predicting is the dangerous direction** — it is how a
patient gets cleared who should not have been.

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06380-4) · *Critical Care*

## 2. A trial sequential analysis saying the evidence is sufficient, and a GRADE certainty of very low to moderate

**Journal · Published:** *Pain Research & Management*, **indexed 24 October** with a deposited publication date
of **1 January 2026 — a placeholder, flagged rather than repeated as fact.** 0 citations. **Twenty-one
randomised trials, 2,186 participants** — about 104 per trial — of **perioperative transcranial electrical
stimulation** in elective surgery. **Searched to September 2025 with an update search in September 2026.**
**Risk of bias by RoB 2, plus sensitivity, publication-bias, subgroup, meta-regression and outlier analyses,
trial sequential analysis and GRADE.**

> Compared with the control group, the tES group showed **lower pain scores on postoperative day 1
> (SMD = −0.57, 95% CI: −0.84 to −0.30), reduced total opioid consumption (SMD = −0.47, 95% CI: −0.74 to
> −0.20), a lower incidence of postoperative delirium (RR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.79), and a lower incidence
> of postoperative nausea or vomiting (RR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.75 to 0.95).** No significant differences …
> **in postoperative day 1 quality of recovery (SMD = 0.44, 95% CI: −0.18 to 1.05)** … **tES might increase
> the risk of mild and transient stimulation site discomfort (RR = 3.10, 95% CI: 1.96 to 4.90).** …
> **TSA results indicated that the current sample size was sufficient to confirm the beneficial effects of tES
> on postoperative day 1 pain, total opioid consumption, and postoperative delirium. The certainty of evidence
> ranged from very low to moderate.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the most methodologically complete meta-analysis this archive has reported:
risk-of-bias assessment, publication-bias testing, sensitivity analysis, outlier analysis, subgroup analysis,
meta-regression, trial sequential analysis and GRADE, all in one paper.** **And it produces the first trial
sequential analysis in this archive to cross a boundary in the positive direction.** Compare the three
reported in the previous week: oxygen targets after cardiac arrest, trending toward futility; vasopressor
combination therapy, lack of firm evidence; haemoadsorption, 48% of the required information size and neither
boundary crossed. **Today: sufficient, for three separate outcomes.**

**Which makes the tension with the GRADE rating the thing to understand, because the two are not in conflict —
they answer different questions.** **A trial sequential analysis asks whether there is enough data. GRADE asks
how much the data can be trusted.** **You can have ample data from biased studies, and that is what "sufficient
sample size" alongside "very low to moderate certainty" describes.** **The authors' conclusion respects the
distinction — "considering the limited overall quality of current evidence, whether tES can serve as a
universal adjuvant analgesic method still needs further clarification" — and a reader who takes the TSA
sentence alone will get the opposite impression.** **Enough of a possibly-biased estimate is still a
possibly-biased estimate.**

**The blinding question is better here than for most of this week's interventions, and the harm finding
undermines it.** Transcranial stimulation can be sham-controlled, and sham stimulation is standard, so this is
not the music-therapy problem. **But stimulation site discomfort at RR 3.10 (1.96–4.90) is itself an
unblinding mechanism: a patient who feels tingling under the electrode knows which arm they are in.** **And
the outcomes that moved are a self-reported pain score, patient-controlled opioid consumption, and an
observer-rated delirium assessment.** **The one null is quality of recovery — also self-reported, which cuts
against the simple unblinding story and is worth noting rather than explaining away.**

**The delirium result sits directly against a trial this archive reported a week ago, and the relationship
cannot be settled from the abstract.** **On [18 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-18/) this brief
reported MODEST — transcranial electrical stimulation and delirium among postoperative patients, *JAMA Network
Open*, published 3 August 2026 — a trial this archive has since used repeatedly as its standing example of a
subgroup overclaim: six prespecified factors, one crossing at P = .046, on six events against six.** **MODEST
falls inside this review's September 2026 update search window, so it may be among the 21 trials, and the
abstract does not list the included studies.** **If it is included, a meta-analysis reporting RR 0.45 for
delirium contains a trial that was null for delirium; if it is not, a 2026 trial in a major journal was missed
by a search that ran after it published.** **Both possibilities are worth knowing and neither is determinable
from here.**

**Finally, the heterogeneity analysis is the practically useful part and almost no reader will reach it.**
**Initial stimulation timing and the number of sessions explained heterogeneity in day-1 pain; timing,
modality and target site explained it in opioid consumption.** **Translated: when you stimulate, how often,
with which current and where all change the answer** — which means "transcranial electrical stimulation" is
not one intervention, and a pooled standardised mean difference of −0.57 is an average across several
different treatments.

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1155/prm/4293899) · *Pain Research & Management*

## 3. A review that reports a pain benefit and destroys it in the next sentence

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Robotic Surgery*, **24 October 2026** — **one day old**, 0 citations.
**Systematic review and meta-analysis**, searched through 2026, risk of bias by **ROBINS-I V2** and certainty
by GRADE. **Nine non-randomised studies, 813 patients**, uniportal versus multiportal robotic-assisted
thoracic surgery for anatomical pulmonary resection. **Seven of the nine studies rated at critical risk of
bias.**

> **Nine non-randomised studies involving 813 patients were included, with seven studies rated at critical
> risk of bias. Postoperative complications occurred in 16.0% of uniportal and 30.2% of multiportal patients,
> with no statistically significant pooled difference (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.22-1.06; p = 0.069; I² = 66%).** No
> significant differences were identified in major complications, conversion, mortality, operative time, blood
> loss, chest-tube duration, length of stay, or lymph-node yield, or R0 resection. **Uniportal surgery was
> associated with shorter console time (MD −23.5 min, 95% CI −35.6 to −11.4) and lower postoperative day-1
> pain (MD −0.6 points, 95% CI −0.9 to −0.3). The 0.5-point pain difference was below commonly used thresholds
> for clinical importance and lost statistical significance when digitised values were excluded (p = 0.092).**
> … **Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested differences in length of stay and operative time by comparator
> port count, potentially reflecting confounding by operative era and experience.**

**Interpretation.** **The sentence to admire is the one that follows the positive result.** **Having reported a
day-1 pain difference of 0.6 points favouring uniportal surgery, the abstract immediately says the difference
is below thresholds for clinical importance and that it lost significance once figure-digitised data were
removed.** **That is a finding being dismantled by its own authors in the next clause, in the abstract, where
everyone will see it.** **Nothing in this week's reading comes closer to the opposite of a title that overstates
its content.**

**Two technical points about that sentence.** **"Digitised values" means some data were read off published
graphs rather than taken from text or tables — a recognised source of extraction error — and excluding them
moved p from 0.003-or-better to 0.092.** **A result that depends on figure-digitised data is a result that
depends on the reviewers' ruler.** **And there is a small internal inconsistency: the results sentence gives a
mean difference of −0.6 points and the next sentence calls it "the 0.5-point pain difference".** Trivial, and
worth noting because it is the kind of slip that propagates into citations.

**The complication figures are the most interesting thing in the review and the authors are right not to
claim them.** **16.0% against 30.2% is an absolute difference of 14.2 percentage points — a near-halving —
with a risk ratio of 0.49 that does not reach significance at p = 0.069, across 813 patients and nine studies,
with I² of 66%.** **Either a large real effect that nine small non-randomised studies cannot establish, or
exactly what confounding produces.** **The authors name the confounder explicitly: operative era and
experience.** **Uniportal robotic surgery is the newer technique, so its series come from more experienced
surgeons operating later, on cases they selected** — and that is sufficient to generate a fourteen-point
complication difference with no causal contribution from the number of ports.

**Seven of nine studies at critical risk of bias is the limitation that governs everything else**, and
ROBINS-I's "critical" category means the study is too confounded to provide useful evidence on the effect of
interest. **A meta-analysis in which seven of nine contributing studies are in that category is reporting the
shape of a literature, not the effect of a technique**, and the conclusion says so: very-low-certainty
evidence, sensitive to individual studies.

**The one robust-looking finding is a console time reduction of 23.5 minutes, and it is not a patient
outcome.** **Console time is how long the surgeon spends at the robot — a theatre-efficiency and
surgeon-fatigue measure.** **It is worth having and it belongs in a different column from complications and
pain**, and a reader deciding between the two approaches on the strength of this review is deciding on
theatre throughput.

## 4. Delirium and cognitive impairment joined by a slash, at 24 hours, in patients given a sedative

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Anesthesia*, **22 October 2026** — **three days old**, 0 citations.
**Systematic review and meta-analysis. Four studies, 479 patients.** Dexmedetomidine in elderly patients
having general surgery, **administered as a 1 µg/kg loading dose followed by 0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h intraoperative
maintenance.** **Outcome: 24-hour postoperative delirium / early postoperative cognitive impairment.**

> Pooled analysis showed that dexmedetomidine was associated with **significantly lower odds of 24-h
> postoperative delirium/early postoperative cognitive impairment compared with control treatment (pooled OR
> 0.38, 95% CI 0.24-0.61; p < 0.001).** … **Larger, long-term studies are needed to confirm these findings and
> establish standardized dosing protocols.**

**Interpretation.** **The outcome is two different diseases joined by a solidus, and that is the problem
rather than a quibble about wording.** **Postoperative delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of
attention and awareness, screened with instruments like the Confusion Assessment Method. Early postoperative
cognitive impairment is a measured decrement on neuropsychological testing.** **They have different
pathophysiology, different time courses, different measuring instruments and different consequences, and
pooling them means the odds ratio of 0.38 describes a mixture whose composition the abstract does not
state.** **How many of the events were delirium and how many were cognitive impairment is the first thing a
reader needs and it is not there.**

**The second problem is the time point, and it is the week's recurring one.** **Twenty-four hours after
surgery is still largely within the tail of anaesthetic emergence, and the intervention is a sedative.**
**Dexmedetomidine produces a distinctive arousable sedation, and a patient under its residual effect scores
differently on a delirium screen than a patient recovering from a volatile anaesthetic — not necessarily
better or worse, but differently.** **This archive has now met the same structure three times in a week: an
intervention that alters the instrument used to measure its own effect.** **[On 23
October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-23/) it was dexmedetomidine raising recalled caesarean satisfaction
thirteen-fold with quality of recovery unchanged; on the same day it was music therapy moving observer-rated
agitation while every monitored variable stayed flat.** **Here it is a sedative improving a 24-hour delirium
score.** **The honest version of the finding is that dexmedetomidine is associated with better early cognitive
screening scores, and whether that is a brain effect or a sedation effect is exactly what a 24-hour endpoint
cannot separate.**

**Four studies and 479 patients is also a very small slice of a very large literature, and the abstract does
not say so.** **Dexmedetomidine and postoperative delirium has been the subject of many randomised trials and
several large meta-analyses; the narrow inclusion criteria here — elderly, general surgery, a 24-hour
composite — are what reduced it to four studies.** **The interval, 0.24 to 0.61 with an implied standard error
near 0.24, is reasonably tight for that sample, which tells you the event rate was high.** **But a reader
should not take a four-study meta-analysis as an update on a question with dozens of trials, and nothing in
the abstract positions it against them.**

**What is reported well: the dose.** **A 1 µg/kg load followed by 0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h is a specific, usable
regimen, and the authors flag standardised dosing as an open question rather than implying consensus.**
**Dexmedetomidine is now a three-document thread in this archive within a week** — the caesarean trial, a
queued head-and-neck delirium trial, and this — **and the dose has been stated consistently enough across them
to be worth tracking as its own variable.**

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00540-026-03927-1) · *Journal of Anesthesia*
