## 1. A sputum threshold that does not get worse as it gets higher

**Journal · Published:** *Chest*, **3 October 2026** — six days old, **the newest paper here and the last
item on the backlog queue.** Retrospective cohort, **4,821 patients** from a university tertiary airway
clinic linked to **Ontario health administrative databases, 2006–2019.** Negative binomial regression with
generalised estimating equations.
Nair, Zhang, Lauks, Kjarsgaard, Thawanaphong, Zhu, Terebessy, To

> **Results** Of the **4821 patients included, 3714 (77%) had asthma only and 1107 (23%) had COPD only.**
> Among patients with asthma, **sputum eosinophilia (>3%) was associated with higher asthma-specific** health
> services utilisation. Compared with patients with sputum eosinophils <3%, **both the 3–15% and >15%
> eosinophil groups had similarly increased rates of asthma-specific emergency department visits (RR = 1.92,
> 95% CI 1.49–2.47 and RR = 1.88, 95% CI 1.17–3.03).** Conversely, **sputum eosinophil counts were not
> significantly associated with HSU in COPD, whereas high sputum neutrophil counts were associated with
> increased all-cause hospitalizations (RR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.11–2.64).** Overall, those with **higher
> eosinophil (>15%) and lower neutrophil counts (<65%)** had significantly higher rates of asthma-specific
> and asthma-related HSU.

**Interpretation. The two risk ratios in the asthma arm are the finding, and it is the absence of a gradient
that matters.** 3–15% eosinophils gives **RR 1.92** for emergency visits; above 15% gives **RR 1.88** — the
same, with overlapping intervals. **So crossing 3% roughly doubles the emergency-visit rate and going on to
triple or quintuple the eosinophil count adds nothing.**

**That makes this a threshold, not a dose, and the distinction has a practical consequence.** A biomarker
with a dose-response invites titration — treat harder when the number is higher. **A biomarker with a
threshold invites a binary decision, and the number above the threshold carries no further information about
risk.** The wide interval on the >15% group (1.17–3.03, from a smaller stratum) means the two cannot be
statistically separated anyway — **but the point estimates do not even hint at a gradient**, which is the
cleaner reading.

**The COPD arm is a different disease in the same sputum pot, and the paper is careful about it.**
Eosinophils predicted **nothing** in COPD; **neutrophils above the cut-off predicted all-cause
hospitalisation at RR 1.71.** So the same assay, read the same way, points at a different cell and a
different outcome depending on the diagnostic label — and the outcome it predicts in COPD is **all-cause**
rather than disease-specific, which is a weaker claim about mechanism and a stronger one about frailty.

**Three limits worth stating.** **Sputum counts were treated as a fixed baseline exposure** across a window
running to 2019 — airway inflammation is not fixed, and anyone who has done serial counts knows they move
with treatment and season. **The cohort is a tertiary airway clinic**, so this is severe disease referred for
inflammometry, not the asthma population. And **administrative-database outcomes capture utilisation, not
exacerbation**: an emergency visit is a health-system event shaped by access, distance and insurance as much
as by airway inflammation, and in Ontario the socioeconomic adjustment helps but cannot remove that.

**Where it sits in this archive.** This is a **scope stretch and is written as one** — airways rather than
anaesthesia or critical care, carried on the queue since 3 October as its weakest fit, and cleared today
because the window was empty. **But it lands on a thread that is not a stretch at all.** On 6 October this
archive recorded that **biomarkers are reaching guidelines as triage for a test rather than as selection for
a therapy**; on 8 October, that **blood biomarkers appeared in 3 of 16 traumatic brain injury guidelines and
only for CT decision-making.** **Sputum inflammometry is the counter-example: a cheap bedside biomarker that
genuinely does select therapy** — eosinophil-directed steroid and biologic treatment in asthma. **And its own
evidence here says it works as a switch, not a dial.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.09.091) — paywalled beyond the abstract.

## 2. The same idea, moved to cardiac arrest, by deliberately destabilising the blood pressure

**Journal · Published:** *Resuscitation Plus*, **24 July 2026** — two and a half months old. **Open access**
(PMC13471274). **This is a protocol, not a result**: a single-centre, single-arm **phase II
proof-of-concept** trial, **49 comatose out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survivors** enrolled within 12 hours
of return of spontaneous circulation. **ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05679739.** Routed here from
[yesterday's thread](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-08/).
Lim, Woo, Gandhi, Beqiri, Smielewski, Ong, Sharma

> **Introduction** Optimizing cerebral blood flow after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) is key to
> neuroprotection… **Randomized trials comparing fixed mean arterial pressure (MAP) targets have been
> uniformly neutral, as between- and within-patient heterogeneity in cerebral autoregulation renders uniform
> targets inadequate.**
>
> **Methods** … With constant ventilatory settings… **MAP is actively manipulated via vasopressor titration
> to generate slow MAP fluctuations.** Changes in cerebral oxygenation (**near-infrared spectroscopy**) and
> blood flow velocities (**transcranial Doppler**) are continuously correlated with MAP by **ICM+ software**
> to characterize each patient's cerebral autoregulation and **derive an individualized optimal MAP
> (MAPopt). MAP is then actively titrated to MAPopt**, with this **assess-and-titrate cycle repeated at three
> timepoints across 72 h** post-ROSC. **Primary endpoint is the mean change in neuron-specific enolase from
> baseline to 72 h.** Secondary endpoints include… neurological outcome at discharge, and health-related
> quality of life at six months, **compared against historical controls.**

**Interpretation. The first sentence of the introduction is yesterday's finding, written by a different group
about a different disease.** *"Randomized trials comparing fixed MAP targets have been uniformly neutral,
as… heterogeneity in cerebral autoregulation renders uniform targets inadequate."* **Two literatures have
reached the same conclusion about fixed targets from separate trial programmes, and this one states it as
settled background rather than as a claim needing defence.** I have not verified the individual post-arrest
trials behind that sentence and am not naming them from memory; **what is checkable is that randomised
comparisons of higher against lower mean arterial pressure after cardiac arrest do exist** — a 2020
double-blind pilot against a 65 mmHg target, and a 2023 analysis of higher versus lower MAP and kidney
function — **and that this protocol's authors treat the fixed-target question as closed.** The convergence is
the interesting part: **neither field appears to be citing the other's trials.**

**The method is more aggressive than COGiTATE's and that is the thing to notice.** COGiTATE read each
patient's autoregulatory curve from the fluctuations that occur anyway. **This protocol manufactures the
fluctuations: it titrates vasopressors up and down to swing the mean arterial pressure, in a comatose patient
hours after cardiac arrest, in order to find where autoregulation is best.** The scientific logic is sound —
you cannot fit a curve without variation in the predictor — but **the intervention being tested requires
deliberately moving a brain-injured patient's blood pressure away from wherever it currently sits, three
times in 72 hours.** **That is precisely why a single-arm phase II safety and feasibility study is the right
next step**, and it is why the protocol's own framing is feasibility rather than efficacy.

**The endpoint is a surrogate, and the design around it is weak in two ways the protocol states openly.**
The primary outcome is **change in neuron-specific enolase to 72 hours** — a validated prognostic marker
after cardiac arrest, so a defensible surrogate. But **this is a single arm compared against historical
controls**, which is the weakest comparator available: post-arrest care has changed continuously, NSE assays
are not standardised across platforms, and any secular improvement will read as treatment effect. **Set
against the archive's surrogate-endpoint thread, this is the familiar bargain** — a biomarker endpoint buys
a 49-patient study where an outcome endpoint would need thousands — **and the protocol is explicit that its
purpose is to "generate effect-size estimates to inform a definitive multicenter trial."** It should be read
as what it says it is.

**One thing to carry forward about who is doing this work.** **Beqiri and Smielewski are co-authors here, and
both were on COGiTATE and on its 2024 secondary analysis**; the ICM+ software used to derive MAPopt is the
same tool used to derive CPPopt in the traumatic brain injury trials. **The method is travelling with its
inventors from one disease to another.** That is how a technique spreads, and it also means the
cardiac-arrest application is **not an independent replication of the traumatic brain injury work** — it is
the same instrument and partly the same people, pointed at a new population. **Worth one sentence whenever
the two literatures are cited as agreeing.**

[Read the protocol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resplu.2026.101430) — open access.

## 3. In children, being below your optimum is dangerous and being above it is tolerated

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **4 August 2025** — **fourteen months old**, 4 citations, **open
access** (PMC12323160). Secondary analysis of the prospective multicentre **STARSHIP** study: **98 children
aged 1–16**, **10 UK paediatric intensive care units**, 2018–2023, high-frequency physiology and **12-month
GOS-E Peds** outcomes, **none treated with decompressive craniectomy.**
Svedung Wettervik, Smith, Hånell, Bögli, Hutchinson, Agrawal, Smielewski, and the STARSHIP study team

> **Results** There was a transition from favourable to unfavourable outcome **when PRx exceeded +0.00 for
> longer episodes.** Furthermore, there was a transition towards worse outcome **when CPP went below 40 mmHg
> and above 100 mmHg** for sustained durations. **For ΔCPPopt, the transition towards poor prognosis occurred
> for values below −20 mmHg, but positive ΔCPPopt was tolerated.** In the two-variable heatmaps, **PRx above
> +0.50 together with ICP above 20 mmHg, CPP below 60 mmHg, or negative ΔCPPopt were particularly associated
> with unfavourable outcome.**

**Interpretation. The asymmetry is the finding and it is directly actionable.** ΔCPPopt is the distance
between the delivered perfusion pressure and the patient's own autoregulatory optimum. **Being more than 20
mmHg below it predicts poor outcome. Being above it does not.** So the error is not symmetric, and **if you
must miss an individualised target, miss it high.**

**That is a stronger statement than yesterday's adult cohort could make, and it points the same way.** The
187-patient adult study found the tolerable lower limit of perfusion pressure tracking autoregulation — a
patient whose autoregulation has failed needs a higher pressure. **Here, in children, the harm is explicitly
one-sided.** Two independent cohorts, different ages, same direction: **the risk lives below the optimum.**

**The PRx threshold is worth separating from the perfusion thresholds, because it is more striking.** The
transition to unfavourable outcome occurs **when PRx exceeds +0.00** — not +0.25, not +0.30, the values
commonly quoted in adults, but **zero.** PRx is a correlation coefficient between arterial and intracranial
pressure; zero means the vasculature has stopped buffering at all. **In these children, any sustained
positive value was already on the wrong side of the line**, which is a tighter tolerance than adult practice
assumes.

**And the absolute pressure window is wide at both ends: worse outcomes below 40 and above 100 mmHg.** A
60-mmHg span is not a target, it is a cliff edge on either side — **which is exactly the argument against
absolute pressure targeting that this archive reframed on [3 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-03/)**,
now with paediatric numbers attached. The useful target is the individual one; the absolute numbers mark
where everyone does badly.

**Two limits, one of them a strength in disguise.** **98 patients across 10 units over five years is a thin
cohort for heatmap analysis**, and the paper calls itself exploratory and visualisation-based rather than
inferential. **But the exclusion of children treated with decompressive craniectomy is deliberate and
clarifying** — yesterday's adult study found decompressive craniectomy patients tolerated low perfusion
pressure *worse*, so removing them removes a known effect modifier rather than hiding one. **The honest
summary is a set of thresholds generated for prospective testing, which is what the conclusion asks for.**

**The thread as it now stands, across five documents and three populations.**

| Population | Document | What it adds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult TBI, 16 guidelines | Scoping review (1 Oct 2026) | **Pressure targets are the first thing guidelines disagree about** |
| Adult TBI, 187 patients | <em>Critical Care</em> cohort (20 Apr 2026) | Tolerable lower limit **tracks autoregulation** |
| Adult TBI, 60 patients | **COGiTATE** (16 Aug 2021) + its 2024 reanalysis | Individualising is **feasible and safe**; the mechanism works; **no outcome trial in five years** |
| **Paediatric TBI, 98 children** | **STARSHIP secondary analysis (today)** | **Harm is one-sided: below the optimum, not above** |
| **Post-cardiac-arrest, 49 planned** | **neuro-intact protocol (today)** | The same reasoning, **arrived at independently**, now in a protocol |

**What the table shows is a field with a consistent mechanism, consistent observational support across two
ages and two diseases, one feasibility trial, and nothing powered for outcome anywhere.**

*The index-growth anomaly is resolved, and the resolution is that it was the wrong measurement. **This watch
spent four days on whole-database growth falling to roughly a third of a ~7,900/day baseline, and declared it
a finding yesterday with the caveat that the baseline window might have contained a backfill. It did, and
that is the whole explanation.** The fourteen-day lookback promised yesterday was run today: **25 September
to 9 October holds 43,217 records, about 2,880 a day**, which matches the daily whole-database differences of
the past four days (+2,089, +3,873, +2,754, +3,190 — mean 2,976). **But the baseline window tells the
story**: 27 September to 5 October now holds **31,154 newly-dated records, about 3,460 a day, while whole-
database growth across those same nine days was 63,180 — nearly double.** **So in that window about half the
database's growth was records dated outside it, and today almost none is. **Stated plainly: the deposit rate
for current literature has not fallen. The retrospective backfill stream stopped, and comparing a
backfill-inclusive baseline against a backfill-free present produced a four-day false alarm.** The metric is
changed accordingly: **from today the record carries the trailing fourteen-day bucket sum**, which measures
newly-dated deposits only, and the whole-database difference is kept beside it as the second number rather
than the first. **The daily buckets, the canaries and the no-bucket-decreased check were right throughout and
are what should have been trusted.***
