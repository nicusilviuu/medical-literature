**Items 1 to 3 are one argument spanning four years, and it ends somewhere non-obvious.**
Videolaryngoscopy demonstrably works: it reduces failed intubation and hypoxaemia across 222 randomised
trials. One of the outcomes used to show that it works is **improved glottic view.** And two prospective
studies — one published today in 5,302 adults, one in 2024 in 904 paediatric intubations — find that
**the glottic view obtained under videolaryngoscopy does not tell you whether the tube will go in.**

Item 4 is the carried-over paediatric airway paper from last week, which belongs in the same entry: it
is about the other decision at induction, which device to use at all.

## 1. Glottic view versus tube delivery — 5,302 intubations, 44 hospitals

**Journal · Published:** *Anaesthesia*, **2 October 2026** — **published today.** Prospective multicentre
observational study, VCISpain Trial Investigators Group.
Fernández-Vaquero, Charco-Mora, Gómez-Ríos, Sastre, de Luis-Cabezón, Greif, Vives

> **A good view of the glottis with videolaryngoscopy does not translate consistently into
> straightforward tracheal tube delivery, suggesting a dissociation between visualisation and procedural
> execution.** When videolaryngoscopy documentation is based on laryngeal view alone, it does not reliably
> reflect the performance of tracheal intubation. We evaluated this relationship and the reproducibility
> of a structured classification framework…
>
> We conducted a prospective multicentre observational study across **44 hospitals.** Videolaryngoscopic
> tracheal intubations were assessed independently by an airway operator and an observer using a
> **three-domain classification: blade geometry; glottic view using the percentage of glottic opening
> (POGO) score; and tracheal tube delivery.**…
>
> A total of **5302 tracheal intubations** with paired assessments were analysed. The overall inter-rater
> agreement for the three-domain classification was high (**Krippendorff's α = 0.86, 95%CI 0.85–0.87**).
> Among tracheal intubations with **poor view (POGO < 25%), 37/151 (24.5%) were classified as easy.**
> Among tracheal intubations with POGO ≥ 25%, **527/5151 (10.2%) were classified as difficult or failed.
> Even with excellent glottic view (POGO > 75%), the initial strategy was classified as difficult or
> failed in 252/4025 (6.3%) of cases.**
>
> **The ease of tracheal intubation with videolaryngoscopy cannot be inferred from glottic view alone.** A
> structured approach that incorporates **what was used, what was seen and what was done** may improve
> communication in clinical practice.

**Interpretation.** The dissociation runs in both directions, which is what makes this more than a
measurement-error story. **A quarter of the worst views were easy intubations. One in sixteen of the best
views was difficult or failed.** And the number that is easiest to miss: of the 527 difficult-or-failed
intubations among adequate views, **252 — nearly half — occurred with an excellent view (POGO > 75%).**
So the single largest group of difficult videolaryngoscopic intubations is the group where the operator
could see the cords perfectly well.

**This answers a question this archive posed five weeks ago.** On 28 August the brief reported
[*Anaesthesia*'s systematic review of the tools used to classify and document videolaryngoscopic
intubation](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-28/), which assessed their accuracy and inter-rater
reliability. **Today's paper is a prospective multicentre validation of exactly the kind of framework that
review was looking for** — three domains instead of one, with a Krippendorff's α of 0.86 across 44
hospitals, which is a serious reliability figure for a bedside classification scored by two people in
real time. **The archive has now reported the problem statement and the candidate answer five weeks
apart**, and that is the third time in a fortnight a thread here has closed that way.

**The clinical content of the recommendation is modest and worth stating plainly rather than inflating.**
This is an observational study; it does not show that using the three-domain classification improves any
patient outcome. What it supports is **documentation and handover** — "what was used, what was seen and
what was done" — and the argument is that recording a POGO score alone, or a Cormack-Lehane grade alone,
tells the next anaesthetist something that does not predict what they need to know. **That is a claim
about communication, not about technique**, and the paper says so.

**Where it sits in this archive's longest-running thread.** The 19 September brief grouped papers under
[monitors that measure something real without helping
anyone](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-19/). Glottic view is a near-perfect instance and an unusually
pure one: **it is not a noisy proxy for intubation difficulty, it is a different thing that happens to be
visible.** The camera resolves the anatomy question and leaves the mechanical one — tube through cords,
around a hyperangulated blade, into a trachea at an angle the operator cannot change — untouched.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.70417)

## 2. The same finding, two years earlier, in children — and the sharper version of it

**Journal · Published:** *Anaesthesia*, **11 July 2024** — **twenty-seven months old**, 20 citations.
Prospective observational study within a structured universal videolaryngoscopy implementation programme.
Sasu, Gutsche, Kramer, Röher, Zeidler, Peters, Köhl, Krause, Zöllner, Dohrmann, Petzoldt

> We aimed to determine if universal, first-choice videolaryngoscopy reduces the incidence of restricted
> glottic views and to determine **the diagnostic performance of the Cormack and Lehane classification to
> discriminate between easy and difficult videolaryngoscopic tracheal intubations in children.**…
>
> There were **904 tracheal intubations in 809 children** over a 16-month period. First attempt and overall
> success occurred in 607 (67%) and 903 (> 99%)… Difficult videolaryngoscopic tracheal intubation occurred
> in 47 (5%)… **Direct glottic view during laryngoscopy was restricted in 117 (13%) and the
> videolaryngoscopic view in 32 (4%) (p < 0.001). Videolaryngoscopy improved the glottic view in 57/69
> (83%) tracheal intubations where the vocal cords were only just visible, and in 44/48 (92%) where the
> vocal cords were not visible by direct view.**
>
> **The Cormack and Lehane classification discriminated poorly between easy and difficult
> videolaryngoscopic tracheal intubations with a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic
> curve of 0.68 (95%CI 0.59–0.78) for the videolaryngoscopic view compared with 0.80 (95%CI 0.73–0.87) for
> the direct glottic view during laryngoscopy (p = 0.005).**
>
> …**The Cormack and Lehane classification was not a useful tool for grading videolaryngoscopic tracheal
> intubation in children.**

**Interpretation.** Included because it reached the same conclusion as today's paper two years earlier, in
a different age group, with a different grading system — and because **it contains the sharper version of
the finding, which today's paper does not report.**

Read the two AUROC figures together: **0.68 for the videolaryngoscopic view against 0.80 for the direct
view, in the same patients, p = 0.005.** The direct laryngoscopic view predicted difficulty *better* than
the videolaryngoscopic view did. So videolaryngoscopy did two things at once in this cohort: **it improved
the view — restricted views fell from 13% to 4% — and it degraded the view's diagnostic value.**

**That is not a paradox, and the mechanism is almost arithmetic.** If a camera around the corner shows you
the cords in nearly everybody, then "I can see the cords" stops separating the easy cases from the hard
ones, because it no longer separates anybody from anybody. The information was in the *failure* to see,
and the device removed the failure. **A test loses discrimination when the thing it tests for stops
varying** — and 92% of previously invisible cords became visible.

**Two consequences follow, and the second is the uncomfortable one.** First, carrying Cormack-Lehane
across from direct laryngoscopy to videolaryngoscopy is not a conservative choice; the grade means
something different and measures less. Second, **the same logic applies to the POGO score in today's adult
study** — POGO is also a visualisation measure, and today's paper shows it too fails to separate easy
from difficult. **Neither paper tests a view measure that works.** What today's study proposes instead is
not a better view score but the addition of two non-view domains.

One limit on both papers: **"difficult" is an operator-assessed judgement in each**, not a hard outcome
like hypoxaemia or failure. Both are single-system or multicentre observational designs measuring a
classification against a rating. And this one is **a single-centre implementation programme using one
device family (C-MAC)**, with 67% first-attempt success — a figure worth noticing in its own right, since
overall success exceeded 99%.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16366)

## 3. The evidence base whose surrogate is now in question

**Journal · Published:** *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, **9 July 2022** — **just over four years old**,
**105 citations**, in PubMed Central. A condensation of the Cochrane review published **4 April 2022**
(10.1002/14651858.cd011136.pub3, now **127 citations**), whose fuller abstract was also retrieved for this
entry and is quoted where it gives detail the condensation omits.
Hansel, Rogers, Lewis, Cook, Smith

> A total of **222 RCTs (with 26 149 participants)** were included. **Most studies had unclear risk of bias
> in at least one domain, and all were at high risk of performance and detection bias.** We found that
> videolaryngoscopes of any design likely **reduce rates of failed intubation (Macintosh-style: RR = 0.41,
> 95% CI 0.26–0.65; hyperangulated: RR = 0.51, 0.34–0.76; channelled: RR = 0.43, 0.30–0.61;
> moderate-certainty evidence)** with increased rates of successful intubation on first attempt **and
> better glottic views** across patient groups and settings. **Hyperangulated designs are likely favourable
> in terms of reducing the rate of oesophageal intubation**, and result in improved rates of successful
> intubation in individuals presenting with difficult airway features (P = 0.03).

From the Cochrane version, the glottic-view and harm detail:

> …probably **improve glottic view when assessed as Cormack-Lehane grade 3 and 4 (RR 0.38, 95% CI 0.29 to
> 0.48)** [Macintosh-style]… **(RR 0.15, 0.10–0.24)** [hyperangulated]… **(RR 0.14, 0.09–0.21)**
> [channelled]… Macintosh-style and channelled VLs likely reduce rates of **hypoxaemic events**
> (RR 0.72, 0.52–0.99 and RR 0.25, 0.12–0.50)… **We were unsure of the findings for dental trauma because
> the certainty of this evidence was very low**… **We were not able to pool data for time required for
> tracheal intubation owing to considerable heterogeneity (I² = 96%, 99%, 98%).**

**Interpretation.** This is the document that settled the question, and the point of putting it third is
that **items 1 and 2 do not overturn any of it.**

Look at what the review actually established with moderate certainty: **fewer failed intubations, and for
two device classes fewer hypoxaemic events.** Those are hard outcomes. They are not in dispute, and
nothing in today's paper touches them. **What items 1 and 2 undermine is one of the review's supporting
outcomes — improved glottic view — as a thing worth measuring.** And the review's own numbers show how
central that outcome was: the effect on Cormack-Lehane grade 3/4 views is the largest in the whole
analysis, **RR 0.14 to 0.38 depending on blade design**, far larger than the effect on failed intubation.
The view improved dramatically; the failures improved modestly.

**That is the opposite resolution from the surrogate story in [yesterday's
brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-01/)**, and the contrast is instructive. There, volatile sedation
in ARDS improved three surrogates and then killed more patients. Here, videolaryngoscopy improved a
surrogate enormously and the real outcomes improved too — just much less, and apparently not *because* of
the surrogate. **A surrogate can be misleading by pointing the wrong way, or by pointing the right way for
the wrong reason and at the wrong magnitude.** The second failure mode is less dramatic and probably more
common.

**Two caveats on the review itself, both stated by its authors.** **All 222 trials were at high risk of
performance and detection bias** — unavoidable, since you cannot blind an operator to which laryngoscope
they are holding, and the outcome assessors are usually the operators. And intubation time **could not be
pooled at all (I² of 96–99%)**, which is a striking admission in a review that pooled everything else.

**Where this leaves the universal-videolaryngoscopy question**, which this archive has been tracking: on
[21 September the guidelines watch reported a SEDAR/SEMES/FLAME multi-society push for universal
videolaryngoscopy that said its own evidence was weak](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/),
alongside DAS 2025. **Items 1 and 2 are not an argument against universal videolaryngoscopy** — the
Cochrane outcomes support it. They are an argument that **the documentation and teaching built around it
need rebuilding**, because the grade everyone was trained to record is the part that stopped meaning what
it meant.

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2022.05.027)

## 4. Supraglottic airway versus tracheal intubation at induction in children with twitchy airways

**Journal · Published:** *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, **29 September 2026** — three days old.
Multicentre retrospective cohort, 12 tertiary institutions, Japan Paediatric Difficult Airway in
Anaesthesia (J-PEDIA) registry, July 2022 – February 2025.
Uchinami, Soejima, Nishida, Yamauchi, Watanabe, Ichiyanagi, Kaiho, Kojima

> Children with airway hyperresponsiveness face higher risks of perioperative respiratory adverse
> events… evidence remains limited regarding **induction-phase** airway management… We aimed to investigate
> the association between **SAD placement (vs tracheal intubation)** and respiratory adverse events during
> induction-phase airway management… using **propensity score-based inverse probability of treatment
> weighting.**
>
> We included children aged <18 yr undergoing general anaesthesia who had **an active upper respiratory
> tract infection, an upper respiratory tract infection within the preceding 14 days, asthma
> exacerbation, or household smoking exposure.** Of the **27 844 encounters, 4878 were analysed**
> (tracheal intubation 3005 [61.6%]; SAD 1873 [38.4%]). After adjustment, SAD placement was associated
> with a **lower risk of respiratory adverse events (adjusted risk ratio 0.41; 95% CI 0.19–0.62;
> P<0.001)**, airway management-related adverse events (**aRR 0.32; 0.19–0.45**), and **severe oxygen
> desaturation (aRR 0.47; 0.19–0.75)**…
>
> …**These findings support consideration of SADs when clinically appropriate while acknowledging potential
> residual confounding.**

**Interpretation.** Reported here after being carried on the outstanding list for three runs, which is
longer than it should have been for a paper this directly in scope.

**The effect sizes are large and that is the problem as much as the point.** An adjusted risk ratio of
0.41 for respiratory adverse events, 0.32 for airway-management events, and 0.47 for severe desaturation —
a 53–68% relative reduction from choosing a device — is bigger than most interventions in paediatric
anaesthesia achieve. **Device choice in these children is not randomly assigned**, and the direction of
confounding is predictable: an anaesthetist who looks at a wheezy four-year-old and judges them safe for
a supraglottic airway is making a clinical judgement that the propensity model can only partly capture.
**The healthier children get the SAD.** The authors name residual confounding themselves rather than
leaving it to a reader, and the honest reading is that **the direction is probably right and the magnitude
is probably inflated.**

Two features of the denominator are worth stating. **4,878 of 27,844 encounters were analysed** — 17.5% —
because the cohort is restricted to children with active or recent upper respiratory infection, asthma
exacerbation or household smoking exposure. And **household smoking exposure sits in that list alongside
active infection**, which is a much milder exposure; the composite "airway hyperresponsiveness" population
is heterogeneous, and the abstract does not report the effect by subgroup.

**Note for future runs, because two papers from this registry have been confused here before.** This is
**not** the J-PEDIA paper that has sat on the outstanding list since 9 September — that one is an
*Anesthesia & Analgesia* analysis of **extreme weight-for-age** and airway adverse events at induction,
which still has no abstract deposited. **Two different papers, one registry, both about induction-phase
airway events in children.** Only this one has been reported.

It belongs in this entry rather than a separate one because items 1–3 are about **what you can tell from
looking** once you have decided to intubate, and this is about **whether to intubate at all.** The
[SFAR airway recommendations reported on 30 September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) put
human factors and organisational dimensions in a third of their document; device selection in the twitchy
paediatric airway is the kind of decision that framework exists to support.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2026.07.046)
