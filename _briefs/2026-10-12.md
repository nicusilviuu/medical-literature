## 1. A quality-of-life trial whose quality-of-life effect is a mortality effect

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 September 2026** — six weeks old, 1 citation. **Stepped-wedge cluster
randomised trial in the ICUs of 20 public hospitals in Brazil**, enrolment June 2024 to May 2025.
**NCT06343545.**
Rosa, Moraes, Trott, Dos Santos, Miozzo, Andrade, de Souza, Wanderley, Mocellin, de Almeida, Dos Santos,
De Matos, De Carli, Caserta and colleagues

> **Interventions** A multicomponent telehealth-based rehabilitation program integrating **an ICU
> intervention focused on ventilator liberation; a ward intervention targeting risk stratification and
> individualized rehabilitation plans; and a postdischarge 2-month personalized centralized telerehabilitation
> program.**
>
> **Results** Among **1916 enrolled patients** … **1063 assigned to the intervention and 853 to usual care.**
> At 90 days after hospital discharge, mean EQ-5D-3L utility scores were higher in the intervention group
> (**0.16 [0.31] vs 0.12 [0.28]; adjusted difference, 0.049; 95% CI, 0.0002 to 0.098; P = .04**) **but did not
> differ among survivors (0.60 [0.32] vs 0.59 [0.32]; adjusted difference, −0.045; 95% CI, −0.138 to 0.045;
> P = .34).** Compared with usual care, the intervention resulted in **lower 90-day all-cause mortality
> (71.8% [676 of 941] vs 78.3% [584 of 746]; adjusted difference, −7.6%; 95% CI, −14.7% to −0.6%; P = .03)**
> and shorter mechanical ventilation duration (**9.9 [10.3] vs 15.5 [15.9] days**; adjusted difference,
> −6.2 days; P < .001).
>
> **Conclusions** … **improved 90-day health-related quality of life, potentially influenced by reduced
> mortality.**

**Interpretation. The EQ-5D utility scale assigns death a score of zero, so a trial that reduces mortality
raises the mean utility of its cohort without improving anyone's health.** That is exactly what the two
reported analyses show: **the whole-cohort difference is positive and barely significant (0.049, lower bound
0.0002), and the survivor-only difference is negative and null (−0.045).** **The quality-of-life finding is a
mortality finding in a quality-of-life wrapper**, and the authors say so in their own conclusion —
*"potentially influenced by reduced mortality."* **Read the survivor analysis first: among people alive at
90 days, this programme did nothing measurable.**

**Which makes the mortality result the real claim, and it needs scrutiny the abstract makes possible.**
**Ninety-day all-cause mortality of 78.3% in the control arm is extraordinarily high** for acute hypoxaemic
respiratory failure on invasive ventilation — far above what trials in this population usually report —
**and the absolute reduction of 7.6 points from a rehabilitation programme is correspondingly large.** Two
things follow. **First, the population is not transferable**: these are Brazilian public-hospital ICUs with
a case mix and baseline mortality unlike most settings where this intervention would be considered.
**Second, a stepped-wedge design attributes to the intervention whatever changed over calendar time**, and a
7.6-point mortality fall is the kind of effect that secular improvement, co-intervention or the Hawthorne
response produces as readily as telerehabilitation does.

**And the mortality denominators are not the randomised arms.** The trial randomised **1,063 and 853**; the
mortality analysis uses **941 and 746** — **122 and 107 patients missing, 229 in total, unexplained in the
abstract.** On the outcome that carries the entire result, that is the first thing to look for in the full
paper. **This archive has now found mismatched denominators in four trials in six days**, in *Chest*,
*Critical Care* twice, and now *JAMA*.

**What is solid, and it is not trivial: ventilation duration fell from 15.5 to 9.9 days**, a 6.2-day adjusted
reduction with a tight interval, and the ICU component of the intervention **targeted ventilator liberation
specifically.** That is a coherent mechanism acting on the outcome closest to it. **If the mortality effect is
real, that is the plausible route to it** — and a trial designed around 90-day utility was not the best
instrument for demonstrating it.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.10617) — paywalled beyond the abstract.

## 2. Fourteen hours, estimated with impossible precision

**Journal · Published:** *Pharmacotherapy*, **1 September 2026** — six weeks old, 1 citation, **in PubMed
Central** (PMC13501059). Systematic review and meta-analysis, **librarian-led search of eight sources
through July 2024 with updates to December 2025**, ROBINS-I. **CRD42024595819.**
Rizwan, Brown, Barry, Guppy, Gerberi, Gajic, Herasevich

> **Sixteen nonrandomized studies** were included in the qualitative synthesis, and **12 treatment-oriented
> studies** in the primary meta-analysis. …
>
> **Phenobarbital-based pathways were not associated with significantly different intubation (11 studies; OR
> 0.62; 95% CI, 0.20–1.87; I² = 73%) or hospital length of stay (five studies; MD −1.75 days; 95% CI, −5.07
> to 1.56; I² = 63%)** … **ICU length of stay was shorter with phenobarbital-based pathways (eight studies;
> MD −0.60 days; 95% CI, −0.79 to −0.41; I² = 0%).** In exploratory subgroup analyses, **front-loaded,
> protocolized phenobarbital-first pathways** were associated with lower intubation and shorter hospital
> stay, **but certainty of evidence was very low.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.**

**Interpretation. The only significant result is 0.6 of an ICU day — about fourteen hours — and it is
reported with zero heterogeneity across eight observational studies, which is the detail to stop on.** An
I² of 0% means the eight estimates are statistically indistinguishable from one another. **ICU length of stay
is one of the most locally determined outcomes in critical care** — it depends on bed pressure, step-down
availability, discharge rounds and weekend staffing — **and eight different ICUs agreeing to within noise on
a 14-hour difference is more surprising than the difference itself.** The likeliest explanations are a narrow
set of similar centres or outcome definitions that truncate variation; either way, **a precise pooled
estimate from heterogeneous settings with I² = 0 deserves suspicion rather than confidence.**

**Meanwhile the outcomes that matter are null and heterogeneous.** **Intubation: OR 0.62 with an interval
from 0.20 to 1.87 and I² = 73%** — which is to say unknown. Hospital length of stay: null, I² = 63%.
**So the clinically meaningful endpoint is undetermined and the trivial one is precise**, and the review is
explicit that all 16 studies are non-randomised.

**The interesting contribution is not an effect estimate at all, and the authors frame it correctly.**
*"This review distinguishes phenobarbital-first, protocolized ICU pathways from adjunctive or mixed
phenobarbital strategies, suggesting that implementation design may influence outcomes."* **The claim is that
the drug is not the intervention — the pathway is**, and the subgroup where benefit appears is the
front-loaded, protocolised one. **At very low certainty and in exploratory analyses, which they say twice.**

**That lands exactly on a thread this archive has been building.** The [4 October
brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-04/) reported a scoping review of analgosedation protocols in which
**the most frequently reported barrier was a protocol insufficiently detailed to cover the breadth of patient
needs**, and this fortnight has produced four guidelines whose stated rationale was implementation rather
than new evidence. **Here is the same proposition in a drug comparison: the same agent gives different results
depending on whether it is deployed as a protocol or as a rescue**, and nobody has tested that
prospectively. **The review's own recommendation — prospective randomised confirmation — is the right one,
and what it would have to randomise is the pathway, not the drug.**

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1002/phar.70197) — in PubMed Central.
