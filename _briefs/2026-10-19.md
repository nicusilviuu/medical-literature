**And a correction against yesterday's entry, which matters because I presented it as an instrument
definition.** I wrote that a past `FIRST_IDATE` bucket "is complete by construction and cannot grow", and used
that to discard a near-finding. **The first half is right and the second is wrong: 16 October has fallen from
37,260 to 37,243, and 17 October from 3,650 to 3,645** — both stable on two reads today. **A past index-date
bucket cannot grow, and it can shrink, because records are removed from the database.** So it is an **upper
bound on what was indexed that day, not a count of it.** The near-finding I discarded stays discarded — a
stable bucket still proves nothing — but the reason I gave was wrong by half, and anyone using these series
needs the corrected version.

**The decrements are the other new thing, and they are small and routine.** `PMC` lost 440 records and `NBK`
one, on a day when `MED` gained 8,223. **Every previous decrement this archive has seen was an event — 61,746,
then 80,022.** This is the first observation of ordinary churn: **the per-source counts are not monotonic even
on a normal ingest day**, and a loss of a few hundred is background rather than signal. **That raises the
threshold at which a decrement is worth reporting, and the threshold was previously "any".**

Both reference windows grew — 1–30 September by **1,414** to 156,973, and 1–7 October by **861** to 36,694 —
which is the indexing frontier filling in behind, exactly as it should.

## 1. High-flow oxygen after thoracoscopic lung resection: a positive trial its authors will not generalise

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **20 August 2026** — **two months old**, 0 citations.
**Single-centre, open-label randomised trial**, **210 patients** in the modified intention-to-treat analysis
(**HFNC 104, conventional oxygen 106**) after **VATS lobectomy or segmentectomy for stage I–IIIa non-small-cell
lung cancer.** **Primary outcome: severity of postoperative pulmonary complications during hospitalisation**,
by ordinal logistic regression.

> **HFNC reduced PPC severity (ordinal OR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.28-0.91; P = 0.022) and moderate-to-severe PPCs
> (23.1% vs. 39.6%; absolute risk difference, −15.6%; 95% CI, −28.0% to −3.2%; P = 0.010).** … **No differences
> were observed in physiological outcomes or length of stay.**
>
> **Conclusion** … **prophylactic HFNC reduced in-hospital PPC burden** … **These findings suggest that
> prophylactic HFNC may provide benefit as a potential preventive respiratory support option in selected older
> or higher-risk postoperative patients, although routine prophylactic use in all patients undergoing lung
> resection is not supported by the present findings.**

**Interpretation.** **This is a positive trial whose authors decline to generalise it, and the restraint is
the right call for a reason the abstract makes visible.** An ordinal odds ratio of 0.50 on complication
severity, and moderate-to-severe complications falling from about 40% to about 23%, is a large effect in a
population where pulmonary complications are the dominant morbidity after lung resection. **And length of stay
did not move, nor did any physiological outcome.** A complication-severity benefit with no effect on how long
anyone stayed in hospital is an odd pair — it either means the complications prevented were the ones that do
not extend admission, or it means a single-centre trial of 210 patients cannot resolve length of stay. **The
authors' refusal to recommend routine use rests on exactly that kind of gap, and "selected older or
higher-risk" is where the honest claim sits.**

**Three stated numbers do not reconcile, and the archive's habit of dividing percentages back out found it.**
**23.1% is 24 of 104 and 39.6% is 42 of 106** — no other integers round there. **Those rates differ by 16.5
percentage points. The stated absolute risk difference is 15.6**, with a confidence interval symmetric about
it. **And 15.6 is exactly 39.6 minus 24.0.** So either the HFNC rate is 24.0% (25 of 104) and "23.1%" is the
slip, or the rate is 23.1% and the difference should read 16.5. **One of the three figures is wrong by about a
point and I cannot tell which from the abstract.** It does not change the direction or the significance —
the interval excludes zero either way — **but a reader quoting "a 15.6 point reduction from 23.1% to 39.6%"
would be quoting three numbers that cannot all be true.**

**Scope note on the comparator, which is the real limitation.** This is **prophylactic** high-flow oxygen
against **conventional** oxygen in extubated postoperative patients — not against non-invasive ventilation,
and not as rescue. **It says nothing about the question in the archive's queue**, which is high-flow versus
non-invasive ventilation to prevent reintubation in patients already in difficulty. **Open-label is
unavoidable here and matters more than usual**, because complication grading in an unblinded trial of a
visible respiratory device is a judgement made by people who can see the allocation.

## 2. Lidocaine and rebound pain: the same structure as yesterday's trial, handled the opposite way

**Journal · Published:** *Drug Design, Development and Therapy*, **5 October 2026** — **two weeks old**,
0 citations. **Single-centre randomised placebo-controlled phase II trial, 120 adults**, interscalene
brachial plexus block plus general anaesthesia for **arthroscopic rotator cuff repair**, allocated 1:1 to
saline or **lidocaine 1 mg/kg loading then 2 mg/kg/h from incision to closure.** **Primary outcome: rebound
pain within 24 hours.**

> **Rebound pain occurred in 14/60 participants (23.3%) with lidocaine and 22/60 (36.7%) with saline [risk
> difference −13.3%, 95% CI −28.8% to 3.1%; relative risk 0.64, 95% CI 0.36–1.12; P = 0.163].** Median peak
> movement-evoked NRS scores were **5 in both groups** (P = 0.136). **The mean intraoperative remifentanil
> infusion rate was lower with lidocaine (mean difference −81.67 μg/h, 95% CI −101.47 to −61.86; P < 0.001).**
> Baseline-adjusted UCLA Shoulder Scores were higher and CSI-9 scores lower with lidocaine (both P < 0.001);
> **these outcomes were exploratory.** **Although 60 participants per group were enrolled, a corrected post hoc
> calculation indicated that approximately 82 evaluable participants per group were required for the
> prespecified effect.**
>
> **Conclusion This trial did not establish a statistically significant reduction in 24-hour rebound pain** …
> **Because the study was potentially underpowered, a clinically meaningful effect cannot be excluded.** …
> **Larger adequately powered trials are needed before lidocaine can be recommended to prevent rebound pain.**

**Interpretation.** **Hold this report next to [MODEST](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-18/), reported here
yesterday, because the structure is identical and the handling is opposite.** Both are modest single-centre
randomised trials; both have a null primary outcome; both have positive secondary findings. **MODEST's
conclusion led with a subgroup interaction at P = .046 and the phrase "suggested a benefit". This one leads
with "did not establish a statistically significant reduction", discloses a corrected post-hoc power
calculation showing it needed 82 per group and enrolled 60, labels its positive secondaries "exploratory", and
ends by refusing to recommend the drug.** **Same evidential situation, two reporting choices**, and the
difference is editorial rather than statistical.

**The disclosure about power is the part worth copying.** "A **corrected** post hoc calculation indicated that
approximately 82 evaluable participants per group were required" says, in one sentence, that the original
sample-size calculation was wrong and that the trial was built too small. **Authors almost never say this.**
It also makes the null honest rather than reassuring: a risk difference of −13.3 points with an interval from
−28.8 to +3.1 is entirely compatible with a worthwhile effect, and the paper says so.

**The one firm positive is intraoperative and mechanistic: remifentanil consumption fell by about 82 μg/h**,
with a tight interval and P < 0.001. **That is a real pharmacological signal and it is not the outcome anyone
cares about** — it tells you the lidocaine was doing something, not that the patient benefited. **The shoulder
scores and the central-sensitisation inventory are flagged exploratory by the authors and should be quoted that
way or not at all.**

**For the rebound-pain pair this archive now holds, the comparison is informative in a way neither paper is
alone.** The [dexamethasone substudy](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-15/) reported on 15 October found
rebound pain nearly halved — **35.7% to 18.4%, RR 0.51 (0.30–0.88)**. Lidocaine here: **36.7% to 23.3%,
RR 0.64 (0.36–1.12)**. **The placebo rates are almost identical — 35.7% and 36.7% — despite different
definitions of rebound pain, different blocks and different operations**, which is a quiet piece of external
validation for the phenomenon itself. **The point estimates differ by less than the intervals**, so the honest
summary is that dexamethasone has the evidence and lidocaine has the same direction without the sample size.

## 3. Remimazolam woke them faster and kept them in recovery longer, because the protocol said so

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of International Medical Research*, **12 October 2026** — **one week old**,
0 citations. **Prospective, single-blind randomised trial, 86 patients** having short gynaecological surgery:
**propofol plus remifentanil (n = 43) or remimazolam plus remifentanil (n = 43)**, with **sugammadex 2 mg/kg
at the end in both arms and 200 μg flumazenil added in the remimazolam arm.**

> **Eye-opening and extubation times were significantly shorter in the remimazolam group (p < .001). However,
> the postanesthetic care unit stay times were significantly longer in this group due to institutional safety
> protocols (p < .001).** No statistical differences were found in the **Quality of Recovery-15K scores or
> other long-term recovery profiles at 4 h or 1 day** postoperatively.
>
> **Conclusions Remimazolam-flumazenil significantly improved immediate recovery; however, its effects on the
> broader recovery trajectory were not significantly different from those of propofol** …

**Interpretation.** **This is the cleanest example this archive has found of a pharmacological gain cancelled
by an institutional rule, and the authors name the mechanism in the result sentence.** Remimazolam with
flumazenil reversal produced faster eye-opening and faster extubation, at P < 0.001. **And patients then spent
longer in recovery, also at P < 0.001, "due to institutional safety protocols"** — almost certainly a mandated
observation period after flumazenil, because flumazenil is short-acting relative to the benzodiazepine it
reverses and re-sedation is the recognised hazard. **So the drug's advantage was real, was measured, and was
spent on a policy written to contain the drug's other property.**

**That is worth separating into two claims, because they have different lifetimes.** The pharmacology — faster
emergence with remimazolam plus flumazenil than with propofol — **is a property of the drugs and will hold
anywhere.** The nullification — longer PACU stay — **is a property of one hospital's protocol and will not.**
**A unit without a mandatory post-flumazenil observation period would bank the emergence benefit; a unit with
one cannot.** The trial measures both and reports them together, which is right, but anyone generalising from
it needs to know which half travels.

**And the broader recovery result is the one that should govern practice: nothing differed at four hours or one
day.** Quality of Recovery-15K was **numerically higher** with remimazolam and **not significantly so** — the
authors say exactly that, including the word "numerically", which is the correct way to report a non-significant
trend. **Eighty-six patients is small**, single-blind rather than double, and short gynaecological surgery is
the population where emergence differences are most visible and matter least.

**For the archive's remimazolam thread this is the fifth document and the first to test reversal.** The thread
holds a trial-sequential meta-analysis against sevoflurane, a nausea trial, the bispectral-index crossover that
showed the monitor reads differently at matched depth, and the
[ASGE clinical practice update](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-17/) published two days ago. **The open
question remains the one the ASGE abstract did not answer** — whether society guidance addresses
depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring under a drug that recalibrates it. **This trial does not touch that**, but it
adds the practical finding that the drug's advertised advantage can be consumed by the safety protocol its
reversal agent requires.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1177/03000605261495755) · KCT0008314

## 4. Bivalirudin against heparin on 407 patients, and an interval presented as reassurance

**Journal · Published:** *Annals of Cardiac Anaesthesia*, **12 October 2026** — **one week old**,
0 citations. **Systematic review and meta-analysis: three randomised trials, 407 patients**, bivalirudin
versus unfractionated heparin for anticoagulation in adult cardiac surgery.

> **There was no significant difference in the primary outcome; reexploration for hemorrhage between groups
> (risk ratio: 1.38, 95% confidence interval: 0.42 to 4.48).** There were no observable differences in any of
> the secondary outcomes; **thromboembolic events (myocardial infarction, stroke, pulmonary embolism),
> mortality, or postoperative transfusion requirements** … **All studies included were assessed to have some
> concern for risk of bias. Interstudy heterogeneity was low across all comparisons.**
>
> **Conclusion Bivalirudin has not been shown to have any significant difference to UFH on clinical outcomes**
> … **and as per current international guidelines, can be considered for safe use in those patients for whom
> UFH is contraindicated.** The study is limited by the bias of the included studies and the variability of
> bivalirudin drug dosage and its therapeutic monitoring; **further, well-powered RCTs are required.**

**Interpretation.** **The primary result is an absence of evidence and the middle of the conclusion reads like
evidence of absence.** A risk ratio of **1.38 with an interval from 0.42 to 4.48** on reexploration for
haemorrhage spans **a 58% reduction to a four-and-a-half-fold increase.** From three trials and 407 patients,
in a comparison where the outcome of interest is surgical bleeding, **that interval excludes essentially
nothing.** The same applies to the secondary outcomes: "no observable differences" in mortality and
thromboembolism across 407 patients is a statement about the sample size.

**To be fair to the authors, the conclusion does three things and only the middle one overreaches.** It opens
with the accurate "has not been shown to have any significant difference", and it closes by naming the
limitations — risk of bias in all three trials, variable dosing, variable therapeutic monitoring — and calling
for well-powered trials. **The problem is the clause between them: "can be considered for safe use in those
patients for whom UFH is contraindicated", attributed to "current international guidelines".** **That
recommendation is not derived from this meta-analysis; it is imported into it.** The guidelines may well say
so, and bivalirudin is the established answer for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. **But the sentence reads
as though 407 patients supported it, and they do not.**

**One methodological detail cuts against over-reading the reassurance further.** **"Interstudy heterogeneity
was low across all comparisons"** is offered as a strength and, with three small trials, is closer to
uninformative — this archive noted on 11 October that near-zero heterogeneity is also what you get when trials
carry too little information to disagree. **Three trials cannot demonstrate consistency.**

**What the paper is genuinely useful for is as a map of the evidence base: three randomised trials, 407
patients, all with risk-of-bias concerns, and no dosing or monitoring standard across them.** For a drug used
when heparin is contraindicated in cardiac surgery — a situation nobody can randomise around — **that is the
real finding, and it is a finding about how little there is.**
