## 1. The title says the combination improves outcome; the paper's own trial sequential analysis says there is no firm evidence

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Intensive Care Medicine*, **19 October 2026** — **three days old**,
0 citations. **Systematic review and meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis**, searched to
**27 September 2026**. **Twenty-one randomised trials, 3,873 adults** with septic shock, **norepinephrine plus
a vasopressin receptor agonist versus norepinephrine alone.**

> The results showed that the combination … **was associated with lower mortality at the longest complete
> follow-up compared with norepinephrine alone (RR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.99, P = .02), but with an increased
> risk of digital ischemia (RR = 2.21, 95% CI 1.15 to 4.27, P = .02). Subgroup analysis indicated that the
> combination … may provide survival benefits for more severe septic shock patients (RR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.81 to
> 1.00, P = .04).** Moreover, compared with norepinephrine alone, the combination of norepinephrine plus
> vasopressin **showed a trend towards reducing the longest follow-up mortality (RR = 0.89, 95% CI 0.80 to
> 1.00, P = .05). TSA indicated lack of firm evidence for a beneficial effect.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **may reduce mortality** … **(low quality of evidence), but may increase risk of digital
> ischemia (low quality of evidence).**

**Interpretation.** **The title is "Vasopressors Combination Therapy … Improve Septic Shock Outcome". The
abstract's final result sentence is "TSA indicated lack of firm evidence for a beneficial effect." These are
the same document.** **This archive has catalogued titles that overstate, conclusions that overstate and
subgroups that overstate; it has not before found a title that asserts in the indicative what the paper's own
pre-planned sequential analysis explicitly denies.** **The conclusion section is honest — "may reduce", "low
quality of evidence", twice — so the overstatement is confined to the one line that gets indexed, cited in
passing and read by everyone who never opens the paper.**

**The four effect estimates make the point without any need for interpretation: upper bounds of 0.99, 1.00,
1.00, and for digital ischaemia 4.27.** Mortality overall RR 0.90 (0.83–0.99). Severe subgroup 0.90
(0.81–1.00). Norepinephrine plus vasopressin specifically 0.89 (0.80–1.00). **Three of the four mortality
estimates touch unity, and a trial sequential analysis exists precisely to tell a reader that a pooled
estimate sitting on the boundary with the information size unmet is not yet a finding. It was run, it said so,
and the title ignored it.**

**The subgroup claim is weaker than it looks and the numbers give it away.** **"May provide survival benefits
for more severe septic shock patients (RR 0.90)" — the overall estimate is also RR 0.90.** **The point estimate
is identical; what differs is only that the subgroup interval happens to close at 1.00 rather than 0.99.**
**So there is no evidence here of a larger effect in sicker patients — only the same effect, measured in a
smaller group, with an interval that crosses differently.** **Reporting that as a subgroup benefit is the same
error this archive recorded in MODEST and in the oxygen-target review's hypothermia stratum, in a form that is
easier to catch because the two point estimates are printed side by side.**

**The harm is the most precisely useful thing in the abstract and it is the part a clinician can act on.**
**Digital ischaemia RR 2.21 (1.15–4.27)** — a doubling, with a wide interval but excluding unity. **Against a
mortality relative risk of 0.90 whose own TSA is unconvinced, a doubling of digital ischaemia is the firmer
finding of the two.** **Neither is given as an absolute risk, which is the omission that matters: without
event rates a reader cannot set fingers against lives, and that trade-off is the entire clinical question.**

**"Mortality at the longest complete follow-up" also deserves a flag.** It is a defensible choice and it is
also a choice among several time points, made by the reviewers rather than the trials. **An endpoint defined
as "the longest available" is not a prespecified time; it is whatever each trial happened to report furthest
out**, and pooling across heterogeneous horizons inflates apparent precision.

**Context from this archive, with the distinction stated precisely.** **Yesterday's brief reported a post hoc
analysis of VANISH, which randomised early vasopressin *instead of* norepinephrine** — a replacement strategy.
**Today's meta-analysis pools trials of vasopressin *added to* norepinephrine.** **The two questions are
adjacent and not the same, and VANISH's own finding of no interaction with the vasopressor arm does not
transfer to the add-on question.** On [10 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-10/) this archive also
noted a phase II multimodal vasopressor signal and said a physiological signal was not a basis for a strategy
claim. **Three vasopressin documents in two weeks, and the firmest thing in any of them is a finger
complication.**

## 2. "GRADE-Assessed" is in the title, and not one certainty rating appears in the abstract

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Intensive Care Medicine*, **19 October 2026** — **three days old**,
0 citations, **same journal and same week as item 1**. **Systematic review and meta-analysis**, searched to
**1 May 2026** (PROSPERO CRD420261391864). **Six randomised trials, 9,743 children** under 18 with sepsis or
septic shock, **balanced salt solution versus normal saline.** Primary outcome: **hospital or 30-day
mortality.**

> …the certainty of evidence was assessed using **Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and
> Evaluations (GRADE) for all outcomes.**
>
> **Six RCTs involving 9743 participants were included. For the primary outcome, BSS and NS demonstrated no
> significant differences in hospital or 30-day mortality rates (3.5% vs 3.7%; RR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.81-1.16,
> P = .72). Secondary analyses revealed that BSS significantly reduced the requirement for renal replacement
> therapy (RRT) (RR: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.47-0.97) and hyperchloremia (RR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.57-0.75).**
>
> **Conclusion** … **no significant difference in short-term mortality** … **however, BSS provided significant
> clinical and physiological advantages, including reduced incidences of hyperchloremia and the need for RRT.**

**Interpretation.** **The title announces that the review is GRADE-assessed, the methods confirm certainty was
rated for all outcomes, and the results and conclusion contain no certainty rating for any outcome.** **For
three days running this archive's guidelines watch has reported documents that describe their grading framework
and withhold the result; here is the same failure in a meta-analysis that puts the framework in its own
title.** **The ratings exist — someone did the work — and a reader of the abstract cannot tell whether the
renal-replacement finding is high certainty or very low.** That single word would change how the result should
be used.

**On the substance, this is a well-shaped null with two secondaries of unequal weight, and the difference
between them is the thing to get right.** **Mortality: 3.5% against 3.7% in 9,743 children, RR 0.97
(0.81–1.16).** The interval bounds the absolute difference between about **0.7 fewer and 0.6 more deaths per
hundred**, so a clinically important mortality effect in either direction is excluded at this size. **That is
a useful null, not an absence of evidence.**

**Hyperchloraemia, RR 0.65 (0.57–0.75), is the tightest estimate in the paper** — an implied standard error
near 0.07 on the log scale — **and it is also the least interesting, because balanced solutions contain less
chloride.** **Demonstrating that giving less chloride produces less hyperchloraemia is a check that the
intervention was delivered, not a patient outcome.**

**Renal replacement therapy, RR 0.68 (0.47–0.97), is the finding that matters and it is the fragile one.**
**The upper bound is 0.97 — it clears unity by three hundredths — and the implied standard error of 0.18 is
two and a half times that of the chloride estimate**, which is what a rarer outcome in the same cohort looks
like. **Starting renal replacement in a child is a hard, operator-independent endpoint and a 32% relative
reduction would be worth having.** **But a secondary outcome in a review whose primary is null, resting on an
interval that barely excludes no effect, with no event rate given and no certainty rating published, is a
hypothesis.** **The conclusion's word "significant" is carrying both the chloride result and the
renal-replacement result, and they are not the same kind of claim.**

**One structural note in the review's favour: it is a paediatric question answered with 9,743 randomised
children.** **Yesterday's guidelines watch reported a French-speaking paediatric guideline that could grade
five of twenty-one recommendations because the trials do not exist. In fluid choice they do.** **Where a
paediatric question has been randomised at scale, the contrast with the rest of paediatric critical care is
worth noticing.**

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/08850666261498038) · PROSPERO CRD420261391864

## 3. Measurement-guided meropenem dosing, recommended on evidence in which two of twelve studies acted on the measurement

**Journal · Published:** *The Annals of Pharmacotherapy*, **19 October 2026** — **three days old**,
0 citations. **Systematic review**, searched to **15 August 2026**, risk of bias by RoB 2 or ROBINS-I.
**Thirteen reports representing 12 unique studies (2 randomised, 10 non-randomised)** reporting meropenem
concentrations during **continuous renal replacement therapy**.

> **Total clearance ranged from 3.60 to 6.6 L/h. Lower or primary PK/PD target non-attainment ranged from 0% to
> 26.7%, while aggressive-target non-attainment ranged from 0% to 53.3% in most analyses and reached 93% in 1
> study when a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 8 mg/L was assumed.** Continuous or prolonged
> infusion may facilitate sustained exposure, **but comparative evidence was limited. Four used routine TDM;
> only 2 implemented protocolized TDM-guided dose adjustment. Low exposure was associated with higher
> mortality in 1 adjusted observational analysis, but evidence for clinical outcomes was of very low
> certainty.** … **Conclusions Available evidence supports a shift from standardized empirical dosing toward
> individualized, measurement-guided meropenem therapy in patients receiving CRRT.**

**Interpretation.** **The descriptive finding is solid and clinically important: a standard meropenem dose does
not produce a predictable concentration in a patient on continuous renal replacement.** **Total clearance
spans 3.60 to 6.6 litres per hour — a 1.8-fold range — and that is before the MIC of the organism enters the
calculation.** **At an assumed MIC of 8 mg/L, 93% of patients in one study missed the aggressive target.**
**A clinician giving one gram eight-hourly to a patient on haemofiltration does not know what exposure the
patient is getting, and this review establishes that with twelve studies.**

**The recommendation, however, outruns the evidence in a specific and nameable way, and the review's own
numbers show where.** **Only 4 of 12 studies used routine therapeutic drug monitoring, and only 2 implemented
a protocol that changed the dose in response to it.** **So of twelve studies, ten measured nothing or measured
without acting.** **Set against the three conditions this archive applies to a monitored variable: the
measurement is certainly closer to the pathology than the dose (condition 1, met); a protocol converting the
number into an action existed in two of twelve studies (condition 2, met in one sixth of the evidence base);
and the clinical outcome rests on one adjusted observational analysis that the authors themselves rate very
low certainty (condition 3, nominally met and evidentially thin).**

**This is the inverse of the trial this archive reported yesterday and the comparison is the useful part.**
**The [electrical impedance tomography trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-21/) randomised the
measurement, protocolised the response and had an adjudication team score the outcome.** **Here the
measurement is better justified physiologically — a drug concentration is not a surrogate for exposure, it is
exposure — and the protocol and the outcome are almost absent.** **The authors are candid about it: "may
support individualized dosing when multidisciplinary expertise is available" is a conditional recommendation
about service capability, not a claim about patients.**

**And it closes a loop that this archive has been running for three days.** On 20 October the guidelines watch
reported the [joint ADQI and ELSO statements](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-20/) on running renal
replacement during ECMO, which delivered a research agenda alongside its recommendations. On 21 October it
reported the [Chinese 34-recommendation renal replacement
guideline](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-21/), which took positions on filters, fluids and dose with
no grade distribution at all. **Today's review covers what those two documents leave out: whether the
antibiotic given through that circuit reaches a therapeutic concentration. The answer is that nobody reliably
knows, because almost nobody measures it.** **A guideline that specifies the membrane and the replacement
fluid and says nothing about β-lactam exposure has specified the easier half.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1177/10600280261489958) · *The Annals of Pharmacotherapy*

## 4. Bystander CPR up nine points, and the effect does not survive modelling the time trend

**Journal · Published:** *The Lancet Public Health*, **online ahead of a 1 November 2026 issue date** — stated
plainly rather than blurred. 0 citations. **Stepped-wedge, cluster-randomised controlled trial in 15 French
EMS agencies** (NCT03633370), recruiting **27 August 2018 to 20 February 2022**. Intervention: **dispatcher
training for cardiac-arrest recognition and telephone-assisted CPR, geolocation-app activation of trained
volunteers, and structured audit and feedback.** Primary outcome: **bystander-initiated CPR before EMS
arrival.**

> **Of the 15 EMS agencies initially randomised, two withdrew before recruitment began and were replaced, and
> one enrolled no patients.** … **14 EMS agencies recruited a total of 2160 patients. The intention-to-treat
> analysis included 1684 patients (686 control, 998 intervention) enrolled across the 12 originally randomised
> EMS agencies.** … **Bystander-initiated CPR before EMS arrival occurred in 421 (61·4%) patients in the
> control group and 708 (70·9%) patients in the intervention group (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 1·54 [95% CI
> 1·09-2·19]; p=0·017). Estimates were consistent across six prespecified sensitivity analyses (OR range,
> 1·38-1·55), remaining significant in four, but not after adjustment for cluster-specific secular trends
> (1·38 [0·98-1·94]) or baseline characteristics (1·39 [0·97-1·98]).**
>
> **Interpretation** … **although the estimate was sensitive to the modelling of secular trends, and it did
> not translate into improved clinical outcomes.**

**Interpretation.** **Start with the credit, because it is unusual: the trial's own Interpretation section
names the two things that undermine its headline.** **The estimate is sensitive to secular-trend modelling,
and the process gain did not become a clinical one.** **Most trials put that in a limitations paragraph; this
one puts it in the sentence that gets quoted.**

**The secular-trend problem is not a technicality in this design — it is the design's central vulnerability.**
**In a stepped-wedge trial every cluster crosses from control to intervention in the same direction, so the
intervention is confounded with calendar time by construction.** **The analysis that separates them is
precisely the one adjusting for cluster-specific secular trends, and that analysis gives OR 1.38 with an
interval of 0.98 to 1.94.** **So the honest reading is that bystander CPR rose by nine percentage points over
three and a half years in these services, and the trial cannot cleanly attribute that rise to the
intervention.**

**And the calendar in question is the worst one available for this outcome.** **Recruitment ran from August
2018 to February 2022.** **Bystander CPR behaviour, dispatcher protocols and public willingness to put hands
on a stranger's chest all changed substantially during 2020 and 2021, in both directions and at different
times in different regions.** **The abstract does not mention the pandemic.** **For a design whose validity
turns entirely on whether the time trend is modelled correctly, a trial spanning 2020 should say what it did
about it**, and that is the first thing to look for in the full text.

**The attrition is large and its reason is creditable.** **2,160 patients were recruited and 1,684 (78.0%)
entered the intention-to-treat analysis** — **476 patients, 22%, dropped because they came from the two
agencies that replaced withdrawals.** **Restricting to the 12 originally randomised clusters is the right
call for randomisation integrity and it costs a fifth of the data.** The arms are also unequal — **998
intervention against 686 control, a ratio of 1.45** — which a stepped-wedge produces by design, since clusters
accumulate more person-time after crossover than before.

**On the clinical point, this belongs with the monitoring thread and it is the same shape as closed-loop
oxygen.** **Condition 2 is met emphatically: a scripted protocol, recognition targeted within 60 seconds of
call pick-up and first compression within a further 60.** **Condition 3 is met: bystander CPR before EMS
arrival is observed independently of the dispatcher.** **And the patient outcome did not move** — the trial
says so itself. **That is the oxygen pattern again: the process measure improved and the thing the process
exists to change did not.** **It is also the strongest version of the pattern yet, because bystander CPR is
not a surrogate invented for a trial; it is the link in the chain of survival that observational data most
consistently associates with living.** **Raising it by nine points and seeing no outcome change is a result
worth sitting with rather than explaining away** — and the most likely explanation, that the trial was far too
small to detect a survival difference, is one the abstract does not quantify.
