## A past bucket grew by 835, and the bound is available again

**Correction 14, published on Sunday, rested on a bucket that grew by three records.** Bookshelf gained
three, all three carried 14 November, and the 14 November bucket rose by three — which overturned the
standing rule that a past index-date bucket cannot grow.

**Today the 17 November bucket grew from 4,889 to 5,724. That is 835 records — two hundred and
seventy-eight times the observation the correction was built on.**

| Bucket | 17 November | 18 November |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 17 November | 4,889 | **5,724 (+835)** |
| 16 November | 2,103 | 2,103 |
| 15 November | 6,089 | 6,089 |
| 14 November | 8,217 | 8,215 (−2) |
| 18 November (today) | — | 3,036 |

**A rule overturned by three records and then confirmed by 835 is in a different position from one
overturned by three alone.** **Correction 14 replaced "a past bucket cannot grow" with "a past bucket is a
reading at an instant, which can rise or fall", and today's reading does not test that statement so much as
settle it.** **It also closes the loose end from yesterday, when the 14 November bucket moved without
Bookshelf moving and this archive could only say the mechanism explained two of three movements: a bucket
plainly moves for reasons beyond any one source, and at a scale where chasing the source of each movement is
not the right use of a daily run.**

**Every source moved upward today, which makes the subtraction bound available for the first time since
13 November.** Whole **49,269,968 → 49,273,782, up 3,814**: `MED` +2,530, `PPR` +1,235, `PMC` +49, every
other source flat, **summing to 3,814 exactly with a residual of zero.** **With no shrinking source, gross
additions equal the net gain, so at most 3,814 records are new to the index. Today's bucket holds 3,036 and
yesterday's grew by 835, which is 3,871 between them — so at least 57 records in those two buckets are
redated rather than new.** By subtraction, with its precondition met and checked.

**Two run conditions worth recording.** **The index was slow: the opening reading, fifteen sequential
queries, took about eight minutes against well under two on every previous day, and two command batches had
to be moved to the background to finish.** Nothing failed and no retry was triggered — the API was simply
slower. **And the Europe PMC helpers were moved out of `/tmp` into the repository**, beside yesterday's
classifier, with a short README recording what each does and why they live there. **Yesterday's entry wrote
that an instrument needed on a dated action must not depend on a scratch directory surviving; the helpers had
survived twenty-four days by luck, and waiting for a container to make the point would have been the wrong
way to learn it.** Windows: September unchanged, **1–7 October down one after four static days**, October
month down 39.

## 1. Intensive care for sepsis that needs no organ support: no benefit overall, benefit in two subgroups

**[Association of Care Intensity With Survival and Long-Term Cost-Effectiveness in Sepsis Without Initial
Organ Support After Emergency Admission](https://doi.org/10.1097/CCM.0000000000007435)** · *Critical Care
Medicine* · **published 13 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Retrospective cohort from a 140-hospital Japanese database, April 2016 to March 2024. Adults admitted by
emergency ambulance with community-acquired sepsis who needed no extracorporeal support, ventilation,
catecholamines or renal replacement before or on the day antimicrobials started.** 28,211 patients — general
ward 18,349, high-dependency unit 8,706, intensive care 1,156, **summing exactly.** Overlap propensity-score
weighting; cost-effectiveness as three-year cost per quality-adjusted life year.

> ICU and HDU admissions were not associated with lower mortality overall (HDU vs. GW; RR, 0.99; 95% CI,
> 0.92-1.06 and ICU vs. GW; RR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.92-1.22). **However, HDU admission was associated with lower
> mortality among patients with Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score greater than or equal to 6
> (RR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.75-0.99) or Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score less than or equal to 12 (RR, 0.81; 95% CI,
> 0.71-0.92).** … ICU and HDU admissions were not cost-effective overall (ICERs > 200,000 USD/QALY). However,
> the HDU subgroup with GCS score less than or equal to 12 (ICER, 47,738 USD/QALY; 95% CI, 45,918-49,558) was
> cost-effective under both thresholds…

**The overall nulls are clean and useful: 0.99 reconstructing to p = 0.78, and 1.06 to p = 0.42.** **For a
patient with sepsis who arrives needing no organ support, this is 28,211 patients saying that a higher level
of care does not change whether they survive.** That is a finding worth having in a system where the bed is
scarce.

**The subgroups are where the reading needs care, and the paper is better than most at signposting them.**
**High-dependency care was associated with lower mortality at a SOFA score of 6 or more (0.86, upper bound
exactly 0.99, p = 0.033) and at a Glasgow Coma Scale of 12 or less (0.81, p = 0.0014).** **The second is the
firmer of the two and the more plausible: a septic patient who is confused needs watching, and watching is
what a high-dependency unit is.** **The SOFA subgroup is the weaker claim — an upper bound of exactly 0.99
is the least informative significant result available, and no interaction test is reported for either
subgroup**, so this archive's standing rule applies: a difference in significance between strata is not a
significant difference.

**Now the cost-effectiveness ratios, which cannot be right as reported.** **The incremental
cost-effectiveness ratio for the Glasgow-Coma subgroup is 47,738 US dollars per quality-adjusted life year
with a confidence interval of 45,918 to 49,558 — a half-width of 1,820 dollars, or ±3.8%. The SOFA subgroup
is ±6.0%.** **An incremental cost-effectiveness ratio is a ratio of two uncertain quantities, built here on
three-year costs and modelled quality-adjusted survival, and a ±4% interval implies essentially no
uncertainty in the utility weights, the survival extrapolation, or the cost attribution.** **Cost-effectiveness
analyses conventionally report wide and often unbounded intervals for exactly this reason, and a ratio that
straddles a decision threshold is usually reported as a probability of being cost-effective rather than as a
point with tight bounds.** **The clinical conclusion may well stand; the precision claimed for the economics
does not, and a reader should treat 47,738 as an estimate and not as a measurement.**

**My reading.** **Admit the confused septic patient to a high-dependency bed and do not admit the alert one
with a low SOFA score to intensive care on the grounds that sepsis is serious: 28,211 patients say the
second changes nothing.** **The cost-effectiveness arithmetic is directionally useful and falsely precise**,
and the subgroup findings need the interaction tests the abstract does not report.

## 2. A review of three reviews, a vote count, and a conclusion in the title

**[A systematic review of albumin and septic shock: It is not a question of if but a question of
when](https://doi.org/10.1097/JXX.0000000000001390)** · *Journal of the American Association of Nurse
Practitioners* · **published 16 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Joanna Briggs Institute methodology. PubMed, Embase and CINAHL searched from 2014 to 2026 with the terms
"albumin AND fluid resuscitation AND sepsis AND crystalloid".** The result: **three systematic reviews with
meta-analyses were included.**

> **Two of the three meta-analyses demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in 90-day mortality
> among septic shock patients resuscitated with albumin compared with crystalloid.** Hyperoncotic albumin
> (20% or 25%) may provide additional benefit when administered at an appropriate time during resuscitation.
> **Evidence suggests that early administration of hyperoncotic albumin following an initial 30mL/kg balanced
> crystalloid bolus may improve mortality outcomes in patients with septic shock.** Implications for practice
> Incorporating measures of microvascular and endothelial changes may help guide the selective use of
> hyperoncotic 20% albumin following initial crystalloid resuscitation…

**The method is vote counting, which is the practice meta-analysis exists to replace.** **"Two of the three
meta-analyses demonstrated a statistically significant reduction" is a tally of p-values across three
overlapping reviews of a largely shared trial literature** — so the three are not three independent pieces
of evidence, and counting how many crossed 0.05 discards the effect sizes, the precision and the overlap all
at once. **A review of reviews can be a legitimate design when the question is how the reviews disagree; it
is not a way to add their conclusions together.**

**And the recommendation is considerably stronger than the evidence described.** **"Early administration of
hyperoncotic albumin following an initial 30 mL/kg balanced crystalloid bolus may improve mortality
outcomes" is a protocol — a concentration, a sequence and a trigger volume — derived from three reviews, two
of which were significant, with no pooled estimate anywhere in the abstract.** **The title goes further
still: "It is not a question of if but a question of when" asserts that albumin works and reduces the open
question to timing.** **Nothing in the abstract establishes the first half of that sentence.**

**The one part this archive will credit is the framing of the mechanism.** **The observation that updated
sepsis guidance no longer excludes albumin from initial resuscitation is correct, and the suggestion that
microvascular and endothelial measures might identify who benefits is the right shape of hypothesis** — it
is what a phenotype argument looks like, and it echoes the immunoglobulin consensus this archive reported
yesterday, which endorsed a therapy where a measurable deficiency existed and declined to where there was
only inflammation.

**My reading.** **Treat this as a position paper rather than as evidence.** **The underlying question is
genuinely open and genuinely important — hyperoncotic albumin after crystalloid, timed to endothelial
failure, is a reasonable thing to want to test — and three vote-counted reviews cannot support a
milligram-and-millilitre recommendation.** **A reader who wants the albumin evidence should read the three
meta-analyses, which this paper names but does not pool.**

## 3. Epidural first, then spinal: no more general anaesthetics, nearly twice the hypotension

**[Association between prior epidural labour analgesia and increased incidence of hypotension after spinal
anaesthesia for caesarean section](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X261472513)** · *Anaesthesia and Intensive
Care* · **published 17 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Retrospective propensity-score-matched cohort, caesarean deliveries April 2016 to March 2020. 161 patients
matched in each group: women who had epidural labour analgesia and then received a standard-dose spinal,
against women who received a spinal without a previous epidural. Primary outcome: conversion to general
anaesthesia.**

> …general anaesthesia conversion rates were comparable (3.3% vs 2.5%, P = 0.544); all conversions resulted
> from block failure. **However, the epidural labour analgesia-to-spinal anaesthesia group experienced
> significantly more frequent hypotension (55.9% vs 30.4%, P < 0.001) and required higher doses of ephedrine
> (mean 8.3, standard deviation 9.8 mg vs mean 5.9, standard deviation 11.1 mg, P = 0.036).**

**The primary outcome is null and uninformative, and the secondary outcome is large.** **Conversion to
general anaesthesia was 3.3% against 2.5% — about five events against four on 161 patients each — so the
comparison this study was designed to make rests on nine events and could not have detected anything.**
**The hypotension difference is 25.5 percentage points, 90 patients against 49, and p below 0.001.**
**The title leads with the secondary finding, which is the honest choice given the numbers, and it means the
paper's useful result is not the one it set out to measure.**

**The ephedrine comparison should not be read as printed.** **The means are 8.3 and 5.9 mg and the standard
deviations are 9.8 and 11.1 — so the standard deviation exceeds the mean in both groups, by 18% and 88%.**
**On a quantity that cannot go below zero, that means the distributions are heavily right-skewed: most women
received little or none and a few received a great deal.** **A comparison of means at p = 0.036 on
distributions of that shape is fragile, and a median with an interquartile range would have said more.**
**The direction is consistent with the hypotension finding and the magnitude should not be quoted.**

**Interpretation.** **The clinically useful statement is the one the authors make: a standard spinal dose
after epidural labour analgesia is feasible — it did not raise block failure or high block when preventive
strategies were used — and it costs more hypotension.** **The mechanism is unsurprising and worth saying
out loud: residual epidural local anaesthetic extends the block achieved by the spinal, so the same
intrathecal dose produces a higher sympathetic block in a woman who already has one.** **The practical
consequence is not a smaller dose, which is what previous practice reached for and which risks failure; it
is anticipating the vasopressor requirement.**

**Two limits.** **Retrospective and single-centre over four years, with propensity matching on measured
characteristics only** — and the decision to site an epidural in labour is not random. **And "hypotension"
is a threshold-defined outcome whose definition the abstract does not give**, which this archive has spent
the fortnight noting is where comparability goes.

## 4. Staying ventilated after burn surgery, and the number that survives the obvious explanation

**[Perioperative factors associated with postoperative continuation of mechanical ventilation after initial
burn surgery](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00540-026-03960-0)** · *Journal of Anesthesia* · **published
17 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Retrospective cohort of patients having acute-phase burn debridement between 2018 and 2024. 418 patients,
of whom 75 (17.9%) required postoperative ventilation rather than extubation in the operating room.
Multivariable logistic regression.**

> Age (aOR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00-1.06), percentage of total body surface area (%TBSA) burned (aOR = 1.08, 95%
> CI 1.03-1.12), **preoperative intubation (aOR = 11.8, 95% CI 3.02-46.4)**, intraoperative fluid
> administration volume (ml/kg) (aOR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.01-1.06), and **intraoperative maximum
> vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) (aOR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.10-1.26)** were independently associated with
> postoperative MV continuation. **This association between the intraoperative maximum VIS and postoperative
> MV continuation was maintained in the sensitivity analysis excluding preoperatively intubated patients
> (aOR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.08-1.25).**

**Two of the five associations are close to tautological and should be set aside.** **Preoperative intubation
with an odds ratio of 11.8 is largely a statement that a patient who arrives intubated tends to leave
intubated** — and its interval, 3.02 to 46.4, spans fifteen-fold, which is what happens when a predictor is
nearly deterministic in a subgroup. **Age at 1.03 has a lower bound of exactly 1.00 and reconstructs to
p = 0.047**, the least informative significant result available.

**The finding is the vasoactive-inotropic score, and the reason is the sensitivity analysis.** **A maximum
intraoperative score of vasoactive and inotropic support carried an odds ratio of 1.18 per point, and when
the preoperatively intubated patients were removed it was 1.16 — essentially unchanged.** **So the
haemodynamic signal is not an artefact of the sickest patients already being intubated**, which is the
obvious objection and the one the authors tested. **That is the right sensitivity analysis, reported with
its number rather than described.**

**What the score adds is a quantity the rest of the model does not capture.** **Burn severity is conventionally
read from the percentage of body surface burned, and that appears here with an odds ratio of 1.08 per
percentage point.** **The vasoactive-inotropic score measures something different: how much pharmacological
support the circulation needed while the surgery was happening**, which is a within-operation measure of
physiological reserve rather than a pre-operation measure of injury size. **The authors say exactly this and
it is the paper's contribution.**

**Limits worth naming.** **Single centre, retrospective, 75 events across five predictors** — which is 15
events per variable, at the lower end of what a logistic model tolerates. **And the outcome is a decision
rather than an event: "continuation of mechanical ventilation" is what the anaesthetist chose**, so the
model predicts clinical judgement as much as physiology, and in a unit with a different extubation culture
the coefficients would differ.

## 5. A protocol for the question yesterday's meta-analysis could not answer

**[Effect of neuromuscular blockade reversal on early postoperative pulmonary function after thoracoscopic
lung resection](https://doi.org/10.1080/07853890.2026.2763165)** · *Annals of Medicine* · **published
16 November 2026** · **this is a trial PROTOCOL and reports no results**

**Reported as a protocol and flagged as one.** Multicentre, double-blind, 240 patients planned, randomised
1:1 to sugammadex 2 mg/kg or neostigmine 0.03 mg/kg with atropine, after unilateral thoracoscopic resection
of one segment or less. **Primary outcome: the percentage fall in forced expiratory volume in one second at
one hour after surgery, against the preoperative baseline.** Registered as NCT07309393.

**It is in this brief because of what it is aimed at.** **Yesterday's brief carried a meta-analysis of eight
trials of sugammadex against neostigmine in older adults, which found less residual blockade and 8.84 fewer
minutes in recovery, and whose two patient-relevant secondary findings — hypoxaemia and hospital stay — did
not survive the authors' own Hartung-Knapp adjustment.** **This protocol proposes to measure the respiratory
mechanism directly instead: not whether a train-of-four ratio recovered, but how much lung function the
patient has an hour after the operation.**

**The design choice to credit is the primary outcome.** **A percentage fall in one-second forced expiratory
volume at one hour is objective, measured in the patient, and plausibly on the path between residual
blockade and pulmonary complications** — which is more than can be said for residual blockade itself, the
endpoint the existing literature rests on. **And the authors are explicit that this is mechanistic: the
discussion says the findings "may inform the design of future confirmatory trials with patient-centered
outcomes", which is an unusually honest statement that 240 patients and a spirometry endpoint will not
settle whether anyone gets pneumonia.**

**Two reservations.** **One hour after thoracoscopic lung resection is very early, and a patient's
one-second volume at that moment is dominated by pain, splinting and the residual anaesthetic as much as by
neuromuscular recovery** — the protocol measures pain scores alongside, which is the right control and only
a partial one. **And a resection of one segment or less is the mildest thoracic surgery there is**, chosen
sensibly to isolate the reversal effect and at the cost of applying least to the patients at highest
pulmonary risk.

**Recorded so that the result can be looked for.** **A trial registered in 2026 with 240 patients should
report in 2028, and this archive now has the protocol, the registration number and the endpoint written
down.**
