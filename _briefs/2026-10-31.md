## 1. A randomised trial that missed its primary outcome and reported three secondary benefits on nineteen events

**[Efficacy of Stellate Ganglion Block for Preventing Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation After Cardiac
Surgery: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.126.051373)** —
*Journal of the American Heart Association*, **first published 30 October 2026**. ChiCTR2300068572.

**A real trial with a real placebo.** Single centre; **140 patients undergoing valve surgery with
cardiopulmonary bypass**, randomised 1:1 to **ultrasound-guided right stellate ganglion block with 7 mL of
0.5% ropivacaine or 7 mL of normal saline**; **132 completed and entered the modified intention-to-treat
analysis (65 block, 67 saline)**. The rationale is autonomic: a stellate block reduces sympathetic outflow to
the heart, and sympathetic activation is one of the mechanisms proposed for postoperative atrial fibrillation.

**The primary outcome is as flat as a primary outcome gets:**

> POAF occurred in **43% of the SGB group and 40% of the saline group (odds ratio 0.99 [95% CI, 0.46–2.15],
> adjusted P = 0.989).**

**An odds ratio of 0.99 with P = 0.989.** Reconstructing from the percentages, that is about **28 of 65
against 27 of 67.** The block did nothing to the arrhythmia it was given for.

**And then three secondary outcomes all turn positive:**

| Secondary outcome | Block | Saline | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intraoperative VF/VT needing defibrillation after cross-clamp release | **6%** (≈4) | **19%** (≈13) | OR **0.21** (0.06–0.80), P = 0.022 |
| Postoperative high-sensitivity troponin I | — | — | median difference **−2,562 ng/L** (−4,301 to −45), P = 0.045 |
| Postoperative acute kidney injury | **5%** (≈3) | **18%** (≈12) | OR **0.24** (0.06–0.98), P = 0.046 |

**My reading, and the arithmetic is the argument.** **The two binary secondaries rest on four events against
thirteen, and three against twelve — nineteen and fifteen events in total.** Both odds ratios have upper
bounds that nearly touch one (0.80 and 0.98) and P values of 0.022 and 0.046. **And the troponin interval's
upper bound is −45 ng/L against a point estimate of −2,562 — the interval misses zero by 1.8% of the
estimate.** **Three secondary findings, all barely significant, in a trial whose primary outcome was
null.**

**The abstract reports "adjusted P" without saying what the adjustment was for**, and nothing indicates a
multiplicity correction across the secondary outcomes. **With a null primary and three marginal secondaries,
multiplicity is the first thing a reader needs and the abstract does not supply it.**

**None of which makes the secondaries uninteresting — and the defibrillation finding is the one to keep.**
Ventricular arrhythmia on cross-clamp release is a reperfusion event with a plausible sympathetic component,
it is observed in theatre rather than ascertained later, and a thirteen-to-four difference in a double-blind
trial is the kind of signal worth a dedicated trial. **The honest statement is: preoperative stellate ganglion
block does not prevent postoperative atrial fibrillation, and generated a hypothesis about reperfusion
ventricular arrhythmia that needs its own primary outcome.** The conclusion says *"potential benefits"*, which
is the right register.

**Archive context: stellate ganglion block has not appeared in this archive before**, and this is the fifth
postoperative-atrial-fibrillation document in six days — after the
[26 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/) finding that STS and APHRS invert each other's
recommendation classes, and the [29 October brief's](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-29/) PROPER drainage
protocol and 302-patient anticoagulation meta-analysis.

## 2. A negative cohort that says exactly which question it could not ask

**[Plasma magnesium levels and postoperative atrial fibrillation after cardiac surgery: a cohort
study](https://doi.org/10.1093/icvts/ivag289)** — *Interdisciplinary CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery*,
**first published 30 October 2026**. Retrospective, **435 adults** at a cardiovascular specialist hospital;
isolated coronary bypass in 46.6%; overall mortality 3.2%; **postoperative atrial fibrillation 16.8%.**

**Plasma magnesium was assessed three ways** — at ICU admission, as the mean value, and as the lowest value —
each as the primary exposure in multivariable models adjusted for preoperative, intraoperative and
postoperative covariates.

> **Under active repletion, hypomagnesemia occurred in only 3.4% of patients. None of the three magnesium
> measures was independently associated with POAF.** In contrast, **age, type of surgical procedure, severe
> left ventricular dysfunction, and postoperative acute kidney injury** were independently associated.

**Interpretation, and the paper's own framing is what makes it worth reporting.** **Hypomagnesaemia in 3.4% of
435 patients is about fifteen people.** A cohort in which the exposure of interest is almost absent cannot
test whether that exposure causes the outcome — **and the conclusion says so precisely, restricting its claim
to magnesium "within the observed normal-to-high range".** **That is a restriction-of-range problem named
rather than papered over, and it is the right way to publish a null.** The finding is not *magnesium does not
matter*; it is *once you have repleted everybody, further variation in the normal range does not matter*,
which is a useful operational statement and a different one.

**The paper is also careful about a direction of inference that the day's other item invites.** It notes that
acute kidney injury *"showed a concurrent association with POAF and may act as a marker of shared postoperative
risk factors rather than a causal factor."* **Set that beside item 1, where stellate ganglion block was
associated with less acute kidney injury and less atrial fibrillation was not observed** — **two papers the
same day, both touching the kidney injury–atrial fibrillation link, and one of them explicitly warns against
reading it causally.** **Neither establishes the direction, and the pair is a good reminder that a shared
marker of perioperative insult will correlate with everything.**

**My reading.** **Two negative studies on postoperative atrial fibrillation in one day, from opposite
methodological ends — a randomised trial of an intervention and a cohort study of a biomarker — and both are
well-reported nulls.** What survives is the risk-factor list, which is unglamorous and actionable: **age,
procedure type, severe left ventricular dysfunction.** Those are known before the incision, which is where
this archive's postoperative-atrial-fibrillation thread keeps pointing: **the guidelines argue about
prophylaxis, and the best-established predictors are all fixed at the time of listing.**

## 3. The intervention yesterday's marker implied, in 87 trials — and four significant outcomes under five minutes

**[Processed electroencephalogram-guided anesthesia and its impact on postoperative delirium and perioperative
efficiency: A systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2026.112351)** —
*Journal of Clinical Anesthesia*, **first published 30 October 2026**. Searches 11 April 2025; PROSPERO
CRD420251067170. **Eighty-seven randomised trials, 30,611 patients — 13,586 with processed EEG guidance and
17,025 without**, which sums exactly.

| Outcome | Effect | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propofol use | SMD **−0.42** | −0.74 to −0.10 |
| Volatile agent use | SMD **−1.27** | −1.88 to −0.66 |
| **Postoperative delirium (≤10 days)** | **RR 0.73** | **0.63 to 0.84** |
| Extubation time | MD **−2.90 min** | −3.64 to −2.16 |
| Wake-up time | MD **−1.83 min** | −2.42 to −1.25 |
| Time to orientation | MD **−4.11 min** | −6.99 to −1.23 |
| PACU length of stay | MD **−2.81 min** | −4.66 to −0.96 |

**Benefits were most consistent for BIS; Entropy and SEDLine estimates were less precise for want of
studies.** **"Heterogeneity was substantial for several outcomes."**

**Why this closes a loop opened yesterday.** The [30 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-30/)
carried a 300-patient study in which **burst suppression duration discriminated delirium with an area under
the curve of 0.871**, and this page argued that **burst suppression is probably a mediator of sedation depth
rather than an independent marker, so the actionable version of the finding was already the recommendation:
keep patients out of burst suppression.** **Today's meta-analysis is the intervention that operationalises
exactly that, in 87 randomised trials: guide the depth, give less drug, get 27% less delirium.** **The
mechanism is coherent end to end — less agent, less excessive depth, less delirium — and the two papers
arriving a day apart is the clearest mechanistic chain this archive has assembled.**

**And the delirium estimate is the part to trust.** RR 0.73 with an interval from 0.63 to 0.84 across
30,611 patients is a tight, substantial effect, and the drug-use reductions give it a mechanism — **the
volatile SMD of −1.27 is a reduction of more than one standard deviation, which is large.**

**The efficiency outcomes are where this page parts company with the abstract's framing.** *"Efficiency
outcomes favored pEEG"* is true and all four intervals exclude zero — **and all four effects are under five
minutes.** **Extubation 2.90 minutes earlier, waking 1.83 minutes earlier, orientation 4.11 minutes earlier,
and 2.81 minutes less time in recovery.** **At 30,611 patients, a three-minute difference is statistically
unassailable and operationally meaningless: no theatre list, recovery roster or staffing model is built on
three minutes.** **This is significance without importance, and reporting the four together as "efficiency
outcomes" invites a reader to add them up into something they are not.** **The delirium result does not need
them.**

**Two further cautions.** **Substantial heterogeneity is declared and no certainty rating is offered** — no
GRADE, no sensitivity framing in the abstract — and **87 trials spanning surgical, endoscopic and dental
procedures is enormous clinical heterogeneity to pool.** **And the device subgrouping is honest but
consequential: the evidence is really about BIS**, so a department using Entropy or SEDLine is extrapolating
from *"less precise"* estimates. The conclusion says this.

## 4. The same question, a different design, a day later — and the two agree

**[Effects of perioperative dexmedetomidine on postoperative delirium in older surgical patients: an updated
meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-026-01636-2)** — *European
Geriatric Medicine*, **first published 30 October 2026**. Three databases to **7 July 2026**; PROSPERO
CRD420251001512. **Twenty-two randomised trials, 4,161 patients**, older surgical patients under **general
anaesthesia**.

- **Postoperative delirium: RR 0.56 (0.47–0.66), P < 0.00001, I² = 24%** — a 44% relative reduction.
- **Postoperative nausea and vomiting: RR 0.79 (0.62–0.99), P = 0.04, I² = 0%.**
- **Higher intraoperative and postoperative bradycardia, and higher intraoperative hypotension.**

**The comparison that makes this worth an entry is with yesterday's page.**

| | Published here 30 October | Published here today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design | 5 RCTs, **1,613** patients | **22 RCTs, 4,161** patients |
| Setting | **sedation for regional anaesthesia**, ≥60 years | **general anaesthesia**, older patients |
| Comparator | **propofol** | control / non-dexmedetomidine |
| **Delirium** | **RR 0.48 (0.36–0.64)** | **RR 0.56 (0.47–0.66)** |
| Heterogeneity | I² 13.4% | I² 24% |

**Two meta-analyses of dexmedetomidine for postoperative delirium, published one day apart, on different
anaesthetic techniques and against different comparators, giving 0.48 and 0.56 with overlapping intervals.**
**That is a convergence test the field rarely gets, and it passes.** **The effect survives changing the
anaesthetic technique and the comparator**, which is a stronger claim than either paper makes alone and is
only visible because both landed in the same window.

**And today's paper supplies the cost yesterday's omitted.** The regional-anaesthesia review reported the
benefit and said nothing about harms; **this one reports higher bradycardia both intraoperatively and
postoperatively, and higher intraoperative hypotension.** **That closes the ledger this archive has been
assembling all week** — the [25 October brief's](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-25/) dexmedetomidine
meta-analysis whose outcome joined delirium and cognitive impairment with a slash, and the
[27 October brief's](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-27/) finding of significantly more intraoperative
hypotension during pulsed field ablation. **Dexmedetomidine reduces delirium, reliably, and costs heart rate
and blood pressure, reliably.**

**One reporting defect, and it is partial rather than absent.** **The adverse events are named as secondary
outcomes and reported only as directions — "was associated with a higher incidence" — with no risk ratios and
no intervals.** **A reader cannot weigh a 44% delirium reduction against an unquantified bradycardia risk**,
and that is precisely the trade a clinician is being asked to make. **This archive has now logged three
abstracts in six days that name an outcome and give no result; this is the fourth case in that family, and the
first where direction is given and magnitude withheld.**

**My reading.** **The delirium benefit of dexmedetomidine is now about as well established as this literature
can make it** — two independent syntheses a day apart, different designs, concordant estimates, low to
moderate heterogeneity. **The open question is no longer whether it works but in whom the haemodynamic cost is
acceptable**, and neither meta-analysis is designed to answer that. **The PONV reduction is a genuine
secondary bonus** at RR 0.79 with no heterogeneity, though its upper bound of 0.99 means it only just clears.
