Items 1 and 2 are both attempts to use a blood test to decide who gets which drug in critical illness. **One
finds its biomarker useless and its second biomarker pointing backwards; the other finds a biomarker that
works in two independent cohorts and may explain why two previous studies disagreed.** Read together they
are a fair picture of where precision medicine in the ICU actually is.

## 1. Ferritin and suPAR as treatable traits for anakinra — REMAP-CAP

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **1 October 2026** — four days old. **Exploratory analysis of the
COVID-19 immune modulation domain of REMAP-CAP.** Bayesian cumulative logistic regression. Open access in
PubMed Central. NCT02735707.
Boyle, Ryan, Berry, Berry, Derde, Lawler, Lorenzi, O'Kane, Shankar-Hari, van de Veerdonk, Gordon et al.

> **Background Identifying treatable traits in critically ill patients offers potential to enable a precision
> medicine approach**… to evaluate whether plasma ferritin or soluble urokinase plasminogen activator
> receptor (suPAR) represent treatable traits for anakinra…
>
> **Methods** … Adults within 24 h of receiving respiratory or cardiovascular organ support… The primary
> outcome was **organ support-free days (OSFD) until day 21.** Analyses used a **Bayesian cumulative logistic
> regression model**, presented as **posterior median odds ratio with 95% credible intervals, for anakinra
> being superior to control.**
>
> **Results** In ferritin analyses, **1243 patients** were included. Patients with increasing ferritin levels
> had numerically fewer OSFDs. **The posterior probabilities of anakinra having benefit (OR > 1) were 64%,
> 71%, and 21% across increasing ferritin terciles, compared to 53% in analyses that did not adjust for
> ferritin.** **145 patients** were included in the suPAR analyses. Patients with **high baseline suPAR
> (≥ 6 ng/ml; n = 85)** had numerically fewer OSFDs than patients with low suPAR (**−1.0 (−1.0, 9.0) vs.
> 13.0 (−1.0, 17.0)**). The posterior probabilities of anakinra having benefit were **94.8% (OR 2.28
> [0.85–6.06]) in patients with low baseline suPAR, and 38.6% (OR 0.88 [0.39–2.00]) in patients with high
> baseline suPAR.**
>
> **Conclusion Plasma ferritin does not identify patients with COVID-19 who benefit from treatment with
> anakinra, whilst anakinra may be beneficial in patients with low, rather than high, baseline plasma
> suPAR. These hypothesis-generating findings contrast with the results of clinical trials in non-critically
> ill patients with COVID-19.**

**Interpretation.** **The suPAR result is a reversal, and the authors name it as one.** Outside the ICU,
elevated suPAR has been used as the *inclusion* criterion for anakinra — the selection logic being that high
suPAR marks the hyperinflammatory patient the drug is designed for. **Here, in patients already receiving
organ support, the posterior probability of benefit was 94.8% in the low-suPAR group and 38.6% in the high-suPAR
group.** If that holds, the enrichment strategy has the sign wrong in critical illness.

**Two things make it more interesting than a failed subgroup, and one makes it much weaker.**

More interesting: **the ferritin analysis is a clean negative with an internal gradient.** Posterior
probability of benefit ran 64%, 71%, **21%** across rising terciles against 53% unadjusted — so the highest-ferritin
patients, the ones a hyperinflammation hypothesis would target first, had the *lowest* probability of
benefit. **Ferritin is not a null biomarker here; it is a mildly misleading one.** And **both biomarkers
point the same way: the sicker-looking inflammatory phenotype did worse with the anti-inflammatory drug.**

Much weaker: **the suPAR analysis rests on 145 patients, 85 high and 60 low.** The headline odds ratio is
**2.28 with a credible interval of 0.85 to 6.06**, which includes no effect and includes substantial harm.
**The 94.8% posterior probability and that interval are the same information stated two ways**, and a reader
who takes the first and not the second will badly overread it. Bayesian posterior probabilities are not
P values and do not need to clear 95% to be informative — but **"94.8% probability of benefit" from 60
patients in the favourable arm is a hypothesis, which is exactly what the authors call it.**

**One structural point worth naming.** This is a **biomarker-by-treatment interaction in a subgroup of a
subgroup**: the immune modulation domain of a platform trial, then split by a biomarker measured in stored
plasma for only 145 of them. **The ferritin analysis had 1,243 patients because ferritin was measured at
study sites; suPAR had 145 because it needed stored samples.** So the stronger claim rests on the weaker
dataset, and the direction of that asymmetry is the opposite of what you would want.

**Why it matters beyond COVID-19.** This archive has followed the treatable-traits and phenotyping programme
for weeks. **The programme's promise is that trials fail because populations are heterogeneous, and that
biomarkers will find the responders. This is a careful, well-powered test of that promise in a platform
trial, and the first biomarker does not work while the second works backwards.** That does not refute the
approach; it does show that **a biomarker validated outside the ICU cannot be assumed to carry the same sign
inside it.**

## 2. White cell count decides which anti-pseudomonal antibiotic is better — in two cohorts

**Journal · Published:** *AJRCCM*, **1 October 2026** — four days old. **Post hoc subgroup analyses of the
ACORN randomised trial and of a separate instrumental-variable study.**
Rzewnicki, Chanderraj, Qian, Semler, Casey, Rice, Coopersmith, He, Nuppnau, Prescott, Admon et al.

> **Rationale** Empiric anti-pseudomonal coverage is an important part of treatment of sepsis, but there are
> limited data to guide antibiotic selection. **The Antibiotic Choice on Renal Outcomes (ACORN) randomized
> trial found no difference in mortality when comparing cefepime and piperacillin-tazobactam, while an
> instrumental variable (IV) study found improved mortality with cefepime.**
>
> **Objectives** To determine if white blood cell (WBC) count modifies the effect of antibiotic choice on
> mortality.
>
> **Results There was a significant interaction between WBC and treatment group in predicting 28-day
> mortality in both the ACORN trial and the IV study (OR 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92–0.98], P <.01 and OR 0.95 [95%
> CI, 0.94–0.96], P <.01)**, with likelihood ratio testing revealing better model fit with inclusion of the
> interaction term (**F = 8.17** and **F = 42.9**). **In the ACORN cohort, patients with a baseline WBC count
> of 16 or higher had significantly lower odds of mortality when treated with piperacillin-tazobactam
> compared to cefepime (OR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.29–0.90]).**
>
> **Conclusions** WBC modified the effect of antibiotic choice on mortality in 2 large clinical studies.
> **Further research is needed to confirm this effect in prospective studies and to determine if WBC count
> could be used for predictive enrichment in future trials.**

**Interpretation.** **The framing in the rationale is the whole argument and it is elegant: two studies asked
which antibiotic is better and gave different answers, and the proposal is that they differed because their
populations differed on one cheap, universally measured variable.** If white cell count modifies the effect,
then a trial enrolling more leucocytosis will favour piperacillin-tazobactam and one enrolling less will
favour cefepime, and neither is wrong.

**What makes this stronger than the usual post hoc subgroup is replication.** The interaction appears **in
both datasets, with nearly identical odds ratios — 0.95 in each** — and in both the model fits better with
the interaction term included. **A subgroup finding that reproduces in an independent cohort with a different
design is a different class of evidence from one that does not**, and this archive has spent the last week
taking apart single-cohort subgroup claims.

**Three cautions, and the first is the one the authors lead with themselves.** **Both analyses are post hoc**
— ACORN was not designed to test this, and the IV study is observational with all the assumptions that
instrument carries. The authors ask for prospective confirmation rather than claiming practice change, and
the conclusion's phrase — **"could be used for predictive enrichment in future trials"** — is a proposal
about trial design, not about tonight's prescription.

**Second, the interaction odds ratio of 0.95 is per unit of WBC**, so the effect at any given white count
depends on the distance from the reference, and the clinically quotable number is the threshold analysis:
**WBC ≥ 16, OR 0.51 (0.29–0.90) favouring piperacillin-tazobactam.** That interval is wide and its upper
bound is close to 1.

**Third, the threshold of 16 is reported from the ACORN cohort only.** The interaction replicated; **the
specific cut-point did not, or at least is not reported as having done so.** An interaction that reproduces
and a threshold that is derived once are different findings, and only the first has been replicated.

**It also lands on live ground here.** [The SPILF community-acquired pneumonia guideline reported on 2
October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-02/) defines indications for anti-pseudomonal beta-lactams
and for combination therapy; [an *AJRCCM* paper on antibiotic duration in sepsis logged on 29
September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/) is still unwritten. **A guideline choosing the drug class,
a trial choosing within it, and a biomarker choosing between the two options inside the class — that is the
antimicrobial decision tree being worked at three levels at once.**

## 3. Lung ultrasound under the 2023 ARDS definition

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Critical Care*, **1 October 2026** — four days old. Prospective
single-centre study, 224 patients, Ningxia Medical University General Hospital, January to September 2025.
ChiCTR2600117533.
Chen, Bai, Ma, An, Ma

> The primary objective was to **describe the distribution characteristics of lung ultrasound (LUS) findings
> in patients meeting the 2023 global definition of ARDS.** The **secondary, exploratory** aim was to
> evaluate the prognostic association of LUS scores with 28-day mortality…
>
> **224 patients**… LUS signs (regional/total scores, pleural line abnormalities) collected **within 24 h of
> admission**… **The 28-day mortality rate was 29.46% (66/224).** Total LUS aeration score correlated
> positively with pleural line abnormalities (**r = 0.505**). **Survivors showed higher proportions of
> A-lines and normal pleural lines, while non-survivors had more fused B-lines. Pleural line abnormalities
> showed the strongest exploratory association with 28-day mortality (AUC = 0.617, 95% CI: 0.550–0.681,
> P = 0.008).**
>
> **Conclusion** … suggesting **potential value for bedside risk stratification.**

**Interpretation.** **The honest summary of this paper is in its own numbers rather than its conclusion. An
area under the curve of 0.617 is a poor discriminator** — 0.5 is a coin toss and 0.617 with a lower bound of
0.550 is barely distinguishable from one. **"Potential value for bedside risk stratification" overstates
what a 0.617 AUC can support**, and the gap between that phrase and that number is the thing to carry away.

**To the paper's credit, the framing is right even where the conclusion drifts.** The primary objective was
**descriptive** — what lung ultrasound looks like in patients meeting the new definition — and the mortality
association is labelled **secondary and exploratory** twice. **Judged as a description it is useful**, and
the descriptive finding is the real content: survivors had more A-lines and normal pleural lines,
non-survivors more fused B-lines, and the aeration score tracks pleural abnormality at r = 0.505.

**Why it is here is the category rather than the result.** This is **the second paper in two days using the
2023 global ARDS definition** — [yesterday's TISA-818 phase 2
trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-04/) was the first, and this archive flagged 2023-definition papers
as a category to watch on sight. **The two illustrate what the new definition is for and what it costs.** It
admits non-intubated patients, which is why TISA-818's signal sat in its non-intubated subgroup; and it
admits a more heterogeneous population, which is one reason a bedside sign in that population discriminates
at 0.617. **A broader definition buys earlier enrolment and pays for it in prognostic precision.**

**Two limits.** **Single centre, 224 patients, one country**, and lung ultrasound is the most
operator-dependent common ICU investigation — inter-rater reliability is not reported in the abstract, which
for an imaging-sign study is the number a reader most needs. And **mortality in this cohort was 29.46%**,
which is high enough that the population is not the mild end of the new definition.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155764)

## 4. Three versus six weeks of steroids for immune-related pneumonitis — and what the conclusion leaves out

**Journal · Published:** *AJRCCM*, **1 October 2026** — four days old. **Randomised noninferiority trial**,
106 patients. **Secondary scope** — immune checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis is oncology-pulmonology rather
than critical care — included because it is a clean randomised answer to a duration question and because of
how it is reported.
Fujimoto, Abe, Murotani, Onishi, Sato, Miyauchi, Takase, Tanaka, Kijima, Taniguchi, Otsuki et al.

> **Optimal corticosteroid treatment duration for ICI-related pneumonitis remains uncertain. No randomized
> trials have addressed this issue.** … To determine whether a **3-week** corticosteroid taper is
> **noninferior** to the guideline-recommended **6-week** taper… The primary endpoint was **treatment success
> at 8 weeks, defined as resting room-air SpO2 ≥ 90% without steroid escalation or prolongation.**
>
> **106 patients** were randomly assigned (median age 72; grade 2, 73%). **Treatment success rates were 66.7%
> and 85.2% in the 3- and 6-week groups**, which **did not demonstrate noninferiority** (difference = −18.5
> percentage points; **80% CI, −29.0% to −7.9%**; P = .621). **A predefined exploratory analysis indicated
> superiority of the 6-week regimen (P = .013). Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 12% and 24% of the 3- and
> 6-week groups, respectively; however, all were manageable with clinical interventions.** Quality of life
> and **overall survival were comparable (HR 1.03; 95% CI, 0.46–2.29).**
>
> **Conclusions This study establishes the 6-week corticosteroid regimen as an evidence-based standard.**

**Interpretation.** **The efficacy result is clean and the conclusion is half of it.** Three weeks did not
demonstrate noninferiority, a predefined exploratory analysis favoured six weeks, and an 18.5 percentage
point difference in treatment success is clinically large. **So far the conclusion is earned.**

**What it omits is in the same sentence of the results: grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 12% of the
three-week group and 24% of the six-week group.** The longer course doubled the rate of serious adverse
events. **"All were manageable with clinical interventions" is a mitigation, not a reason to leave the number
out of the conclusion** — and a trial that establishes a standard has an obligation to state what the
standard costs. The honest summary is **six weeks works better and harms more**, with equal quality of life
and equal survival, and the trade is one a clinician should be told about rather than have resolved for them.

**Note the statistical shape, because this archive has a rule about its mirror image.** On 29 September it
recorded that a failed superiority test must never be upgraded to equivalence. **This is the reverse
direction and it is handled correctly: a failed noninferiority test, plus a predefined exploratory
superiority analysis that was positive.** Because the superiority analysis was **predefined**, that is a
legitimate inference rather than a fishing expedition — and it is worth crediting, since the easy error here
would have been to report "noninferiority not shown" and stop.

**One reconstruction that does not work, reported because yesterday's did.** Yesterday the counts behind a
set of percentages were recoverable by division. **Here they are not: 85.2% pins one arm at 46/54, but
66.7% is consistent with 34/51 or 36/54, and 54 + 54 = 108 against 106 randomised.** So either the arms were
unequal or two patients were not analysed, and the abstract does not say. **The technique has a failure mode
and this is it** — when two candidate denominators both fit and their sum contradicts the stated total,
report the ambiguity rather than picking one.

**On scope, plainly:** mild grade 1–2 pneumonitis is managed on the ward and in clinic, not the ICU. It is
reported here because **steroid duration is a question this archive meets repeatedly** — the SPILF
community-acquired pneumonia guideline reported on 2 October specifies indications and modalities for
systemic corticosteroids — and because a randomised answer to any steroid-duration question is rare enough
to be worth having.
