## 1. Four thousand seven hundred children, and the answer is to stop measuring

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 September 2026** — six weeks old, 1 citation. **Pragmatic,
multicentre, randomised noninferiority trial in 23 paediatric intensive care units in the UK and one in
Switzerland.** Recruitment June 2023 to December 2025. **ISRCTN79668198.**
Tume, Mouncey, Broomhall, Ferrando-Vivas, Orzol, Chang, Au, Brown, Camsooksai, Hall, Khatun, Lampro, Laing,
Latten, Marino, Menzies, Parke, Sadique and colleagues

> **Importance** **Routine assessment of gastric residual volume (GRV) to guide enteral feeding in critically
> ill children is widespread but not based on evidence. Perceived high gastric volumes often lead to
> withholding feeds, impairing nutritional delivery.** …
>
> **Main outcomes** The **clinical co-primary outcome (noninferiority) was a composite of survival and days
> free from mechanical ventilation at 30 days. The nutritional co-primary outcome (superiority) was the
> percentage of children meeting energy requirements by 72 hours.**
>
> **Results** Of the **4700 children randomized** (2352 no routine GRV, 2348 usual care), **4460 were
> included in the intention-to-treat analysis** (median age **8 [1–44] months**; 1925 [42.6%] female). **No
> routine GRV assessment was noninferior** … (median 25 [21–27] days free in both groups; **adjusted odds
> ratio 0.95 [95% CI, 0.86–1.05]**). Per-protocol analysis consistent (**aOR 1.01 [0.90–1.13]**). **The mean
> percentage of energy requirements achieved by 72 hours was 80.3% versus 76.8% (adjusted mean difference
> 3.2 [1.3–5.2] percentage points; P < .001).**

**Interpretation. This is a de-implementation trial, which is the rarest and most useful kind, and the design
is the thing to admire.** Two co-primary outcomes with **different statistical frames**: noninferiority for
the clinical outcome, because the question is whether stopping is safe, and **superiority for the nutritional
outcome, because the claim is that stopping actively helps.** Both were met. **Most trials of removing a
practice only test the first half and then have nothing positive to report**; this one asked for both and got
both.

**The nutritional gain is statistically solid and clinically modest, and the paper's framing is slightly
generous to it.** **80.3% against 76.8% of energy requirements at 72 hours is a raw difference of 3.5 points,
reported as an adjusted 3.2 (1.3 to 5.2)** — real, tightly bounded, and not what would change a child's
outcome on its own. **The two figures are not interchangeable and the adjusted one is the estimate.** **The honest argument
for abandoning routine gastric residual measurement is not that it gains three points of energy delivery. It
is that it costs nursing time every six hours, triggers feed interruptions, and — as this trial now shows —
buys nothing.** That is a stronger case than the nutritional endpoint makes.

**Two things the abstract does not give.** **The noninferiority margin is not stated**, and an adjusted odds
ratio of 0.95 with an upper bound of 1.05 is only interpretable against it; the per-protocol result at 1.01
(0.90–1.13) is reassuring on the same point. And **240 of 4,700 randomised children (5.1%) are absent from
the intention-to-treat analysis**, unexplained in the abstract — small, but on a noninferiority trial the
direction of exclusions matters more than on a superiority trial, because losses tend to push toward the
null and noninferiority is the null.

**Note the population: median age 8 months.** This is predominantly an infant trial, and the practice it
retires is one where the measurement is most technically awkward and the feeds most easily interrupted.
**Where it sits in this archive:** the [4 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-04/) analgosedation
scoping review found ICU protocols lagging their own guidelines, and the **ESPEN practical ICU nutrition
guideline** reported on 8 October exists to help clinicians implement nutrition recommendations. **This trial
removes one of the obstacles those documents are working around.**

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.10639) — paywalled beyond the abstract.

## 2. The bed-angle question, answered four ways, and the answer is 30 degrees

**Journal · Published:** *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, **21 September 2026** — three weeks old.
**An update of the 2016 review that adds 38 new randomised trials, bringing the total to 48 studies and
7,955 participants.** Searches to **3 October 2025**, including **three Chinese databases.** GRADE
throughout.
Jiang, Chen, Li, Li, Dong, Yuan, Tang, Yang, Wang

> **1. Semi-recumbent versus supine: 17 studies, 2640 participants.** Reduces **clinically suspected VAP (RR
> 0.42, 0.33 to 0.52; high-certainty evidence)**, but **may result in little to no difference in
> microbiologically confirmed VAP (RR 0.57, 0.25 to 1.27; low-certainty)**, and **probably little to no
> difference in ICU or hospital mortality** (moderate-certainty). **May reduce duration of ventilation
> (MD −3.26 days, −6.02 to −0.51; low-certainty).**
>
> **2. 30° to 45° versus < 30°: 11 studies.** Reduces **clinically suspected VAP (RR 0.48, 0.39 to 0.59;
> high-certainty)**. **Probably increases pressure ulcers (RR 2.79, 1.28 to 6.11; moderate-certainty).**
>
> **3. 40° to 45° versus 30°: 14 studies.** **Probably little to no difference in clinically suspected VAP
> (RR 0.91, 0.62 to 1.33; moderate-certainty)**, may reduce microbiologically confirmed VAP (RR 0.53, 0.28 to
> 0.99; low-certainty). **Probably increases pressure ulcers (RR 2.42, 1.16 to 5.06; moderate-certainty).**
>
> **4. Semi-recumbent versus semi-recumbent lateral or lateral rotation: 7 studies.** Semi-recumbent
> **probably increases** clinically suspected and confirmed VAP (**RR 1.99, 1.48 to 2.70**; and RR 5.14 in one
> study; both moderate-certainty).

**Interpretation. Comparisons 2 and 3 together answer the question every guideline leaves vague, and the
answer is 30 degrees.** Raising the head from below 30° to 30–45° cuts suspected pneumonia by half at **high
certainty.** Raising it further, from 30° to 40–45°, **does nothing** to suspected pneumonia at moderate
certainty **and roughly doubles pressure ulcers.** **Guidelines customarily say "30 to 45 degrees"; this
review says the benefit is at the bottom of that range and the harm is at the top.** That is a directly
actionable reading of an intervention performed on every ventilated patient in the world.

**Now the finding that should temper the whole review, and it is visible in comparison 1.** The benefit at
**high certainty is on clinically suspected VAP (RR 0.42)**. The estimate for **microbiologically confirmed
VAP is null with wide intervals at low certainty (RR 0.57, 0.25 to 1.27)**. **Bed position cannot be blinded**
— the clinician deciding whether this patient has pneumonia can see the angle of the bed, and "clinically
suspected VAP" is a judgement built from radiology, secretions and inflammatory markers in a patient whose
position is known. **So the outcome with the strongest evidence is the one most exposed to ascertainment
bias, and the outcome that would settle it is underpowered.**

**This archive has now seen that exact configuration three times in five days** — the [multisensory virtual
reality delirium trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-07/) with an unblindable intervention and an
observer-rated outcome, and the obstetric team-training meta-analysis where the positive signal sat in cohort
studies. **The pattern is worth stating as a rule: when an intervention cannot be blinded, rank the outcomes
by how much judgement they require, and read the most objective one first.** Here that is mortality, which
is null at moderate certainty, and microbiological confirmation, which is null at low certainty.

**Comparison 4 is the one nobody expects and it deserves a sentence of its own.** Against **semi-recumbent
lateral or lateral rotation positioning, plain semi-recumbent positioning was worse** — RR 1.99 for suspected
VAP at moderate certainty, and RR 5.14 for confirmed VAP in a single study. **No major guideline recommends
lateral or rotational positioning**, and seven trials in 708 patients now suggest the position everyone uses
is not the best of the available options. **That is a hypothesis, on a comparison with one study for the
confirmed outcome, and it is the most interesting thing in a 48-study review.**

**The honest summary of all four comparisons: the head of the bed should be up rather than flat, 30° is
enough, higher costs skin, and the size of the pneumonia benefit rests on a diagnosis that the intervention
itself can influence.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd009946.pub3) — paywalled beyond the abstract.

## 3. The drug with better haemodynamics is the one your depth monitor cannot read

**Two documents, published three weeks apart, that answer each other.**

**Journal · Published:** *European Journal of Anaesthesiology*, **4 September 2026** — five weeks old.
Systematic review and meta-analysis **with trial sequential analysis**; five databases to April 2025;
**12 RCTs, 739 patients**, mean age 61.6.
Takayama, de Souza, Batista da Hora, Hakkeem, Dias, Bezerra

> **Remimazolam significantly reduced the risk of intraoperative hypotension** (as a binary event) compared
> with sevoflurane **[RR 0.38, 0.25 to 0.57; P < 0.001]. MAP was higher with remimazolam at 30 min** (mean
> difference 6.05 mmHg). **Heart rate was consistently higher** with remimazolam at three timepoints.
> Analyses suggested a **reduction in PONV up to 24 h [RR 0.65, 0.47 to 0.90] and 72 h [RR 0.66, 0.50 to
> 0.89]. TSA just confirmed favouring remimazolam in reducing intraoperative hypotension.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **Sensitivity analyses suggested a potential reduction in PONV; however, these findings
> were not statistically significant in the primary analysis and should be interpreted with caution.**

**Journal · Published:** *Annals of Medicine*, **25 September 2026** — two and a half weeks old. **Open
access** (PMC13618082). **Randomised, double-blind, two-period crossover in 16 healthy ASA I volunteers**,
each receiving both agents at least seven days apart, **titrated to moderate (MOAA/S 3) and deep (MOAA/S 1)
sedation.**
Cai, Hu, Wei, Zheng, Xie, Lin, Pan

> **The bispectral index (BIS) was derived primarily from propofol data, and whether its thresholds apply to
> remimazolam is uncertain.** … **The confirmatory outcome was mean BIS at deep sedation. All other outcomes
> were exploratory.** … **At deep sedation, mean BIS was higher with remimazolam (57.6 ± 7.5) than with
> propofol (46.5 ± 7.9; adjusted difference 11.1 units; 95% CI, 7.3 to 14.8; p < 0.001).** … **emergence time
> did not differ (p = 0.606). At 30 min, processing speed and executive function recovered faster after
> remimazolam** … **All domains converged by 120 min.** … **Spectral data were not recorded, so the basis of
> this offset is undetermined.**

**Interpretation. Put the two together and the clinical problem is immediate: remimazolam looks
haemodynamically better than sevoflurane, and the number anaesthetists use to judge depth reads about eleven
units high on it.** A bispectral index of 57 during remimazolam corresponds to the clinical state that reads
46 during propofol — **so a clinician titrating to a familiar BIS target will tend to give more remimazolam
than the patient needs**, chasing a number that the drug does not produce at that depth. **The index was
built from propofol electroencephalography and the trial says so in its first sentence.**

**This is the archive's monitoring thread in its cleanest form yet.** The videolaryngoscopy finding recorded
here was a device that improved a measurement while degrading its diagnostic meaning; **this is a drug that
changes what an established measurement means without changing the patient's state at all.** The rule is the
same: **a number is only interpretable alongside the conditions under which it was calibrated**, and a
depth-of-anaesthesia index inherits the pharmacology of the agent it was derived from.

**The crossover trial is small and honest about every limit.** **Sixteen healthy volunteers**, one
confirmatory outcome and everything else exploratory, **and the mechanism explicitly unresolved because
spectral data were not recorded** — so whether the offset is beta-band activity, a benzodiazepine-specific
EEG signature, or something else is unknown. **The authors ask for validation in clinical populations rather
than claiming agent-specific thresholds**, which is the right stopping point. And the cognitive findings —
faster processing speed and executive function at 30 minutes, converging by 120 — are exploratory and should
be read as a hypothesis.

**The meta-analysis has an internal contradiction that a reader should see.** Its **Results** report PONV at
**RR 0.65 (0.47 to 0.90), P = 0.01** and at 72 hours **RR 0.66 (0.50 to 0.89)** — intervals excluding 1. Its
**Conclusions** say those findings **"were not statistically significant in the primary analysis"** and came
from sensitivity analyses. **Both statements cannot describe the same analysis**, and the abstract does not
reconcile them. **The defensible reading is the conservative one the Conclusions give**: hypotension is the
established finding, PONV is not.

**And "TSA just confirmed" is a phrase to read carefully.** A trial sequential analysis that *just* crosses
its monitoring boundary on **12 trials and 739 patients** is telling you the evidence has only now reached
sufficiency for the hypotension outcome, not that the question is closed. **The haemodynamic advantage is the
one claim here that both documents and the sequential analysis support.**

*A self-correction about this archive's own index metric, because today the arithmetic became impossible.
**Yesterday's entry reported that the retained days of the trailing window "gained 1,259 records overnight"
against whole-database growth of 1,281, and called the agreement the cleanest confirmation yet.** The method
was: take yesterday's window total, subtract the bucket that rolled off, add the bucket that rolled on, and
attribute the remainder to backfill into the retained days. **Run today, that method gives a retained gain of
1,393 against whole-database growth of 571 — which cannot be true, since the retained days are a subset of
the database.***

*The flaw is a date mismatch. **The rolled-off bucket is measured today, but it entered yesterday's window
total at yesterday's smaller value** — buckets keep filling, as this archive has documented all week — so the
subtraction removes more than was ever there. **Yesterday's figure was computed the same way and the close
agreement with whole-database growth was luck, not confirmation.** The rolling decomposition is retired.*

*The replacement is a **fixed-boundary reference window**, whose growth is unambiguous because its edges do
not move. **Measured today: 1–30 September holds 148,622 records and 1–7 October holds 19,881.** From
tomorrow, the growth of those two fixed ranges is reported beside the whole-database difference, and any
backfill into completed months becomes directly visible. **The substantive conclusion reached on 9 October is
untouched** — the old ~7,900/day baseline mixed current deposits with retrospective ingest, and that
comparison is what produced a four-day false alarm. **What is retired is the arithmetic published yesterday
to support it.***
