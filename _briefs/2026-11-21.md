## 1. The etomidate question, and the number that tells you how to read it

**[Etomidate vs Propofol for Anesthetic Induction: Association With 30-Day Mortality and Postoperative
Complications in a Propensity Score-Matched Retrospective Cohort
Study](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008407)** · *Anesthesia & Analgesia* · **published
20 November 2026** · 155,667 adults at one academic health system

> "A total of 155,667 patients met inclusion criteria, including 3846 (2.5%) who received etomidate and
> 151,821 (97.5%) who received propofol. Thirty-day mortality occurred in 1151 patients (0.74%). Unadjusted
> mortality was higher in the etomidate group (227/3846 [5.9%]) than in the propofol group
> (924/151,821 [0.61%]). After multivariable adjustment, etomidate use remained independently associated
> with increased 30-day mortality (aOR 1.44; 95% CI, 1.21-1.72)... The association persisted in the matched
> cohort (aOR 1.43; 95% CI, 1.14-1.78; P = 0.002), with observed mortality of 225/3832 (5.9%) among
> etomidate recipients and 157/3832 (4.1%) among propofol recipients, corresponding to an absolute risk
> difference of 1.8% and a number needed to harm of 56... Post-induction hypotension was lower in the
> etomidate group (aOR 0.40, 95% CI 0.32-0.49; P < 0.001)."

**Interpretation — read the two propofol mortality figures against each other before anything else.** In the
whole propofol population mortality is **0.61%**. In the matched propofol group it is **4.1%** — nearly
seven times higher. **Matching did not make the groups comparable by improving the etomidate arm; it worked
by finding the sickest 2.5% of propofol patients.** That is exactly what propensity matching is for, and it
is also the measurement of how different these patients were: the crude odds ratio is about 10, the matched
one is 1.43, so **roughly 88% of the apparent effect was confounding by indication.**

**The diagnostics are reported, which is creditable:** the average standardised mean difference fell from
**0.398 before matching to 0.023 after** — a genuinely large imbalance brought to a conventionally
acceptable level, and most papers do not print the before figure.

**The finding that does not have the shape of residual confounding is the hypotension result.**
**Post-induction hypotension was 60% lower with etomidate (aOR 0.40, 0.32–0.49).** Etomidate is chosen
precisely to preserve blood pressure at induction, and it did. **So the drug delivered its intended
haemodynamic effect and the patients still died more** — which means whatever is driving the mortality
signal is not acting through induction-period blood pressure. **That rules out one of the two obvious
confounding routes and leaves the other wide open.**

**And the secondary outcomes are the part most likely to be misread as corroboration.** Sepsis 1.28, acute
coronary syndrome 1.24, cardiogenic shock 1.56, acute kidney injury 1.27 — **every outcome elevated, by a
similar modest amount, in the same direction.** Consistency across endpoints reads as a robust drug effect.
**It is also the exact signature of an exposure that marks unmeasured severity**, because residual
confounding raises everything at once. **A mechanism-specific harm — adrenal suppression, which is the
stated concern — would predict a selective pattern, most obviously in sepsis and shock and much less in
acute coronary syndrome.** The pattern here is flat across organ systems.

**What the design cannot escape, and the authors say so in their last sentence.** A 2.5% exposure
prevalence means the propensity model must balance a small, highly selected group against a very large one;
the matched sample is 3,832 pairs, so **the entire result rests on 382 deaths.** The conclusion reads
"Given the observational design, results should be interpreted with caution", which is the right register
for a paper whose headline is a 1.8-point absolute risk difference extracted from a tenfold crude gap.

**Arithmetic checks.** 1/0.018 = 55.6, matching the stated number needed to harm of 56. The crude odds
ratio computes to about 10.2 from the reported counts.

## 2. A 48% mortality reduction, and the authors' own instrument says no

**[Disparities in Regional Analgesia Utilization for Rib Fractures: Hospital-Level Variation, Trends, and
Clinical Outcomes in a Nationwide Retrospective Cohort Study 2016 to
2022](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008394)** · *Anesthesia & Analgesia* · **published 17 November
2026** · 250,630 hospitalisations, Nationwide Inpatient Sample

> "250,630 hospitalizations met inclusion criteria, of which 7,179 (2.9%) received regional analgesia. After
> adjustment, 23.9% of variation in utilization was attributable to between-hospital differences...
> Lower odds were observed in private nonprofit (OR 0.75; 95% CI, 0.65-0.86) and for-profit hospitals (OR
> 0.42; 95% CI, 0.34-0.53) compared with government hospitals... Regional analgesia was associated with
> lower mortality (OR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.42-0.64) and tracheostomy (OR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.36-0.63) in
> multivariable analysis. In instrumental variable analysis with leave-one-out hospital-year regional
> analgesia rate as the instrument, these associations were attenuated."

**Interpretation — the last sentence is the paper, and it is in the abstract.** A 48% mortality reduction
and a 52% tracheostomy reduction from a nerve block are not plausible effect sizes for an analgesic
intervention. **The authors evidently thought so too: they built an instrument — each hospital-year's
regional analgesia rate, computed leaving the index patient out — and report that it attenuated their own
headline.** That is the correct analysis for exactly this problem, because the question is whether blocks
help patients or whether the patients who get blocks were going to do better anyway, and a hospital's
propensity to use blocks is plausibly unrelated to an individual patient's prognosis.

**Credit for reporting it in the abstract rather than burying it.** This archive has repeatedly had to
reconstruct that kind of caveat from arithmetic; here the authors ran the analysis that threatens their own
result and put the outcome where a reader will see it.

**The one reporting gap is that "attenuated" carries no number.** Attenuated to 0.8 and attenuated to 1.0
are different papers, and the abstract does not say which. **So the honest summary is that the mortality
association is substantially confounded by selection and the residual magnitude is not readable from the
abstract.** That is a smaller criticism than it sounds, and it is the only one.

**The utilisation findings are the solid part and need no instrument.** **Regional analgesia reaches 2.9% of
rib fracture hospitalisations** despite being recommended, and **23.9% of the variation in who gets it is
between hospitals** rather than between patients. **For-profit hospitals have 0.42 times the odds of
government hospitals** — a 58% lower rate at the same patient presentation. Private insurance 1.24, highest
income quartile 1.21, urban teaching 1.39, large bed size 2.08.

**The equity findings are stated plainly and should be quoted plainly: "Private insurance, White race,
highest income quartile, government ownership and urban teaching status were also associated with earlier
receipt."** Earlier receipt matters independently of receipt for an analgesic intervention in rib fracture,
where the purpose is to preserve ventilation in the first days.

**And the trend is the finding that should worry a specialty: disparities "either persisted or widened from
2016 to 2022."** Seven years of attention to inequity in acute pain management, and the gap did not close.

## 3. Anaemia at extubation: three analyses, three nulls, and one incidental number worth keeping

**[Anemia at extubation: association with reintubation, practices over time and impact on respiratory
effort](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11662145/v1)** · **Research Square preprint, posted 20 November
2026, not peer reviewed** · 1,445 patients in the observational cohort, 128 in the physiological cohort

> "The 7-day cumulative incidence of reintubation was 21.6% (95%CI, 18.2-25.1%) in patients with hemoglobin
> <9 g/dL and 23.5% (95%CI, 20.8-26.3%) in those with hemoglobin >=9 g/dL. After adjustment, hemoglobin
> <9 g/dL was not associated with 7-day reintubation (sHR 0.95, 95%CI 0.76-1.18; p=0.640). The median
> hemoglobin level at extubation decreased over time, from 9.9 g/dL in 2016 to 9.2 g/dL in 2024
> (p-for-trend=0.048). Inspiratory effort increased in overall physiological cohort population by 59% across
> extubation, and lower hemoglobin was associated with a slightly greater increase, although not
> statistically significant (pressure-time-product per minute +7.4% per 1-g/dL hemoglobin decrease; 95%CI,
> -3.3% to 18.2%; p=0.174)."

**Interpretation.** Three approaches to one question — an outcome analysis, a practice trend, and a
physiological mechanism — and **the anaemia hypothesis fails all three.** The point estimate for
reintubation is below 1, the physiological effect interval crosses zero, and the authors conclude a null.

**The competing-risk handling is the methodological credit and this archive keeps asking for it.**
Reintubation was assessed with the **Aalen–Johansen estimator and Fine–Gray models with death as a
competing event.** A patient who dies before day 7 cannot be reintubated, and treating that as censoring
would bias the incidence upward in exactly the sicker, more anaemic group whose risk is under study.

**The number worth keeping is incidental to the paper's question: inspiratory effort increased by 59% across
extubation in the whole physiological cohort.** Removing a tube nearly doubles the work of breathing,
measured by oesophageal pressure, in patients judged ready to have it removed. **That is a fact about
extubation rather than about anaemia, and it is the most transferable thing here** — it quantifies the step
every weaning decision is implicitly a bet on.

**The practice trend is real and fragile at once.** Median haemoglobin at extubation fell from 9.9 to
9.2 g/dL over nine years, **p-for-trend = 0.048** — significance by a margin of 0.002 on a spline fit across
nine years. **I would not report the trend as established on that p-value alone; what makes it credible is
that it is consistent in direction with the null outcome result**, in that practice drifted toward
extubating at lower haemoglobin and reintubation did not follow.

**One phrase to read carefully.** The conclusion calls the effort finding "only a modest trend toward
increased post-extubation inspiratory effort" on an interval of −3.3% to 18.2% with p = 0.174. **An interval
that includes a 3% decrease does not support the word "trend", and this is the one place the paper leans
past its own numbers.** It is a mild lean, and the headline conclusion is still the null.

**Third extubation-failure paper in three days, and together they say something.** Yesterday's Bayesian
meta-analysis found an absent or weak cough doubles the odds of failure after brain injury; yesterday's
re-evaluation found no machine-learning model beats a parsimonious clinical score; today's finds
haemoglobin does not matter. **Bedside assessment keeps surviving and the laboratory and the algorithm keep
not adding.**

## 4. Two outcomes that are arithmetically the same outcome

**[Anesthesia Care-Team Staffing Ratios in the United States: Anesthesia Time and Billing Units: A
Retrospective Observational Study](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008399)** · *Anesthesia &
Analgesia* · **published 20 November 2026** · billing records across 47 states, January 2018 to December
2024

> "Care-team cases were associated with longer anesthesia time (adjusted difference +1.67 minutes; 95% CI,
> 1.11-2.23; P < .001) and higher total billing units (adjusted difference +0.11 units; 95% CI, 0.04-0.18;
> P = .003)... Relative to physician-alone cases, anesthesia time was shorter with 1 CRNA directed (-2.52
> minutes), similar with 2 CRNAs (-0.13 minutes), and longer with 3 CRNAs (+3.03 minutes) and 4 CRNAs
> (+6.99 minutes); total billing units followed the same pattern (1 CRNA, -0.23; 2 CRNAs, -0.02 [not
> significant]; 3 CRNAs, +0.21; 4 CRNAs, +0.50). In a Medicare subsample, gross payments differed little
> overall (-$3.53; 95% CI, -$5.13 to -$1.92)."

**Interpretation — the two headline outcomes are not independent, and the arithmetic shows it exactly.**
American anaesthesia billing counts time in units of fifteen minutes. **+1.67 minutes divided by 15 is
0.111 units. The reported billing-unit difference is +0.11.** **The billing finding is the time finding
converted into units, to three significant figures.** Presenting them as two outcomes with two confidence
intervals and two p-values implies two pieces of evidence where there is one. The ratio-level figures carry
a little extra (1 CRNA: −2.52 minutes implies −0.168 units against −0.23 reported; 4 CRNAs: +6.99 implies
+0.466 against +0.50), so modifier and conditional units contribute at the margin — **but the dominant term
is mechanical.**

**Set that aside and the gradient is a real and more interesting finding than the headline comparison.**
**A 1:1 ratio is faster than an anaesthesiologist working alone, by 2.52 minutes.** Two is
indistinguishable. Three and four are slower, by 3.03 and 6.99 minutes. **So this is not "supervision costs
time" — it is "one-to-one saves time and dilution costs it", with the crossover between two and three.**
That is a usable operational statement and the headline +1.67 minutes conceals it by averaging across
ratios.

**And the effect sizes should be stated in the units that matter.** 1.67 minutes on a case is nothing.
Seven minutes at a 1:4 ratio is something, and the Medicare payment differences run from **−$10.44 at 1:1
to +$3.74 at 1:4** — tens of dollars on a case. **The authors call the differences "small overall", which
is accurate and more restrained than the paper's framing invites.** With millions of billing encounters, a
clinically negligible difference reaches P < .001, and significance here is a statement about sample size
rather than about magnitude.

**One bound the abstract is explicit about and a reader should hold onto: the data are billing records from
a revenue cycle management company.** Anaesthesia minutes in a billing record are a billed quantity, not a
stopwatch, and billing practice can itself vary with staffing model. **This is a study of what was billed
under different staffing ratios, which is a legitimate and narrower object than how long the anaesthetic
took.**

## 5. Eighty-five per cent against forty-one, and the difference is probably a procedure

**[Frequency of On-Scene Goal-Directed Field Interventions for Pediatric Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest in
a Regional System of Care](https://doi.org/10.1080/10903127.2026.2761025)** · *Prehospital Emergency Care*
· **published 20 November 2026** · Los Angeles County, CARES registry linked to patient care reports,
January 2021 to December 2022

> "Among 176 eligible pediatric OHCA cases... the median age was 10 months (IQR 3-51), and 162 (92%) had an
> initial non-shockable rhythm... Among 143 patients with non-shockable rhythms, EMS administered
> epinephrine before transport in 58 (41%) and fluids in 12 (8%). Among 13 patients with shockable rhythms,
> EMS delivered defibrillation before transport in 11 (85%). Bag-valve-mask (BVM) use was documented in 80
> (51%) patients, although its timing relative to scene departure could not be determined. Median on-scene
> time was 10 minutes (IQR 5-18). Ten patients (6%) survived to hospital discharge, including 6 (3%) with a
> favorable neurologic outcome."

**Interpretation — the 85% against 41% contrast is the finding, and the likeliest explanation is technical
rather than cognitive.** Defibrillation is a device action with an immediate machine prompt. **Adrenaline in
a ten-month-old during CPR requires intravenous or intraosseous access in an infant**, which is the hardest
procedure in paediatric resuscitation and the one most likely to fail or be deferred to the ambulance.
**The gap is not plausibly a failure to know that adrenaline is indicated; it is plausibly a failure to get
a line.** The 8% fluid figure points the same way, since fluids need the same access.

**The median age of ten months is the fact that organises everything else.** A predominantly infant,
92%-non-shockable population is an asphyxial arrest population, where ventilation matters most and the
shockable-rhythm pathway is nearly irrelevant — **and yet the shockable pathway is the one being executed
on scene 85% of the time.** The system is performing best on the intervention its patients least often
need.

**Which makes the bag-valve-mask number the most uncomfortable in the paper: documented in 51%, with
timing undeterminable.** In an asphyxial arrest population, assisted ventilation is the intervention. **The
paper cannot tell whether half these children went without documented bag-mask ventilation or whether half
the records simply do not record it, and it says so rather than choosing the flattering reading.** Either
answer matters: one is a care failure and the other makes this registry unable to measure the thing it most
needs to measure.

**Two bounds on the numbers.** **176 cases over two years in a county of roughly ten million** — paediatric
non-traumatic OHCA is rare, 13 shockable cases carry the 85%, and **the abstract gives no confidence
intervals**, so 11 of 13 should be read as "most, on thirteen patients". And **the 6% survival and 3%
favourable outcome should not be set against the adult figures this archive reported two days ago** from the
head-up CPR registry; an infant asphyxial arrest cohort and an adult cohort with 10% shockable presentations
are different diseases.

**The conclusion is appropriately narrow: "Further work is needed to identify barriers to timely delivery of
on-scene resuscitative interventions."** A descriptive study that identifies a gap and declines to explain
it is doing its job.
