## 1. TIVA versus volatile inhalational anaesthesia — 2,508 patients, and no difference

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA*, **1 September 2026** — **three weeks old, not new.** Recovered today;
see the run note. Indexed as a **Pragmatic Clinical Trial**, Multicenter, Randomized Controlled Trial
Jhanji, Booth, Hiller, Braun, Dunn, Eggleston, Fenton-May, Frempong, Gilchrist, Jacques, Jefford,
Kandiyali, Kaur, Mason, **Moonesinghe**, **Pearse**, Rao Baikady, Reynolds, **Shelley**, Sinclair,
Teare, Vindrola-Padros and colleagues

> **Importance.** Older adults undergoing major noncardiac surgery experience substantial
> postoperative morbidity and health care use. The **comparative effectiveness of total intravenous
> anesthesia (TIVA) vs volatile-based inhalational anesthesia on recovery and safety remains
> uncertain.**

Design: **pragmatic, multicentre, open-label randomised clinical trial in 49 UK NHS hospitals**,
January 2022 to April 2024, final follow-up October 2024. **Patients aged 50 or older scheduled for
elective major noncardiac surgery**, randomised 1:1 to maintenance with **TIVA (propofol infusion,
n=1254)** or **volatile-based inhalational agents (n=1254)**. All other perioperative care at
clinician discretion. Primary outcome: **days alive and at home at 30 days.**

> Among the **2508 randomized participants**, mean age 67 (SD 8.9), 55% male… **Days alive and at home
> at 30 days were similar between groups (mean 22.5 [SD 6.8] vs 22.4 [SD 6.6] days; incidence rate
> ratio 1.00; 95% CI, 0.99–1.02; adjusted P = .68).** There were **no differences in days alive and at
> home at 90 days; mortality at 30 days, 90 days, or 6 months; or Quality of Recovery-15 score at day
> 3.**

> **Lower rates of thirst, hoarseness, and nausea and vomiting were reported in the TIVA group.**
> Levels of delirium were similar, with the majority (**87.6%**) having **no delirium at day 3**. Major
> complications occurred in **12.4%** of patients overall, with no significant between-group
> differences. **Two cases of certain or probable unintentional awareness under anesthesia were
> reported, both in the TIVA group.**

> **Conclusions.** Among older adults undergoing major noncardiac surgery, **TIVA did not improve days
> alive and at home at 30 days** compared with inhalational anesthesia.

**Interpretation.** The most consequential paper this archive has carried, and it arrives three weeks
late through no fault of the journal.

**An incidence rate ratio of 1.00 with a confidence interval of 0.99 to 1.02 is about as null as
perioperative medicine gets.** Two and a half thousand patients, 49 hospitals, and the two techniques
are indistinguishable on days alive and at home, on mortality at every time point out to six months,
on quality of recovery, and on major complications. Decades of observational literature — TIVA and
cancer recurrence, TIVA and delirium, TIVA and inflammation — have been arguing about a difference
that a properly powered pragmatic randomised trial cannot find.

**It settles, from the other direction, the item this archive carried four days ago.** The
[20 September brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-20/) reported a 1,466-pair international
registry finding **volatile anaesthesia associated with higher donor morbidity, more major
complications, more blood loss and three times the biliary complications** in living donor
hepatectomy — and the interpretation written here was that the comparison was carrying centre
effects, because **all seven high-volume centres used TIVA exclusively or predominantly**, and
because a volatile agent causing three times the bile leaks is not a plausible mechanism.
**A randomised trial in 2,508 patients now finds nothing.** That does not prove the hepatectomy
association was confounding — different operation, different population, different outcome measure —
**but it is exactly what one would expect if it were**, and anyone citing that registry should cite
this alongside it.

**The two awareness cases deserve care rather than emphasis.** Two events, both in the TIVA arm, in
1,254 patients: this cannot be statistically significant and the trial does not claim it is.
**But the direction is mechanistically expected** — TIVA has no end-tidal agent concentration to
alarm on, so depth of anaesthesia is inferred rather than measured unless a processed EEG monitor is
used, and this was a pragmatic trial with depth monitoring at clinician discretion. It is a
hypothesis-consistent imbalance, not a finding, and it is the one result that should change what a
reader does: **if you use TIVA, the case for monitoring depth is not weakened by this trial.** That
sits directly against [yesterday's BJA study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-23/), which showed
BIS failing to register a clear cortical change for ten minutes.

**The symptom differences are the real patient-facing result and they are easy to undersell.**
**Less thirst, less hoarseness, less nausea and vomiting** with TIVA. A trial powered for days alive
and at home found nothing there and something here — and for a patient, postoperative nausea and a
sore, hoarse throat are most of what the day after surgery actually feels like. **Note too that
hoarseness appears as a TIVA benefit here**, four days after [the ten-year *Anesthesia & Analgesia*
series](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-23/) on postoperative hoarseness referred to ENT found the
nasogastric tube and TEE outranking the tracheal tube as associations.

**Two limits worth stating.** **Days alive and at home at 30 days is an excellent pragmatic endpoint
and an insensitive one** for a question about anaesthetic technique — it is dominated by the surgery
and the patient, and a technique effect would have to be large to move it. And **87.6% of patients
had no delirium at day 3**, which makes the delirium comparison uninformative rather than reassuring:
a rate that low leaves very little to detect a difference in.

## 2. Continuous glucose monitoring in the ICU fails exactly where it is needed

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology*, **21 September 2026** — **new
this week**. Prospective ancillary study of the DIRICU study
Seret, Dupuis, Alves de Araújo, Annicchiarico, Macedo, Pinto Teixeira Araujo, **Taccone**, Cnop,
Desaive, Uyttendaele, Preiser and colleagues

> Glycemic management in the ICU remains challenging because **glucose levels fluctuate rapidly and
> monitoring is intermittent.** Interstitial CGM could improve surveillance, but **its performance
> during rapid glucose excursions remains uncertain.**

**Twenty critically ill adults with stress hyperglycaemia, no prior diabetes**, expected ICU stay ≥5
days. **Dexcom G7** compared against **arterial blood glucose** over the first 10 ICU days, both
under stable conditions and **during intravenous glucose tolerance tests (IVGTT)**. A post hoc
**20-minute temporal shift** analysis was performed to account for delayed CGM peaks.

> **Outside IVGTT**, 716 paired values showed moderate agreement (**R² = .58, mean bias +11 mg/dL,
> MARD 17%**), with **98.9% of values in Clarke zones A/B.**

> **During IVGTT, accuracy deteriorated markedly** (1018 paired values): **R² = .028, mean bias −34
> mg/dL, MARD 24%, and only 80.3% of values in zones A/B, with 18.6% in zone D.**

> After temporal shifting, performance improved (**R² = .34, MARD 19%, 94.7% in zones A/B**).

Conclusion: acceptable accuracy when stable, deteriorating during rapid excursions; **hybrid
strategies combining CGM with intermittent blood glucose confirmation may be preferable.**

**Interpretation.** A small study that answers, almost to the day, the question raised by
[yesterday's guidelines entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-23/).

**That entry reported an international position statement on inpatient CGM, insulin pumps and
automated insulin delivery — explicitly limited to *non-critically ill* hospitalised adults, and I
noted that it declined exactly the setting where sensor accuracy is most doubtful. This is the
evidence for why.**

**The number to stop at is R² = 0.028.** During rapid glucose change, the sensor does not merely read
low — **it is essentially uncorrelated with arterial glucose.** A bias can be corrected; an R² of 0.03
means the reading carries almost no information about the value it is supposed to estimate. And
**18.6% of values in Clarke zone D** is not an abstract statistic: zone D is the region where the
monitor fails to detect a glucose level that requires treatment.

**The 20-minute shift identifies the mechanism, and it is reassuring about the device while damning
about the use case.** Shifting the CGM trace back by 20 minutes recovers most of the lost performance
— R² from 0.028 to 0.34, zone A/B from 80% to 95%. **The sensor is not broken; it is late.** That is
interstitial physiology, not a manufacturing defect, and no firmware will fix it. It also means the
failure is worst precisely when glucose is moving fast, which in an ICU is when insulin is running,
when feed stops, when a patient becomes septic — **the moments the monitor exists for.**

**Even the stable-condition performance is not as good as it sounds.** A **MARD of 17%** is well above
what is generally considered acceptable for a device used to make insulin decisions, and a mean bias
of **+11 mg/dL** in a population being managed toward a target is a systematic overestimate.

**Twenty patients, one sensor, one centre, no prior diabetes.** Small, and the authors' modest
conclusion — hybrid monitoring, CGM plus intermittent confirmation — is the right one and matches
what careful units already do.

## 3. Refractory VF — phenotypes rather than shock counts

**Journal · Published:** *Current Opinion in Critical Care*, **18 September 2026** — **six days old**.
A review, carried on this archive's outstanding list since Sunday
Babini, Cucino, **Ristagno**

> Refractory shockable cardiac arrest remains associated with poor outcomes despite prompt CPR and
> repeated defibrillation. This review summarizes recent evidence on **ventricular fibrillation
> phenotyping, vector-change defibrillation, double sequential external defibrillation (DSED),
> waveform analysis, and emerging decision-support approaches.**

> **Refractory VF is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous syndrome rather than a uniform
> failure of repeated shocks.** True shock-refractory or incessant VF, recurrent VF, and mixed
> phenotypes **differ in frequency, prognosis, and potential therapeutic targets.** **Vector-change
> defibrillation using an antero-posterior pad configuration may improve defibrillation efficacy in
> selected patients**, whereas **DSED represents a more complex intervention whose effectiveness may
> depend on shock vector, timing, inter-shock interval, and system-level implementation.**
> Physiological and electrical markers, including **amplitude spectrum area (AMSA), transthoracic
> impedance (TTI), and ECG-based predictive models**, may enable earlier identification of patients
> unlikely to respond to continued conventional defibrillation.

> **Current evidence does not support a single rigid treatment algorithm for refractory VF.** Future
> research should **move beyond shock-count-based escalation.**

**Interpretation.** The conceptual move here is worth more than any of the individual techniques.

**"Refractory VF" has been defined by a number — three shocks, or five — and this review argues the
number describes the treatment rather than the disease.** Splitting it into **truly shock-refractory
or incessant VF, recurrent VF, and mixed phenotypes** turns one label into three conditions with
different prognoses and different targets. A patient whose VF terminates with each shock and then
returns has a different problem from one whose VF never terminates, and counting shocks cannot
distinguish them.

**It pairs directly with the null result this archive carried on Tuesday.** The
[BASIC-IHCA registry comparison of lidocaine and amiodarone](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/)
found no difference between the two antiarrhythmics in in-hospital refractory VF. **If the drug
choice does not distinguish outcomes, the interesting variable is elsewhere** — and this review
argues it is in the shock: its vector, its timing, and which phenotype you are shocking.

**The honest part is the refusal to produce an algorithm.** *"Current evidence does not support a
single rigid treatment algorithm"* is what a review says when the evidence is genuinely unsettled,
and it is a contrast worth drawing with several documents this archive has covered in the last week
that produced confident structure on thin evidence. **AMSA and transthoracic impedance are, however,
squarely in the territory this archive keeps flagging**: measurements that predict responsiveness
without yet having a trial showing that acting on the prediction helps.
