## 1 and 2. Two papers that build a subgroup story and then refuse it

**These two landed in the same sweep and do the same unusual thing.** Each produces a pattern that looks like
effect modification — a result significant in one stratum and not another — **and each then runs the formal
interaction test, finds nothing, and reports the null rather than the pattern.** **That is the correct
analysis and it is rare enough to be the reason both are here.**

### 1. A revised organ-failure score: two-thirds of patients rescored, 0.032 of discrimination bought

**[Mortality Discrimination of SOFA-2 versus SOFA-1 in Older Patients with Sepsis: An Age-Stratified
Single-Centre Cohort Study](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10993609/v1)** · **preprint, Research Square** ·
**posted 1 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**972 consecutive patients aged 60 or over with sepsis**, single centre, 2020 to 2025, with SOFA-1 and SOFA-2
calculated at admission and discrimination compared by paired DeLong tests within age strata. **635 of them —
65.3% — were aged 85 or over**, which is an extraordinarily old sepsis cohort. From the abstract:

> SOFA-2 assigned a different total score from SOFA-1 in 65.3% of patients, with scores decreasing in 45.7%
> and increasing in 19.6%. Overall, SOFA-2 showed higher discrimination than SOFA-1 for ICU mortality
> (AUC 0.699 vs. 0.667), 28-day mortality (0.719 vs. 0.695), and 6-month mortality (0.686 vs. 0.671; all
> P < 0.05).

**The exchange rate is the finding.** **The revision changed the score in two patients out of three — 45.7%
down and 19.6% up, summing to the stated 65.3% — and bought 0.032 of area under the curve on ICU mortality,
0.024 at 28 days and 0.015 at six months.** **Rescoring 65% of a cohort to gain three hundredths of
discrimination is a poor exchange**, and the reason it registers as significant at all is the paired design in
972 patients. **This archive's rule from yesterday applies directly: significance without importance at large
N — and a paired comparison makes N effectively larger still.**

**All eighteen reported areas under the curve lie between 0.667 and 0.726.** **Neither version of the score
discriminates well in this population**, and the choice between them is being made inside a span of 0.059 at
the bottom of the useful range.

**Then the subgroup pattern, and the refusal.** In patients aged 85 or over, SOFA-2 was significantly better on
**all three** endpoints; in those aged 60 to 84, on **only one**. **That is exactly the shape that gets
reported as "the new score works better in the very old."** The authors instead tested it:

| ΔAUC difference between age strata | Estimate | 95% CI | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ICU mortality | 0.013 | −0.012 to 0.038 | 0.308 |
| 28-day mortality | 0.018 | −0.005 to 0.041 | 0.128 |
| 6-month mortality | 0.014 | −0.008 to 0.036 | 0.214 |

**All three null, all three intervals exactly symmetric, and all three straddling zero.** The authors' own
conclusion: *"These findings suggest, but do not establish, potential age-related heterogeneity."*

**This is the rule, and it is worth stating as a rule because it is the single most common error in subgroup
reporting: a difference in significance is not a significant difference.** Significant in one stratum and not
in another is a statement about two sample sizes — 635 against 337 here — and says nothing about whether the
effect differs. **Only the interaction test speaks to that, and here it says no.**

**Two further limits.** **Single centre, retrospective, and a cohort in which 65.3% are 85 or over** — which
makes it the right place to ask this question and the wrong place to answer it for a general ICU.
**And it is a preprint.** A numerical coincidence worth flagging so no one builds on it: **the proportion
rescored and the proportion aged 85 or over are both 65.3%, and they are unrelated quantities.**

**My reading.** **The connection to yesterday is the interesting part.** Yesterday's guidelines watch routed a
document proposing **SIC-2**, which *removes* the organ-failure score from the sepsis-coagulopathy criteria to
make them simpler and more specific. **Today's paper evaluates SOFA-2, which *revises* the same score to make
it predict better.** **Two 2026 revisions of the same instrument moving in opposite directions — one deleting
it from a definition, one refining it as a predictor — and this paper's numbers suggest the refinement is
worth about 0.03.**

### 2. Fluid volume after echocardiography: a strong association and no phenotype that modifies it

**[Fluid Resuscitation Volume After Early Echocardiography and Mortality in Sepsis: Target Trial Emulations
With Strictly Pre-Exposure Confounder Measurement in MIMIC-IV](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11247095/v1)**
· **preprint, Research Square** · **posted 1 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**7,819 Sepsis-3 ICU stays with transthoracic echocardiography within 24 hours of admission; the primary
emulation analysed 7,398.** Three target trials were emulated in one cohort, **with every confounder measured
strictly before the exposure window opened** — the design point the title makes and the one that does the
work. From the abstract:

> Post-echocardiography fluid volume ≥ 30 mL/kg was associated with higher 28-day mortality (HR 1.74, 95% CI
> 1.54–1.97; weighted risk 38.7% vs 24.8%; risk difference + 13.9%, 95% CI 10.8–17.0). The association was
> similar across LVEF strata … and across RV strata … No formal interaction test supported effect modification
> (LVEF: multiplicative p = 0.44, additive p = 0.56; RV: multiplicative p = 0.65, additive p = 0.16; LVEF
> modelled continuously: p = 0.82). A previously observed protective association in hyperdynamic hearts
> without cardiac disease was not confirmed when cardiac disease was ascertained from pre-admission diagnoses
> (HR 1.33, 0.90–1.97).

**38.7% minus 24.8% is the stated 13.9 percentage points**, and the hazard ratio of 1.74 has a log-symmetric
interval. **The association survived every sensitivity analysis the abstract lists**: within pre-echo severity
tertiles and septic-shock strata, at 20 and 40 mL/kg thresholds, as a continuous exposure at **1.14 per
10 mL/kg (1.11–1.17)**, with a six-hour window at 1.43, and after excluding pre-ICU studies at 1.64.

**The methodological upgrade destroyed a previously published subgroup result, and that is the paper's real
contribution.** A protective association of fluid in hyperdynamic hearts without cardiac disease had been
observed before. **Here, when cardiac disease was ascertained from diagnoses recorded at prior admissions
rather than the index one, it became 1.33 (0.90–1.97) — the wrong side of one.** **Ascertaining a baseline
covariate from the admission during which the outcome occurs is how a subgroup finding gets manufactured**,
and this paper shows the specific finding dissolving when that is fixed.

**The bias analysis is unusually complete and one number is worth translating.** **The E-value is 2.60, and
2.29 for the confidence bound.** In plain terms: **an unmeasured confounder would have to be associated with
both high fluid volume and death by a risk ratio of about 2.6, above and beyond everything already adjusted
for, to explain the result away.** **And the negative control outcomes — diagnoses from previous admissions,
which the exposure cannot possibly cause — indicated residual bias acting toward the null, not away from it.**

**Three cautions, two of them about the secondary emulations.** **Vasopressin initiation carried a hazard
ratio of 2.55 (2.13–3.06), and no one should read that as a drug effect.** Vasopressin is started when
noradrenaline is failing; this is confounding by indication of the purest kind, and the paper does not claim
otherwise — but the number will be quoted. **Dobutamine initiation rests on 100 new-users out of 1,201 and
came back at 1.57 (0.95–2.60)**, which is the count-the-events rule applying exactly: **8.3% treated, one
interval crossing one, nothing established.** **And the treated fractions are given for the two secondary
emulations and not for the primary one** — this archive's target-trial rule says state the treated fraction,
and for emulation 1 it cannot be stated from the abstract. **A smaller point: the four LVEF strata sum to
6,189 of 7,398, so 1,209 patients — 16.3% — are outside the ventricular stratification altogether**, and the
abstract does not say why.

**My reading.** **This is the most carefully built observational analysis this archive has read, and its
conclusion is a refusal.** Higher fluid after early echocardiography tracks with higher mortality, strongly
and everywhere, **and echocardiography does not identify a phenotype in whom the association reverses.** The
authors say the right thing: *"These results do not support phenotype-guided fluid restriction derived from
observational subgroup patterns."* **The clinically useful conclusion is not "give less fluid" — the design
cannot establish causation and the authors do not claim it — it is that the echo-guided personalisation story
has now failed its best observational test, and the question needs a randomised trial with protocolised
echocardiography.**

## 3. HERACLES: the balloon pump beats the micro-axial pump on physiology, in twenty-four patients

**[Hemodynamic Effects of Reducing LV Afterload With Impella or Intra-Aortic Balloon Counterpulsation During
Veno-Arterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation in Cardiogenic Shock: HERACLES
Trial](https://doi.org/10.1161/circinterventions.126.017625)** · *Circulation: Cardiovascular Interventions* ·
**published 31 October 2026** · paywalled; the abstract below is the published abstract

**A randomised mechanistic trial. Twenty-four patients on veno-arterial ECMO for cardiogenic shock**,
allocated to left ventricular unloading with an intra-aortic balloon pump or a micro-axial flow pump,
stratified by ischaemic or non-ischaemic cause, with distal coronary, aortic and ventricular pressures and
coronary flow measured with and without unloading.

**The population is as sick as this literature gets: 96% SCAI stage D or E, median ejection fraction 10%,
lactate 4.3 mmol/L, end-diastolic pressure 18 mmHg.** From the abstract:

> The myocardial oxygen supply-demand ratio improved with IABP but not mAFP (1.48 [1.23 to 2.10] vs 0.97
> [0.92 to 1.39]; P=0.036), driven predominantly by differential reduction of TTI. Coronary pressure increased
> but flow was unchanged with mAFP (DFR 1.00 [0.86 to 1.13]). Coronary flow increased marginally with IABP
> (DFR 1.16 [1.00 to 1.51]). LV pressures decreased with both devices and contractility with IABP only, with
> no between-group differences. Thirty-day mortality was similar (38% vs 45%, p=0.84).

**Why this is surprising.** **The field has been moving away from the balloon pump and toward micro-axial
support**, and this archive holds three prior entries on micro-axial devices. **A randomised trial finding
that the older, cheaper, less invasive device improves the myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance while the
newer one does not runs against that current**, and the mechanism the authors give is specific: the balloon
pump reduced the systolic pressure-time integral differentially, and raised coronary flow marginally, where
the micro-axial pump raised coronary pressure without moving flow at all. **Pressure without flow is the
distinction that matters, and it is the kind of thing only an invasive mechanistic trial can see.**

**What the trial does not establish, and the authors are explicit.** **The primary endpoint is a ratio of a
ratio, not an outcome.** **And the mortality comparison is uninformative by construction: 38% against 45% in
twenty-four patients is roughly five deaths against five, about ten in total** — this archive's standing rule
is to count the events behind an estimate before crediting it, and here there is nothing to credit or
discount. The authors' conclusion stops exactly where it should: *"Further research is needed to determine
whether these mechanistic differences translate to clinical outcomes."*

**One observation about the cohort that deserves a line.** **46% male.** Cardiogenic shock trials usually
enrol 70 to 80% men. **In twenty-four patients that is a difference of a few individuals and carries no
inference, but it should be noted rather than passed over**, because a reader comparing these haemodynamics
with a larger shock trial's is comparing differently composed groups.

**My reading.** **This is a good trial of the right question in the wrong size, and the right question is
mechanistic, so the size matters less than it would otherwise.** Twenty-four invasive coronary physiology
studies in SCAI D/E shock is a serious undertaking. **The finding to carry forward is not "use a balloon
pump" — it is that the two devices unload the ventricle by different routes, and that only one of them
improved the supply-demand ratio.** **Set beside the three counterpulsation items this archive has now logged
in three days** — external counterpulsation and vein graft patency yesterday, balloon-pump timing after
bypass grafting in today's sweep, and this — **counterpulsation is having a month, and the mechanistic case
for it is in better shape than its outcome case.**

## 4. A learning curve that could not be told from noise — and the disclosure that matters

**[Learning curve of da Vinci robotic lobectomy: a single-surgeon CUSUM analysis of operative time and
perioperative outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11701-026-04147-1)** · *Journal of Robotic Surgery* ·
**published 31 October 2026** · paywalled; the 5,063-character abstract is quoted below

**47 consecutive da Vinci robotic-assisted lobectomies by one board-certified thoracic surgeon**, September
2024 to July 2026, with wedge resections, video-assisted cases, segmentectomies and single-port cases
excluded. **Cumulative sum of operative time against the group mean, with a permutation test for decision
limits.**

**This is here because of yesterday, and the connection is exact.** **Yesterday's guidelines watch published
the EACTS expert consensus on robotic cardiac surgery minimum standards, written with EACTAIC and EBCP, whose
central mechanism is a society-driven database for learning-curve oversight.** **Today's paper is what
learning-curve oversight looks like when it is actually done — and the first thing it finds is that the curve
is not there.**

> The CUSUM curve for operative time peaked at case 20 (value 265.0), with a nearly equal local peak at case
> 10 (260.5); leave-one-out and smoothing analyses showed the identified peak was unstable between cases 10
> and 20, and the entire observed CUSUM path remained within the 95% permutation-derived decision-limit
> envelope (global p = 0.73), indicating that this descriptive breakpoint could not be formally distinguished
> from random case-to-case variation at this sample size.

**The method is the contribution.** **Cumulative-sum curves are published constantly with a breakpoint read
off the peak and no decision limits at all.** This paper built an empirical 95% envelope from 10,000
permutations of case order, **and the whole observed path sat inside it, at p = 0.73.** **The two candidate
peaks differ by 4.5 units — 265.0 at case 20 against 260.5 at case 10 — which is how little separates a
"learning curve of 20 cases" from one of 10 in this dataset.** **New rule for this archive: a cumulative-sum
breakpoint without decision limits is a description, not a finding, and should not be quoted as a case
number.**

**The operative-time result is null three ways.** Median 315.0 minutes early against 284.0 late,
**p = 0.186**; no monotonic trend with case sequence, **Spearman's rho 0.01, p = 0.93**; and the regression
coefficient for case sequence is **−0.03 minutes per case unadjusted and +0.12 fully adjusted**, both with
intervals spanning roughly three minutes either way. **A reader should note that the median difference of
31 minutes and the regression's −23.1 unadjusted are different estimators of the same quantity**, which is
ordinary but worth seeing stated.

**And then the authors did the thing that almost no learning-curve paper does: they tested whether the surgeon
was selecting easier cases.** **Charlson comorbidity index rose significantly with case sequence
(rho = 0.34, p = 0.02); malignant histology went from 65% to 89% and concomitant procedures from 5% to 19%,
both non-significant and both in the same direction; tumour size did not change.** **So complexity increased
while operative time did not** — which is the only route by which a learning effect could be inferred from
these data, and **adjustment for all of it moved the estimates by a few minutes and the authors still decline
to infer one.**

**The best sentence in the paper is a disclosure.** **By lobectomy case 20, the surgeon had already performed
39 robotic console cases** once segmentectomies, wedge resections and single-port cases are counted.
**So "case 20" is not the twentieth robotic operation; it is the twentieth of one kind, and the denominator a
reader would assume is wrong by a factor of nearly two.** **That is precisely the quantity a society registry
has to define before it can oversee anything**, and it is the practical gap in yesterday's EACTS document: a
competency pathway and an outcome database are only as good as the case count they index.

**Three limits, stated by the authors.** 47 patients and one surgeon; **secondary comparisons uncorrected for
multiplicity**, which matters because the one significant secondary — chest tube duration, p = 0.041 by
Mann-Whitney and p = 0.020 by log-rank — is the sort of result that survives in a summary and should not;
and **no patient in the series had pleural adhesions**, so the findings do not transfer to the adhesion cases
that drive operative time in practice.

**My reading.** **A negative learning-curve paper is more useful than a positive one, because the positive
ones are mostly unfalsifiable.** This series cannot distinguish its own breakpoint from random ordering, says
so, and publishes anyway. **What it leaves standing is narrow and real: chest tube duration improved,
complexity rose without operative time rising, and the surgeon's true robotic experience at the apparent
breakpoint was nearly double the headline case number.** **For anyone building the registry EACTS asked for
yesterday, the third of those is the design requirement.**

## 5. A named gap closed mid-run: half of RELIEF's survivors have chronic kidney disease

**[Perioperative intravenous fluid and chronic kidney disease: long-term follow-up of the Restrictive versus
Liberal Fluid Therapy in Major Abdominal Surgery (RELIEF) randomised
trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2026.05.010)** · *British Journal of Anaesthesia* · **published
23 June 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**This item exists because of a recovery made while running today's sweep, and the route is worth recording
before the content.** Two *British Journal of Anaesthesia* records on this exact subject sat in the archive's
named gaps, both abstract-free: a commentary titled *"absence of evidence or evidence of absence of long-term
effects"* and, in today's sweep, what appears to be its print sibling. **A title-stem search returned a third
record — volume 137, issue 2, published 23 June 2026 — carrying 1,793 characters.** **That is the second
consecutive day on which searching the title stem has recovered an abstract-free named gap**, after
yesterday's PROSPECT guideline. **The route is now established practice, and the gap is closed.**

From the abstract:

> The Restrictive versus Liberal Fluid Therapy in Major Abdominal Surgery (RELIEF) randomised trial found that
> a restrictive intravenous fluid regimen increased the risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) after major
> abdominal surgery. We examined whether AKI after restrictive fluid therapy increased the risk for chronic
> kidney disease (CKD). … The incidence of new or worse CKD ≥ stage 3 did not differ between 422/754 (56%)
> participants assigned to restrictive fluid therapy, compared with 409/766 (53.4%) participants who received
> liberal fluid therapy (adjusted odds ratio 1.13 [95% CI 0.91-1.41]).

**The reported finding is the null, and the finding is not the null.** 422 of 754 and 409 of 766 reconstruct
to 55.97% and 53.39%, matching the stated figures, for a difference of 2.6 percentage points and an odds ratio
whose log-symmetric interval implies p = 0.27. **So randomised fluid strategy did not change long-term kidney
outcome.** **But 831 of 1,520 patients — more than half of everyone followed up — had new or progressive
chronic kidney disease of stage 3 or worse within 48 months of major abdominal surgery.** **That number is the
news, and the abstract reports it only as the denominator of a non-difference.**

**The attrition has to be stated alongside it.** The original modified intention-to-treat population was
2,983; follow-up data were obtained for **1,670 (56.0%)**, and the primary analysis ran on **1,520 — so 1,463
patients, 49.0% of the trial, are absent from this analysis.** **A 56% CKD incidence measured in the half of
the cohort that could be traced is not a population estimate**, and patients lost to a four-year follow-up
after major surgery are not lost at random. **The direction of that bias is not obvious — the sickest may be
dead and untraceable, or the sickest may be the ones still in renal clinics and therefore easiest to trace —
and the abstract does not address it.**

**The authors label the analysis post hoc and that is the right label.** The original trial's result was that
restriction *increased* acute kidney injury; this asks whether that injury became chronic disease, and finds
no randomised difference. **It is a secondary question asked of a trial not designed for it, at half
retention, four to thirteen years after randomisation.**

**One small defect worth noting because it is in a published abstract.** The conclusion reads *"no difference
was found in the incidence of new or progressive CKD was not related to a restrictive perioperative fluid
regimen"* — a duplicated predicate that leaves the sentence without a single grammatical reading. **The
intended meaning is clear from the results and nothing turns on it, but an abstract is the part of a paper
most often read alone.**

**My reading.** **Set beside today's second item, the two together say something sharper than either
alone.** The target trial emulation found higher fluid volume strongly associated with higher mortality in
sepsis, in an observational design that cannot establish causation. **RELIEF is the randomised evidence on the
other side of the same question in a different population, and its long-term kidney outcome is flat.**
**Fluid strategy is a real exposure with real short-term renal consequences — RELIEF established that for
acute kidney injury — and four years later the randomised arms are indistinguishable.** **What is not flat,
and what nobody randomised, is the 56%.** **For a perioperative service the actionable item here is not a
fluid regimen; it is that more than half of these patients need renal follow-up they are probably not
getting.**
