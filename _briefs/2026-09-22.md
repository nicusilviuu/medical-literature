## 1. Ilofotase alfa for renal protection after cardiac surgery — negative

**Journal · Published:** *Intensive Care Medicine*, **21 September 2026** — **new this week**
Mourisse, **Ostermann**, Thiessen, Dumoulin, **Vlaar**, **Hoste**, Mosbahi, Schneider, **Zarbock**,
Boehm, Rosenberger, Thielmann, Dubois and colleagues

> Acute impairment of kidney function after cardiac surgery is common and associated with an
> increased morbidity and mortality. **Ilofotase alfa has demonstrated potential to attenuate renal
> injury through its immunomodulatory effects.**

Phase 2, multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, two-arm parallel group. Adults
at **high risk of renal impairment after on-pump cardiac surgery**, with a **pre-existing eGFR of
25–65 ml/min/1.73m²**, scheduled for complex cardiac surgery. **Two intravenous doses (128 mg)**
perioperatively.

Primary endpoint: **the ratio of the highest serum creatinine within five postoperative days to the
preoperative level (sCrRatio).** Secondary: **major adverse kidney events to day 60 (MAKE60).**

> In total, **244 patients were randomized of whom 204 received two doses** and were included: 109
> ilofotase alfa, 95 placebo. The mean±SD sCrRatio was **1.21±0.42** in the ilofotase alfa group
> compared to **1.27±0.50** for placebo (**p=0.31**). **MAKE60 incidence was 15.9% versus 15.4%
> (p=0.87). No safety concerns were raised.**

> Administration of ilofotase alfa **did not attenuate short- and longer-term renal function loss**…

**Interpretation.** A clean, well-conducted negative trial, and the author list is why it should be
believed — Ostermann, Zarbock, Hoste and Vlaar are much of the critical care nephrology field.

**This archive said in August that cardiac-surgery-associated AKI prophylaxis is *"a graveyard of
negative trials — RIPC, statins, NAC, bicarbonate, with PrevAKI bundles the partial exception."*
Add alkaline phosphatase to the graveyard.** That was written alongside
[the dapagliflozin trial on 24 August](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-24/), which did show an
acute renal signal — and the caveats recorded there (a 52% placebo AKI rate, nothing downstream
moving) look better founded with each new null result in the same space.

**The mechanistic story for ilofotase alfa was a good one**, which is what makes this informative
rather than merely disappointing. Ilofotase alfa is **human recombinant alkaline phosphatase**, and
the preclinical rationale — checked against the record rather than taken from this abstract, which
says only "immunomodulatory effects" — is that alkaline phosphatase **dephosphorylates extracellular
ATP, a pro-inflammatory danger signal released by injured cells**, and protected against kidney
ischaemia-reperfusion injury in mice
([*Frontiers in Medicine*, 28 July 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2022.931293), PMID 35966871).
It has already been through a **sepsis-associated AKI** programme, whose phenotype analysis appeared
in [*Critical Care*, 19 February 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-024-04837-y), PMID 38373981 —
that paper's premise being that *"there is no effective treatment for sepsis-associated AKI"* and
that it remained unclear which patients might benefit. **A second indication failing
on a hard endpoint after a promising mechanism is the normal shape of this field**, and the honest
conclusion is that **the intervention list for cardiac-surgery AKI is now nearly exhausted while the
problem is not.**

**One design point worth noting, in the paper's favour.** The primary endpoint is a creatinine
*ratio* — a continuous measure rather than a KDIGO stage threshold — which is a more sensitive
design than the categorical endpoints that have made earlier trials hard to read, and **it still
found nothing.** A null on a sensitive endpoint is stronger evidence of absence than a null on a
crude one. And MAKE60, the endpoint that matters, was identical to the decimal point.

**The 40 patients who did not receive both doses** (244 randomised, 204 analysed) are 16% of the
trial, and the abstract does not say why they were lost or how they were handled. In a trial this
size that is worth knowing; the full text was not reachable.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08596-y) · PMID 42766022

## 2. Lidocaine versus amiodarone in refractory VF/pVT — in-hospital arrest, and a tie

**Journal · Published:** *Chest*, **21 September 2026** — **new this week**
Niu, Jiang, Zheng, Song, Zheng, Wang, Wang, Zhao, Peng, Sun, Chen, Xu

The gap being filled, stated plainly:

> Current resuscitation guidelines recommend **either amiodarone or lidocaine** for refractory
> ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, **primarily based on out-of-hospital
> cardiac arrest evidence.** Whether the two agents differ in effectiveness for **adult in-hospital
> cardiac arrest** remains uncertain.

The **BASIC-IHCA registry** — nationwide, multicentre, prospective, Utstein-style, **40 hospitals
across China**, July 2019 to December 2020. Crucially:

> All eligible cases were **carefully adjudicated by reviewing medical records to ensure strict
> adherence to the recommended indications** for medication in the guidelines.

> Of **32,545 adult IHCA cases, 612 met inclusion criteria** (lidocaine 259; amiodarone 353).
> Unadjusted: survival **20.1% vs 23.5%** (P=0.31), sustained ROSC **47.9% vs 51.6%** (P=0.38),
> favourable neurological status **17.0% vs 21.0%** (P=0.22). After multivariable adjustment: survival
> **adjusted OR 1.22 (95% CI 0.63–2.37)**; sustained ROSC **1.21 (0.77–1.91)**; neurological outcome
> **1.44 (0.70–2.99)**.

Their conclusion: no significant differences, *"providing real-world evidence that supports guideline
recommendations allowing either antiarrhythmic drug during resuscitation."*

**Interpretation.** A genuinely useful negative, and the adjudication is what makes it one.

**The evidence behind the antiarrhythmic recommendation has always been out-of-hospital** — ALPS and
its predecessors — and in-hospital arrest is a different disease: witnessed, monitored, with a
shorter no-flow time and a very different aetiology mix. **Extrapolating a drug comparison across
that boundary has been an assumption rather than a finding, and this is the first serious attempt to
test it.**

**The adjudication step deserves emphasis because it is what registry studies usually skip.** Going
back into 32,545 records to confirm that the drug was actually given for a guideline indication
removes the commonest failure of this design — patients who got amiodarone for something else
entirely being counted as treated refractory VF. It is also why 612 of 32,545 survive to analysis.

**Read the confidence intervals rather than the p-values, and read them as what they are.** An
adjusted OR of 1.22 with an interval of 0.63 to 2.37 does not establish equivalence; it is
consistent with lidocaine being substantially better *or* substantially worse. **What the study
shows is that no large difference is apparent in this population, not that the two drugs are the
same.** With 612 patients and roughly a fifth surviving, this was never going to resolve a moderate
difference. The authors' framing — supporting guidelines that *permit either* — is the right claim,
and a stronger one than it sounds: **a guideline that offers a choice is only defensible if neither
option is clearly worse, and this is the first in-hospital evidence on that point.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.08.052) · PMID 42767592

## 3. Regret, and who gets ECMO

**Journal · Published:** *Chest*, **21 September 2026** — **new this week**. Qualitative secondary
analysis
Rubin, Jacobs, Siegal, Soled, **Kruser**, Segal

> **Veno-venous ECMO candidacy decisions are made under substantial uncertainty and are
> inconsistent.** With a team including **economist decision-scientists**, we applied **Regret
> Theory** to hypothesize that ECMO clinicians' **perceived past and anticipated regret** is an
> important influence on these decisions and contributes to inconsistency.

Secondary analysis of semi-structured interviews with **24 ECMO clinicians** from international
urban, rural, public and private centres, September to December 2024. Directed qualitative content
analysis by three investigators — **two physicians and one economist** — informed by Regret Theory.

Three themes:

> First, **clinicians prefer utility-based reasoning, but it is often not possible.** Second,
> **regret of omission and commission both influence candidacy decisions.** Finally, **clinicians
> employ various strategies to minimize past and anticipated regret.**

> We demonstrate that regret plays an influential role in how candidacy decisions are made, **which
> may partly explain known inconsistencies in candidacy decisions within centers.**

**Interpretation.** Twenty-four interviews is a small qualitative study, and it is the most
interesting thing in today's brief.

**The finding that matters is the first theme, and it is easy to skim past.** Clinicians *want* to
reason about expected utility — probability of survival, quality of that survival, opportunity cost
of the circuit — and **often cannot**, because the inputs do not exist for the patient in front of
them. What fills the vacuum is not randomness. It is **the memory of the last patient you cannulated
who should not have been, and the last one you declined who might have lived.**

**"Inconsistencies in candidacy decisions *within* centers"** is the sharpest phrase in the abstract.
Not between centres, where different populations and resources explain a lot — **within** a centre,
where the same team applying the same criteria reaches different answers on similar patients.
Regret asymmetry explains that better than any account based on evidence or protocol.

**This is the same structural problem this archive has been circling from other directions.** The
[ISHLT perioperative ECLS consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-14/) and the
[ECPR evidence](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-20/) both run into the fact that the selection
decision, not the technology, is what determines outcomes — and selection is exactly what neither a
consensus document nor a randomised trial has been able to specify. **A trial randomises patients
already judged candidates. The judgement that got them there is upstream of every result we have.**

**The methodological move is worth copying**: putting a decision-scientist economist in the coding
team and reading clinical interviews through Regret Theory. It produces a hypothesis that is
testable — if regret drives inconsistency, then structured decision aids and explicit outcome
feedback should narrow it — rather than a description of how hard the decisions feel.

*Access note: a qualitative study, so the themes above are the finding, not a summary of numerical
results. The supporting quotations are in the full text, which was not reachable.*

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.09.009) · PMID 42767598

## 4. Perioperative catastrophes — the same survey, thirteen years later

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **21 September 2026** — **new this week**
Margolis, Licatino, Adams, Higgins, Janosy, Patel, Dalton, Oloso, Staffa, Afonso, Vinson

> Perioperative catastrophes are sentinel events in anesthesiology with well-described emotional and
> professional consequences. **A landmark national survey published in 2012** demonstrated substantial
> and prolonged distress… Since 2012, anesthesiology practice has evolved within a healthcare
> environment characterized by **persistent workforce strain, heightened attention to clinician
> well-being, and expansion of peer-support programs.**

Voluntary anonymous electronic survey, 2025, to US-based ASA members who had opted in to research
surveys. **44 items, adapted from the 2012 questionnaire to preserve longitudinal comparability.**

> Of **1,725 respondents (response rate 7.1%)**, **94.2% reported involvement in at least one
> perioperative catastrophe** during their careers. Following a memorable event, **72.5% reported
> physical symptoms and 91.6% reported psychosocial symptoms.** Most perceived **impairment in
> ability to provide anesthesia care in the first 4 hours (69.0%) and 24 hours (61.5%)** after the
> event. **Nearly one-fifth (19.7%) reported never fully recovering emotionally, closely mirroring
> rates reported in 2012.** Although informal peer support was common, **formal debriefing and
> institutionally supported time off were infrequently provided. Nearly one-quarter of respondents
> reported considering leaving anesthesiology** as a result of a perioperative catastrophe.

**Interpretation.** The value here is entirely in the comparison, and the comparison is the bad news.

**Thirteen years of increased cultural awareness, peer-support programmes and well-being initiatives,
and the proportion who never fully recover emotionally is unchanged at about one in five.** That is
the finding. The authors state it carefully — *"closely mirroring rates reported in 2012"* — and it
should be read as a failure of intervention rather than a restatement of a known problem.

**The 4-hour and 24-hour impairment figures are the patient-safety finding, and they sit oddly beside
the rest of the archive.** Sixty-nine percent perceived impaired ability to deliver anaesthesia in
the four hours after a catastrophe, **and institutionally supported time off was infrequently
provided.** Those two sentences describe a system that knows its practitioners are impaired and keeps
them on the list. It is worth putting next to [the DAS 2025 guidelines covered
yesterday](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/), which devote a named section to **human
factors**: the human factor with the largest documented effect here is what happens to the
anaesthetist in the four hours after the last disaster.

**The 7.1% response rate is the limitation and it is a serious one.** A voluntary survey of people
who opted in to receive surveys, answering about emotional impact, will over-represent those with
something to say. **The absolute percentages should be treated as upper bounds.** What the design
does support is the *comparison*, since the 2012 survey had the same selection problem and the
instrument was deliberately preserved — **the stability of the 19.7% across thirteen years is more
trustworthy than the 19.7% itself.**

**An older reference point, reported here because it is the comparator rather than for its own sake**:
the 2012 national survey this replicates. Its finding was that distress after perioperative
catastrophe is substantial and prolonged. That remains true, and now it is documented as not having
changed.
