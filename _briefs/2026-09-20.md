## 1. Perioperative management of SGLT2 inhibitors

**Journal · Published:** *Diabetes & Metabolism*, **18 September 2026** — **new this week**.
Indexed as a **Review**
Ji, Zhang, Zhao, Zeng, Jiang, Zhang

Fifteen days after this archive reported the Basel cohort analysis finding that *stopping*
SGLT2 inhibitors before surgery tracked with more cardiac complications, a narrative review
arrives on exactly that question. Quoting the abstract closely:

> Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are established therapies for type 2
> diabetes, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease, creating a perioperative need to balance
> prevention of euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA) against cardiorenal protection.

On current practice and the evidence under it:

> Most guidelines recommend withholding SGLT2 inhibitors for **3-4 days before elective
> surgery**, although recommendations vary by agent, procedure, fasting duration, diabetes
> status, and monitoring capacity. Emerging evidence indicates competing perioperative risks
> and benefits. **Inadequate withholding and emergency surgery are associated with high-anion-gap
> metabolic acidosis and ketoacidosis, whereas observational studies associate preoperative use
> with lower risks of acute kidney injury and short-term mortality.** Heart failure withdrawal
> studies further suggest that **cardiovascular and volume-regulatory benefits may diminish
> rapidly after interruption.**

And the conclusion, which is carefully hedged in both directions:

> These findings **challenge uniform withholding but do not support routine perioperative
> continuation.** Management should be stratified by treatment indication, glycated haemoglobin,
> insulin dependence, hepatic and kidney function, infection and volume status, surgical urgency
> and invasiveness, expected fasting duration, and access to ketone and acid-base monitoring.

The research agenda it sets: define **indication- and risk-based withholding and restart
strategies**, and evaluate euDKA, AKI, worsening heart failure, myocardial injury, mortality and
postoperative recovery **jointly** rather than one at a time.

**Interpretation.** This is a review, not new data, and the reason to report it is that it shows
the field turning — in print — on a recommendation this archive covered a fortnight ago.

**The [5 September brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-05/) carried the Basel secondary
analysis**: 451 patients on chronic SGLT2i therapy, cardiac complications rising monotonically
with days stopped (1.7% continued, 5.7% at one day, 8.4% at two, 11.5% at three or more;
**adjusted OR 1.58 per discontinued day**), and **euglycaemic DKA in exactly one of 451** — in
someone who had stopped. This review reaches the same place from a different direction:
withdrawal studies in heart failure show the benefit going quickly, and observational data
associate preoperative *use* with less AKI.

**What it does not do is tell you to continue the drug, and it is right not to.** The
asymmetry in the Basel data — a large, dose-responsive cardiac signal against a single case of
euDKA — is exactly what confounding by indication produces if the sickest patients are also the
ones whose drugs get stopped longest. The review's answer is stratification, which is honest but
operationally demanding: it asks for HbA1c, insulin dependence, hepatic and renal function,
volume and infection status, fasting duration **and ketone monitoring availability** before a
decision that is currently made by a single blanket rule in a pre-assessment clinic.

**This is the second chronic medication in four days where the guidance is moving from a blanket
stop-rule to stratification** — the [GLP-1 receptor agonist guidance covered on 17
September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-17/) made the same move, from fixed withholding
intervals to risk assessment plus gastric ultrasound. Both drug classes were given blanket rules
when they were new, both rules were built on case reports, and both are now being unwound by
cohort data. The structural lesson is worth more than either drug: **a precautionary rule adopted
on case-report evidence acquires the authority of a guideline without ever acquiring the evidence
of one.**

The stratification tables — which would be the usable output — are in the full text, which was not

## 2. TIVA versus volatile anaesthesia in living donor hepatectomy — 1,466 donor-recipient pairs

**Journal · Published:** *Liver Transplantation*, **18 September 2026** — **new this week**
Lewis, Raptis, Kantsedikas, Prosperi, Raja, Fernandez, Spiro, and the LDLTregistry.org Collaborative

From the abstract:

> The choice between total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) and volatile anesthesia for living donor
> hepatectomy remains controversial, with limited evidence comparing their impact on donor and
> recipient outcomes.

**1,466 donor-recipient pairs**, September 2023 to February 2025, from the International LDLT
Registry. Primary outcome **donor morbidity by Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI)**; secondary
outcomes complications by Clavien-Dindo grade, blood loss, transfusion and biochemical markers.
Multivariable regression adjusted for Human Development Index, surgical approach and graft type,
with a **linear mixed-effects model carrying a random intercept for transplant centre.**

Results, quoted closely:

> TIVA was used for most donor hepatectomies (**82.8%**) but there was significant regional
> variation in its use (p<0.001). **All seven centers contributing more than 50 cases (50.3% of the
> total cohort) used TIVA exclusively or predominantly.**

> Use of volatile anesthesia was independently associated with **higher donor CCI scores
> (regression coefficient 1.69, 95% CI 0.29-3.09, p=0.018)**, **more major complications (6.3% vs.
> 1.2%, p<0.001)**, and **greater median blood loss (250 mL vs. 100 mL, p<0.001)**. TIVA was
> associated with **lower transfusion rates (6.0% vs. 11.6%, p=0.002)** and **fewer biliary
> complications (2.5% vs. 8.3%, p<0.001)**. **Recipient outcomes, including graft function and
> biochemical recovery, were comparable between groups.**

The authors call for **large-scale international multicentre randomised trials.**

**Interpretation.** The largest dataset anyone has on this question, and the authors have written
the caveat into their own results paragraph — which is to their credit and is the thing to read
first.

**All seven high-volume centres used TIVA exclusively or predominantly, and those seven centres
are half the cohort.** So the volatile group is drawn disproportionately from lower-volume centres,
and living donor hepatectomy is a procedure where volume and outcome are strongly linked. The
random intercept for centre is the right tool and it is doing real work, but a random intercept
adjusts for the *average* level of each centre's outcomes — it cannot separate the anaesthetic
from everything else that differs between a unit doing 200 donor hepatectomies and one doing 12,
when the anaesthetic is almost perfectly aligned with that split.

**The effect sizes make the same point from the other side.** A CCI coefficient of 1.69 is small —
the confidence interval nearly touches zero, and CCI runs 0 to 100. But **major complications 6.3%
versus 1.2%** is a five-fold difference, and **biliary complications 2.5% versus 8.3%** a
three-fold one. A volatile agent plausibly affecting hepatic blood flow and ischaemia-reperfusion
is a real mechanism; **a volatile agent causing three times the bile leaks is not**, and biliary
complications after donor hepatectomy are a surgical-technique outcome. That single row is the
clearest evidence in the paper that the comparison is carrying centre effects.

**The honest reading: TIVA is what the best centres in the world do for living liver donors, and
this registry documents that fact robustly.** Whether it is *why* they get better results is a
question this design cannot answer, which is presumably why the authors end by asking for a trial.
For a donor population — healthy volunteers, where the acceptable complication rate is as close to
zero as surgery gets — that question is worth the trial.

## 3. Heart rate variability before post-cardiac-arrest seizures

**Journal · Published:** *Journal of the American Heart Association*, **18 September 2026** —
**new this week**
Sung, Shieh, Kuo, Lee, Jao, Ong, Chen, Jaw, Huang, Chen, Chang

> Seizures are common after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and may exacerbate secondary brain
> injury, yet early prediction remains challenging. Although heart rate variability has been used
> to predict seizures in epilepsy, its role and **optimal prediction window** after cardiac arrest
> remain unclear.

Method: adults resuscitated from non-traumatic OHCA at one academic post-arrest centre, with
**continuous ECG and EEG**, seizures confirmed independently by a neurologist. Five-minute
time-, frequency- and nonlinear-domain HRV features from **0 to 30 minutes before seizure onset**,
normalised to baseline, fed to a **support vector machine** with training, validation and test
sets.

> Among **36 patients**, 20 (56%) experienced **93 seizures**. Model performance was highest **15
> to 20 minutes before seizure onset**. At 18 minutes, the model achieved an **area under the
> receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.812 (95% CI, 0.792-0.832), 76% accuracy, 77%
> sensitivity, and 68% specificity.** Compared with baseline, **low frequency increased 3.28-fold,
> sample entropy 2.58-fold, high frequency 2.43-fold, and the low/high frequency ratio 1.47-fold**
> in seizure segments; corresponding ratios in nonseizure segments remained near baseline.

**Interpretation.** Genuinely interesting physiology, and a claim that should not yet be read as a
monitor.

**The autonomic finding is the substance**: low-frequency power more than tripling and sample
entropy more than doubling in the eighteen minutes before a seizure, with non-seizure segments flat.
That is a real and sizeable signal, and it says something about how post-arrest brain injury and
autonomic outflow are coupled that is worth knowing independently of any prediction algorithm.

**The prediction claim rests on 36 patients and 93 seizures, and the confidence interval gives the
architecture away.** An AUROC of 0.812 with a 95% CI of 0.792 to 0.832 is a *two-point* interval —
far tighter than 36 patients could ever support. That precision comes from counting five-minute
**segments**, not patients: with 93 seizures and 30 minutes of pre-ictal recording each, there are
thousands of segments, and segments from the same patient are not independent observations. Whether
the train/validation/test split separated **patients** or only segments is not stated in the
abstract, and it is the single most important methodological fact about the paper. If it did not,
the model has partly been tested on the patients it was trained on.

**This is the fifth measurement-and-prediction paper this archive has covered in eleven days** —
the [Hypotension Prediction Index](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-10/) (10 September), [low
intraoperative end-tidal CO₂ as an independent mortality marker](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-11/)
(11 September), the [analgesia nociception index](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-16/)
(16 September), and [AI-assisted nerve tracking](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-19/)
(19 September). The thread running through all five is the same:
**the measurement is real, the prediction is plausible, and nobody has shown that acting on it
changes anything.** Here the gap is starker than usual, because these patients already have
continuous EEG — which does not predict the seizure eighteen minutes out, but does detect it when it
happens, in the population where it is already standard. A predictor is only worth having if there
is something to do with the warning, and **no trial has shown that pre-emptive treatment of
post-arrest seizures improves outcome.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1161/jaha.126.050349) · PMID 42757915

## 4. Older but relevant: what the pooled ECPR randomised evidence actually says

**Journal · Published:** *Critical Care*, **3 July 2024** — **two years old, not new**, cited 18
times. Reported here because a **review published on 18 September 2026** characterises it, and the
characterisation is worth checking
Heuts, Ubben, Kawczynski, Gabrio, Suverein, Delnoij, Kavalkova, Rob, Komárek, van der Horst,
Maessen, **Yannopoulos**, **Bělohlávek**, Lorusso, van de Poll

The new review is **"Beating the clock: time-targeted and individualized extracorporeal
cardiopulmonary resuscitation"** (*Current Opinion in Critical Care*, 18 September 2026; Gottula,
Rees, **Yannopoulos**), and its recent-findings paragraph opens:

> **Pooled randomized data confirms a clinically meaningful survival advantage for ECPR in
> refractory shockable out-of-hospital cardiac arrest**, while observational cohorts show a steep
> fall in neurologically favorable survival as low-flow time lengthens beyond 30 min.

The pooled data is the 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis above, written by the investigators of the
trials themselves — its stated rationale being that frequentist analysis had produced *"a
dichotomous interpretation of results based on p-values rather than in the probability of clinically
relevant treatment effects."* What it found, quoted:

> Three randomized trials were included in the analysis (**ECPR, n = 209 patients; conventional
> CPR, n = 211 patients**). The estimated median relative risk of ECPR for 6-month neurologically
> favorable survival was **1.47 (95%CrI 0.73-3.32)** with a mean absolute risk difference of 8.7%
> (-5.0; 42.7%) in patients with all rhythms, and the median relative risk was **1.54 (95%CrI
> 0.79-3.71)** with a mean absolute risk difference of 10.8% (95%CrI -4.2; 73.9%) in patients with
> shockable rhythms. **The posterior probabilities of an absolute risk difference > 0% and > 5% were
> 91.0% and 71.1% in patients with all rhythms and 92.4% and 75.8% in patients with shockable
> rhythms.**

The authors' own conclusion ends: *"These results must be interpreted within the context of the
reported credible intervals and varying designs of the randomized trials."*

**Interpretation.** Read the two documents together and the word doing the work is **"confirms."**

**What the pooled analysis establishes is a three-in-four probability that the benefit exceeds 5%
absolute, from 420 randomised patients in total, with credible intervals spanning 0.79 to 3.71.**
That is a genuinely useful way to express the evidence — considerably more useful than three
individually null-or-positive trials — and it is a fair basis for saying benefit is **more likely
than not**. It is not confirmation. A 75.8% posterior probability of a clinically relevant effect
is, read the other way, a **roughly one-in-four chance there is no clinically relevant effect**,
and the credible interval includes meaningful harm.

**The overlap in authorship is the reason to flag this rather than a reason to dismiss it.**
Yannopoulos is an author of the ARREST trial, of the pooled analysis, and of the review that now
describes the pooled analysis as confirmatory. Every step is legitimate — the trialists are the
people who should be pooling their own data, and they preregistered it. But the language hardens at
each stage, and by the review it reads as settled in a way the credible intervals do not support.

**This corrects the archive's own framing too.** The [1 September
brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-01/) said the ECPR trial literature *"has never resolved
into a consistent benefit"* — accurate about consistency across the three trials, but it understated
what the pooled Bayesian estimate shows. The fair statement is narrower than either: **ECPR in
refractory shockable OHCA is probably beneficial, the probability is around three in four, the
magnitude is very uncertain, and it has only ever been tested in systems that could deliver it
fast.** That last clause is the review's real contribution — its argument that benefit is steeply
time-dependent and that the delivery system, not the therapy, is what needs prospective testing.
That is the right next trial, and it sits directly alongside the [ISHLT perioperative ECLS
consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-14/).
