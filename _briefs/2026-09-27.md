## 1. Caudal versus penile block and fistula after hypospadias repair

**Journal · Published:** *Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine*, **11 September 2026** — **sixteen days
old.** Multicentre randomised **non-inferiority** trial; NCT02861950
Taenzer, Mandler, Gagliardi, Brockel, Vemulakonda, Pestieau, **Polaner** and the **Pediatric Regional
Anesthesia Network (PRAN)** investigators

The question exists because of a retrospective literature:

> **Retrospective studies report conflicting associations between caudal block and urethrocutaneous
> fistula (UCF) formation after hypospadias repair, often without control for hypospadias severity.**

Children **≤2 years** undergoing **primary single-stage midshaft or distal hypospadias repair** at
**16 PRAN centres**, randomised to caudal or penile block with general anaesthesia. Primary outcome
**UCF within 3 postoperative months.**

> Of **210 subjects, 161 (76.6%) completed 3-month follow-up** (caudal 79; penile 82). **UCF occurred
> in 7/79 (8.9%) caudal and 6/82 (7.3%) penile patients (RR 0.98; 95% CI 0.35 to 2.76; p=0.9).
> Proximal meatal position was associated with increased UCF risk (RR 2.8; 95% CI 1.48 to 5.30;
> p=0.002).** Caudal received **less intraoperative (33% vs 56%, p<0.001) and rescue opioid (4.9% vs
> 15.7%; p=0.01)** and had **lower mean PACU FLACC scores (0.49±1.34 vs 1.14±2.3; p=0.03).**

> **In this early terminated randomized trial**, no statistically significant difference in UCF
> formation was observed… **However, incomplete enrollment, loss to follow-up and wide CIs limit
> precision, and clinically important differences between the techniques cannot be excluded. The
> findings should be considered exploratory.**

**Interpretation.** A careful trial about a scare that should never have reached the strength it did,
and the most useful number in it is a secondary outcome.

**The secondary finding explains the primary literature.** **Proximal meatal position carried a
relative risk of 2.8 for fistula.** Proximal hypospadias is the more severe disease, the longer
operation, and — plausibly — the case more likely to receive a caudal rather than a penile block.
**That is a textbook confounder, and the abstract says the retrospective studies were "often without
control for hypospadias severity."** So the association between caudal blocks and fistulae was very
likely an association between *difficult hypospadias* and fistulae, with the block along for the ride.

**But the trial cannot close the question, and the authors are explicit about that.** A relative risk
of **0.98 with a confidence interval of 0.35 to 2.76** is compatible with the caudal block roughly
tripling the fistula rate. **In a non-inferiority trial, that interval establishes nothing** — and the
trial **stopped early** with 210 of its intended enrolment and **23.4% lost to follow-up** on a
three-month surgical endpoint. **The honest summary is that the confounder has been identified and the
trial is underpowered**, which is a real contribution and not the same as exoneration.

**The analgesic result is the part that should change practice, and it is consistent across three
measures**: less intraoperative opioid, a third as much rescue opioid, and lower PACU pain scores.
Those are the outcomes the trial was adequately powered to detect, and they favour the caudal block.
**Given that the harm signal rests on confounded retrospective data, and the benefit is measured in a
randomised comparison, the balance of evidence has moved — without the trial having proven what it set
out to prove.**

**Worth noting how the scare propagated**, because it is the general lesson: a retrospective
association, repeated, becomes a reason for caution; caution becomes practice; and it takes a
16-centre randomised trial, terminated early, to establish only that the original signal was probably
confounding. **The 2.8 for proximal meatus was available in any dataset that had bothered to record
severity.**

## 2. Arrest in hospital on Impella does worse than arrest outside it

**Journal · Published:** *Resuscitation*, **11 September 2026** — **sixteen days old**
Arai, Kojima, Fukamachi, Okumura and the **J-PVAD** investigators

**8,577 patients** with cardiogenic shock treated with Impella, in the Japanese J-PVAD registry,
classified into four arrest presentations: **in-hospital cardiac arrest (n=1,924)**, **no cardiac
arrest (n=4,595)**, **out-of-hospital arrest with ROSC (n=1,321)**, and **OHCA without ROSC (n=737)**.
Primary outcome **30-day mortality from first Impella initiation**; multivariable Cox models and
restricted cubic splines.

> Thirty-day mortality was **48.1% in IHCA, 28.7% in no cardiac arrest, 41.9% in OHCA with ROSC, and
> 62.5% in OHCA without ROSC** (P<0.001). Compared with no cardiac arrest, **IHCA was associated with
> higher mortality (adjusted HR 1.54; 95% CI 1.41–1.69)** and **higher rates of major bleeding,
> Impella-related bleeding, limb ischaemia, ischaemic stroke, and sepsis.**

> Within IHCA, **older age, lactate >4.0 mmol/L, creatinine >1.5 mg/dL, longer arrest time, and
> venoarterial ECMO use** were associated with higher mortality, whereas **myocarditis and electrical
> defibrillation** were associated with lower mortality. **Spline analyses showed nonlinear
> associations for lactate, creatinine, and arrest time.**

**Interpretation.** One ordering in that results paragraph is counterintuitive and it is the whole
paper.

**In-hospital arrest did worse (48.1%) than out-of-hospital arrest with ROSC (41.9%).** Every instinct
says the reverse: an in-hospital arrest is witnessed, monitored, and reached in seconds by people
already treating the patient. **The explanation is that these are not the same disease.** An
out-of-hospital arrest that achieves ROSC has selected for a recoverable rhythm and a survivable
insult. **An in-hospital arrest in a patient already in cardiogenic shock is what progressive
haemodynamic collapse looks like when it happens despite being in hospital, on treatment, being
watched.** It is not an event; it is the endpoint of a trajectory that escalation failed to arrest.

**That reading is supported by what predicted death within the IHCA group**: lactate above 4,
creatinine above 1.5, and longer arrest time — **metabolic debt, organ injury and low-flow duration,
which are all measures of how long the decline ran before the arrest.** And the two protective
factors point the same way: **myocarditis** is a potentially reversible aetiology, and **electrical
defibrillation** means a shockable rhythm.

**The VA-ECMO finding must not be read as harm, and the paper's design cannot distinguish.** In a
registry, ECMO is added to the patients who are failing on Impella alone, so **ECMO use marks the
severity that prompted it.** This archive has made the same point about antithrombin concentrate and
about vasopressors and gangrene; it applies identically here.

**The complication burden is the practical finding.** More major bleeding, more Impella-related
bleeding, more limb ischaemia, more stroke, more sepsis in the IHCA group — **a device inserted into a
patient who has just arrested carries more harm than the same device inserted electively**, which is
the same structure as [the planned-versus-standby V-A ECMO
finding](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-25/) reported two days ago. **Both say that the timing of
mechanical support relative to collapse matters more than the device.**

**And it feeds the ECMO-selection thread directly.** The [regret study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/)
showed candidacy decisions driven by anticipated regret because utility-based reasoning was
unavailable. **This paper supplies some of the missing utilities** — 48% thirty-day mortality after
IHCA on Impella, with lactate, creatinine and arrest duration as gradable modifiers. That is what a
risk-stratification tool would need, and the authors say so.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2026.111310) · PMID 42727697

## 3. Endovascular resuscitation — REBOA, selective aortic arch perfusion, ECPR

**Journal · Published:** ***Emergency Medicine Journal***, **18 September 2026** — **nine days old.**
An expert practice review
Adams, Barratt, **Manning**, Rees, Singer, Barnard

> **Endovascular resuscitation (EVR)** describes a collection of novel therapies that aim to **bridge
> patients in shock states or cardiac arrest to definitive treatment**, through a combination of
> **haemorrhage control and mechanical circulatory support.** The last half-decade has seen landmark
> trials report on several techniques and **adoption is growing despite controversies around risk,
> efficacy and implementation.**

Built around **case scenarios of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and non-compressible haemorrhage**.
It reviews the rationale, then **"the critical initial step of vascular access"**, then the physiology,
technique, efficacy and safety of **three escalating interventions: resuscitative endovascular balloon
occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), selective aortic arch perfusion (SAAP), and extracorporeal
cardiopulmonary resuscitation.**

**Interpretation.** A teaching document rather than new evidence, and it is worth reporting for the
middle of its three interventions.

**Selective aortic arch perfusion is the one most readers will not know**, and it connects to a seam
this archive opened on 19 September. SAAP occludes the descending aorta and perfuses the arch — and
therefore the brain and coronaries — directly. **The [aortic arch and cerebral protection
seam](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-19/) was opened by a study showing carotid perfusion
cannula resistance varying three- to four-fold with no relation to French size**, and the open question
recorded there was whether any guidance exists on cerebral perfusion thresholds. **SAAP is the same
physiological problem arriving from the resuscitation side rather than the cardiac-surgery side**, and
Manning's presence in the author list is notable, since he is associated with the technique's
development.

**"The critical initial step of vascular access" is the sentence that decides whether any of this
happens.** Large-bore arterial access in a patient in arrest or exsanguinating is the rate-limiting
step, and it is a skill rather than a device. **That matches the planned-versus-standby ECMO paper
almost exactly** — there, equipment and vascular access prepared in advance was the whole content of
the "standby" strategy, and 61% of those patients ended up needing it.

**The caveat is the review's own, and it is appropriately placed in the first paragraph**: adoption is
growing *despite* unresolved controversies about risk, efficacy and implementation. **This is a
document teaching physicians to understand techniques whose benefit is not established**, which the
authors justify on the grounds that emergency physicians should be able to advocate for patients who
might benefit and be engaged in implementation. **That is a defensible position and it is not the same
as an endorsement** — and a reader should not take a practice review as evidence that these work.

*A correction to this archive's own record: this review was logged on the outstanding list as being in
***Current Opinion in Critical Care***. **It is in the *Emergency Medicine Journal***, and the
outstanding list has been corrected. The error came from logging it from a sweep line alongside two
genuine *Current Opinion* papers without re-checking the journal.*

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1136/emermed-2025-215376) · PMID 42062049
