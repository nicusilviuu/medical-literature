## 1. and 2. One trial, two papers, two null results

**PITSTOP-Fluids and PITSTOP-Antibiotics are not two trials.** They are one multicentre two-by-two
factorial randomized trial run by four paramedic services and their destination hospitals in Ontario,
enrolled between 23 March 2020 and 20 August 2025 with final follow-up in December 2025, **reported as two
papers under the single registration `NCT03068741`.** Each paper states that patients were additionally
randomized in the other factor and that its analysis is reported separately.

Both enrolled adults with suspected infection, fever of 38.0 degrees or more, and a systolic pressure below
100 mmHg.

**The fluids question.** Liberal fluids, up to 2 L of normal saline regardless of subsequent systolic
pressure, against usual care, fluids only when systolic pressure fell below 90 mmHg and stopped at 100.
**1,686 randomized, 1,669 eligible, 1,646 in the primary analysis.** Median age 76. Separation was achieved:
a bolus went to 826 of 848 in the liberal group and 700 of 821 in the usual-care group, and the volumes were
a median of 1,500 mL against 500 mL. **Hospital mortality 149 of 830 (18.0%) against 149 of 816 (18.3%),
risk ratio 0.98 (95% CI 0.80 to 1.20), P = .82.** Identical numerators, different denominators. **All 17
secondary outcomes were similar**, and pulmonary oedema during transport was infrequent in both arms.

**The antibiotics question.** One gram of intramuscular ceftriaxone against placebo in the ambulance, with
open-label antibiotics available to everyone in the emergency department. **2,091 randomized, 2,048 eligible
for the primary analysis.** Study drug went in at a mean of 37 minutes from paramedic arrival against 39 in
the placebo arm, while hospital antibiotics followed at a mean of 247.3 and 252.8 minutes. **So the
intervention moved the first antibiotic forward by roughly three and a half hours**, which is the whole
hypothesis, and **hospital mortality was 182 of 1,009 (18.0%) against 186 of 1,011 (18.4%), risk ratio 0.97
(95% CI 0.81 to 1.17), P = .77.** All 17 secondary outcomes similar.

**What the pair establishes, and what it does not.** Three and a half hours of earlier antibiotic in a
hypotensive febrile population moved nothing measurable, and nor did an extra 910 mL in the ambulance. **The
honest limit is the population: suspected sepsis identified by paramedics, median age 75 to 76, 18% hospital
mortality. That is not the septic shock cohort the early-antibiotic literature is usually argued over**, and
a null result in this population does not transfer to a patient already hypotensive on vasopressors in an
emergency department.

**Three arithmetic notes, reported as printed.** First, **the antibiotics paper's mortality denominators are
28 patients short of its own analysis set**: 1,009 plus 1,011 is 2,020 against the 2,048 stated as eligible.
Second, its hospital-antibiotic denominators are smaller again, 795 and 792 against randomized groups of
1,026 and 1,022, with no explanation in the abstract. Third, **the fluids paper's reported volume difference,
910.1 mL (95% CI 857.3 to 962.9), is not the difference between the medians it quotes**, which is 1,000 mL;
it is evidently a difference in means reported alongside medians.

**And the two papers report different randomized totals for the same factorial trial**, 1,686 against 2,091.
**The plausible reconciliation is in the eligibility criteria**: the fluids paper requires obtainable
intravenous access and the antibiotics paper does not, so a patient without access could be randomized to
intramuscular ceftriaxone but not to a fluid strategy. **Neither abstract states this**, and it is offered
here as the explanation the criteria imply rather than one the papers make.

## 3. FISSH, and seven years for a difference of 0.6 points

Ringer's lactate against 0.9% saline for all bolus and maintenance fluid through the ICU stay to day 30, in
adults aged 16 or over with septic shock at **30 academic and community hospitals across Canada and Saudi
Arabia**. Blinded to bedside clinicians, patients, caregivers, research staff and biostatisticians.

Entry required a systolic pressure below 90 mmHg or a mean arterial pressure below 65 after a litre, or a
lactate above 4 mmol/L, with suspected infection, within six hours of emergency department presentation or
ICU consultation.

**Screened 2,457, randomized 1,113, ten excluded after randomization: 557 to Ringer's lactate and 546 to
saline.** Mean APACHE II 24.7, mean baseline lactate 4.8 mmol/L, mean creatinine 176.5 micromoles per litre,
and a mean of 5,844 mL of study fluid per patient. **30-day in-hospital mortality 187 of 557 (33.6%) against
180 of 546 (33.0%), relative risk 1.02 (95% CI 0.86 to 1.21), p = 0.80.**

**Two things worth saying about the trial rather than the result.** Enrolment ran from 6 October 2018 to
1 February 2026 — **seven years and four months across 30 sites for 1,113 patients**, about 13 patients a
month in total. And **the paper states that there were no serious adverse events**, in a trial where a third
of the patients died. **Reported as printed**; it can only mean none attributed to the allocated fluid, but
the sentence as written sits oddly next to a 33% mortality rate.

## 4. The meta-analysis that called both fluid results eight days early

A domain-specific systematic review and meta-analysis of adult sepsis and septic shock randomized trials
published between 2000 and 2025, registered as PROSPERO CRD420261289221. **62 trials, 36,739 patients, six
treatment domains, and no mortality benefit in any of them:**

| Domain | Pooled risk ratio | 95% CI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial resuscitation interventions | 0.88 | 0.77 to 1.01 |
| Intravenous fluid quantity | 0.96 | 0.88 to 1.05 |
| Intravenous fluid type | 0.95 | 0.89 to 1.01 |
| Vasopressors | 0.97 | 0.88 to 1.07 |
| Corticosteroids | 0.92 | 0.84 to 1.00 |
| Vitamin C-based therapies | 0.95 | 0.83 to 1.09 |

**This appeared on 17 November. The three trials above appeared on 24 November.** Two of its six domains are
exactly what two of them tested, and **both new results sit inside the pooled intervals that already
existed**: fluid quantity pooled at 0.96 (0.88 to 1.05) against PITSTOP-Fluids at 0.98 (0.80 to 1.20), and
fluid type pooled at 0.95 (0.89 to 1.01) against FISSH at 1.02 (0.86 to 1.21).

**The paper's own explanation for the pattern is a design argument, not a pharmacological one.** Its
landscape analysis found wider dispersion of effect estimates among smaller trials while larger trials
clustered near the null — the classic shape — and its structured thematic analysis named recurrent
constraints across every domain, among them delayed intervention timing and biologic and clinical
heterogeneity. **Delayed intervention timing is precisely what PITSTOP-Antibiotics set out to fix, by moving
the first dose three and a half hours earlier, and it changed nothing.** That is the most interesting thing
on this page: the week's own best explanation for two decades of null results was tested in the same week
and did not hold.

**One figure to read carefully: corticosteroids at 0.92 with an upper bound of exactly 1.00.** A domain
summarised as showing no benefit rests there on a boundary, and the paper's framing of all six domains as
null is reported here as the paper's, not endorsed.

## 5. A counterweight, and an interval that cannot be right

Retrospective registry analysis across **four academic centres with established cardiac arrest programmes**,
2021 to 2024, in survivors of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest with a Glasgow Coma Scale motor score below 6,
all treated with hypothermic temperature control between 33 and 36 degrees using a feedback-controlled
device. A temperature control quality score from 0 to 6 was assigned per patient.

**356 patients.** 224 male (62.9%), mean age 58.1. **262 (73.6%) had a non-shockable arrest and 225 (63.2%)
received no layperson CPR** — a sick cohort, with a median Pittsburgh Cardiac Arrest Category of 4, a mean
lactate of 9.1 mmol/L and a mean pH of 7.09. Median pre-induction time 2.3 hours; 150 (42.1%) had early
neuromuscular blockade.

**Adjusted, each one-point increase in the quality score was associated with improved survival to hospital
discharge, odds ratio 1.32 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.64)**, and the direction of effect was consistent across all
four centres.

**The second result is printed as odds ratio 1.28 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.05 to 1.55, and that
cannot be right.** A lower bound of 0.05 with a point estimate of 1.28 would be an interval spanning more
than an order of magnitude below the estimate and barely above it, and it would not support the sentence it
is attached to, which describes a shift towards improved Cerebral Performance Category. **Almost certainly
1.05 is meant. It is reproduced here exactly as published, and the conclusion drawn from it is not endorsed
on the strength of the interval as printed.**

**The authors state the main limitation themselves**, that confounding by site, injury severity or perceived
prognosis is possible — and in a study where the exposure is how well a team executed temperature control,
perceived prognosis is the confounder that matters most: a patient believed to be unsalvageable receives
less exacting care.

## Also in today's sweep, not given a slot

**A dose-response meta-analysis of PEEP and the injured brain** (`10.1186/s13054-026-06294-1`, Critical
Care) is the strongest methodological work in today's 356 and is held back only because three sepsis trials
and their meta-analysis belong together. 37 studies of 4,471 records; in the within-patient layer of 410
patients, **intracranial pressure rose 0.118 mmHg per cmH2O of PEEP**, which is 0.59 mmHg per 5 cmH2O and
**entirely below the pre-anchored minimal important difference of 2 mmHg**, linear across 3 to 21 cmH2O.
Cerebral perfusion pressure fell 0.177 mmHg per cmH2O, **less than a fifth of its 5 mmHg threshold.**
Certainty low for both; brain-tissue oxygenation not estimable. **The useful form of the answer is not that
PEEP is safe for the injured brain but that its average effect is smaller than the smallest difference
anyone agreed in advance would matter**, with the caveat that evidence above 15 cmH2O came from two contrasts
in a single study.

because all five sit in journals that were already on the list. **A recall test guards only the journals its
own fixtures name.**

### Two findings about per-day counts, one of them new to this archive

**Per-day counts are not monotonic.** Three settled past days each hold fewer records today than yesterday:

| Day | Yesterday | Today | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 21 November | 7,851 | 7,841 | −10 |
| 22 November | 428 | 425 | −3 |
| 23 November | 5,335 | 5,239 | −96 |

**That is 109 records gone from three days that were already complete**, and this archive has not recorded
it before. The 20 November investigation specifically refuted index shrinkage, with two stable re-reads
inside one run. **Those re-reads were minutes apart. These are a day apart, and the direction matches the
3,428-record `PMC` shed.** The consequence is practical: **a record present when a day is swept can be
absent later, so a sweep is a snapshot and not an inventory.**

**And the partial-day effect now has two measurements that disagree.** Yesterday's sweep of 24 November ran
at about 14:06 UTC. **On the title route it returned 17 records; today the same query over the same date
returns 159 — 10.7% of what is now there.** The journal route returned 0 yesterday and 53 today. **But the
whole-database count for that date was 1,615 yesterday against 7,010 today, which is 23.0%.** Two
independent measures of the same partial day, **differing by a factor of more than two, and the scoped
subset was the less complete of the two at that hour.** No explanation is offered; it is recorded as a
disagreement to watch rather than resolved.
