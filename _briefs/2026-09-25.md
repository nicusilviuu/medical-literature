## 1. Hypothermia hides malignant hyperthermia without preventing it

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesia & Analgesia*, **23 September 2026** — **new this week.** An
animal study
Ikeda, Mukaida, Kido, Xia, Ishii, Sumii, Noda, Otsuki, Ikegami, Sotomaru, Noguchi, Yasuda, Tsutsumi,
Miyoshi

The question is a genuinely clinical one, and the abstract sets it up precisely:

> Although hyperthermia exacerbates the pathophysiology of malignant hyperthermia (MH), the response
> to hypothermia remains poorly understood. **MH susceptible-swine studies suggested that hypothermia
> prevents MH, yet clinical cases occur during hypothermic cardiopulmonary bypass.** This study aimed
> to elucidate whether hypothermia **inhibits the MH trigger or merely decelerates the progression**
> of the lethal metabolic cascade.

Model: **heterozygous p.R2509C knock-in mice** (a type 1 ryanodine receptor variant) against wild
type. Caffeine sensitivity of myotubes at **30 °C and 40 °C**; intracellular calcium during
temperature change; then anaesthetised mice exposed to **2.5% sevoflurane at rectal temperatures of
34 °C or 36 °C**.

> R2509C myotubes showed a **leftward shift in the caffeine dose-response curve** and a lower EC50 at
> both 30 °C… and 40 °C… **Cooling myotubes from 36 °C to 34 °C significantly reduced relative
> intracellular Ca²⁺ fluorescence** in R2509C myotubes.

> In vivo, **all mutant mice developed MH and went into cardiac arrest regardless of temperature.
> Survival time was significantly longer at 34 °C** compared to 36 °C (mean difference 45.0 minutes,
> 95% CI 20.9–69.1). **Typical signs such as tachycardia were absent at 34 °C. Despite masked
> clinical signs, progressive hypercapnia, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, and characteristic
> mitochondrial swelling occurred in hypothermia.**

> **Hypothermia decelerates the clinical progression of MH but does not prevent triggering or
> lethality**… **Clinicians should be aware that hypothermia may mask tachycardia and body
> temperature elevation, leaving hypercapnia and acidosis as critical early indicators.**

**Interpretation.** A mouse study with an unusually direct clinical message, and it is the rare
animal paper that should change what a cardiac anaesthetist watches.

**The clinical problem it resolves is a real contradiction.** Swine work suggested hypothermia
protects; case reports describe MH occurring during **hypothermic cardiopulmonary bypass**. Those two
things could not both be simple, and this study explains why: cooling **slows the cascade and
suppresses the calcium signal enough to blunt the visible signs**, while the underlying trigger and
the lethal trajectory are untouched. Forty-five extra minutes of survival, and death anyway.

**The masking is the finding that matters, and it matters most in exactly the setting where MH is
hardest to spot.** On hypothermic bypass, temperature is being controlled externally, so a rising
temperature cannot appear; the heart may be arrested or paced, so tachycardia is meaningless; and
the classic triad is unavailable. **What the paper says remains detectable is hypercapnia, metabolic
acidosis and hyperkalaemia** — and on bypass, CO₂ is managed by the perfusionist through sweep gas,
which means a rising CO₂ production may present as **an unexplained need to increase sweep to hold
the same arterial CO₂**, rather than as a rising number.

**Mitochondrial swelling as an "ultra-early pathological change" is the mechanistic contribution**,
and the authors draw the right inference from it: **muscle damage is not solely driven by thermal
injury.** If the mitochondria are already swelling before the temperature rises, then hyperthermia is
a consequence of the metabolic storm rather than its cause, which is consistent with everything else
here.

**The limits are the ordinary limits of the model and should be kept in view.** One variant in one
species, sevoflurane as the trigger, 34 °C as the "hypothermic" condition — which is mild hypothermia,
far from the 28 °C of deep hypothermic bypass, and the paper cannot say whether deeper cooling
behaves the same way or differently. **No dantrolene arm**, so nothing here speaks to treatment. And
"all mutant mice arrested" means the model is far more penetrant than human MH susceptibility.

**What survives all of that is a warning about monitoring rather than a claim about risk**, and it is
a sound one: **in a cooled patient, absence of tachycardia and absence of pyrexia are not evidence
against MH.**

## 2. Adolescents: severe pain gone in three days, opioids still going in week four

**Journal · Published:** *Anesthesiology*, **22 September 2026** — **new this week.** Prospective
cohort; recovered in today's re-sweep of the degraded window
Sutherland, Hadland, Moon, Fardad, Ramsay, Kallan, **Neuman**

> Between **3 and 5% of adolescents develop new persistent opioid use after surgery.** Little is
> known about their recovery experience, including pain trajectory, pain treatment, and prolonged
> opioid use.

Adolescents enrolled June 2022 to May 2023 before procedures associated with mild, moderate or severe
postoperative pain. **Text-message surveys** at baseline, daily for two weeks, then monthly for five
months. Primary outcome **persistent pain at month 3**.

> The cohort included **340 adolescents** (median age 15) who provided outcome data at month 3,
> **including 141 undergoing major procedures.** Within this group, **persistent surgical site pain
> was present at month 3 among 57.9% (n = 81), while only 9.2% (n = 13) were "unhappy" or "very
> unhappy" with their recovery.** **Severe pain (NRS 7–10) resolved within 3 days** (IQR 1–6).
> **However, prolonged opioid use in weeks 3 and 4 was present among 39.6% (n = 44).**

> The odds of persistent surgical site pain **declined by 25% each month** (OR 0.75; 95% CI
> 0.67–0.83), and **female sex was the only significant individual predictor** (OR 1.95; 95% CI
> 1.11–3.42).

**Interpretation.** Two numbers in this abstract sit three days and three weeks apart, and the gap
between them is the paper.

**Severe pain resolved in a median of three days. Four in ten were still taking opioids in weeks
three and four.** The authors put it carefully — *"an unanticipated number used opioids after
resolution of severe pain for which opioids may be indicated"* — and that is the actionable finding
for anyone who writes a discharge prescription. **The companion baseline paper from the same cohort**
(*BJA Open*, 15 October 2025, same authors) reports that **93.4% of adolescents having severe-pain
procedures received an outpatient opioid prescription, median 18 doses (IQR 12–25).** Eighteen doses
is considerably more than three days of severe pain requires, and the quantity dispensed is the
variable most directly under the prescriber's control.

**The satisfaction dissociation is the more unsettling result.** **57.9% had persistent surgical site
pain at three months and only 9.2% were unhappy with their recovery.** Those two facts describe
adolescents who hurt and do not complain — or who have recalibrated what recovery is supposed to feel
like. **It means patient-reported satisfaction is not a safety signal in this population**, and a
service auditing its paediatric recovery on satisfaction scores would see nothing wrong.

**Female sex as the only individual predictor, at OR 1.95**, echoes the adult persistent-postsurgical-
pain literature, and is worth noting without over-reading: with 141 patients in the major-procedure
group, the study is not powered to find modest predictors, so "only significant predictor" mostly
means "the only one large enough to detect here."

**The same baseline paper records the context that makes the opioid finding worrying**: **21.1%
reported depression, 37.4% anxiety, and 16.6% prior-year substance use** before surgery. That is the
population receiving a median of 18 opioid doses.

[baseline paper, *BJA Open*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bjao.2025.100496) · PMID 41142590

## 3. Planned versus standby V-A ECMO in high-risk PCI — and a model caveat paragraph

**Journal · Published:** *Catheterization and Cardiovascular Interventions*, **23 September 2026** —
**new this week.** Single-centre retrospective observational study
Todurov, Todurov, Zybaha, Kipot, Maruniak

> The role of mechanical circulatory support in high-risk PCI remains controversial, and **the optimal
> timing of its initiation is not well defined.**

**74 consecutive patients**, 2019–2025, allocated **before PCI by a multidisciplinary Heart Team** to
either a **planned strategy (n = 43)** — V-A ECMO started before PCI — or a **standby strategy
(n = 31)**, with equipment and vascular access prepared but ECMO initiated only on predefined
intraprocedural haemodynamic deterioration. Primary endpoint **in-hospital MACCE** (death, MI,
stroke, repeat revascularisation).

> ECMO support during PCI was required in **19/32 (61.3%)** of the standby group. **In-hospital MACCE
> was significantly higher in the standby group (48.4% vs 14.0%, p = 0.002).** Standby also had
> longer ICU stay (8 vs 6 days) and hospital stay (15 vs 12 days), and higher vasoactive-inotropic
> scores. **In multivariable analysis the standby strategy was associated with higher odds of
> in-hospital MACCE (adjusted OR 4.20; 95% CI 1.35–13.10; p = 0.013).**

And then, in the abstract itself:

> Given the **nonrandomized treatment allocation, inclusion of standby patients who did not
> ultimately require ECMO, and limited number of events, these findings are hypothesis-generating and
> do not establish a causal benefit of planned ECMO or harm from rescue initiation.**

**Interpretation.** The conclusion paragraph is better than the result, and that is meant as praise.

**The authors have written their own principal objection into their abstract**, which is rare enough
to note. This archive spent much of the last fortnight supplying caveats that papers omitted — the
ASA physical status study that measured a billing code, the balanced-crystalloid cohort with an
implausible odds ratio. **Here the caveat arrived with the paper.**

**And the objection is the right one.** The standby group is a mixture: patients who sailed through
and never needed ECMO, and patients who deteriorated badly enough mid-procedure to be cannulated in a
hurry. **The second subgroup carries nearly all the events, and being cannulated during a
haemodynamic collapse is a marker of that collapse rather than a consequence of the strategy.**
Comparing "elective initiation" against "a mixture including emergency initiation" is close to
comparing elective with emergency anything, and that comparison has only ever had one answer.

**A numerical point worth flagging, gently.** The methods state **n = 31** in the standby group, and
the results report ECMO was required in **19/32**. **19/31 is 61.3%, the figure given, so the
denominator appears to be a typographical slip** rather than a discrepancy in the data — but it is
the kind of thing worth checking against the full text before quoting the proportion.

**What the study does establish is narrower and still useful**: that a Heart Team applying a
predetermined strategy ended up cannulating **61% of the patients it had judged safe to leave on
standby.** If that number holds elsewhere, it says the pre-procedural judgement separating "needs
support" from "might need support" is not very good — which is a statement about risk prediction, not
about ECMO timing, and it lands beside
[the study of regret in VV-ECMO candidacy](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/) covered on Tuesday.
**Both describe clinicians making ECMO decisions under uncertainty that the available tools do not
resolve.**

*Access note: 74 patients at one centre over six years, retrospective. The full text was not
reachable; the caveat quoted above is the authors' own, from the abstract.*
