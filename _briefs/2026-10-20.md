## The same-day index reading is a fraction of a day, and the fraction varies from a fifth to all of it

**Monday 19 October finished on 42,580 records** — six to seventeen times a normal weekday, the catch-up after
a quiet weekend. **This archive read that bucket four times during yesterday's two runs**, and the readings
are now a calibration of its own instrument:

| Reading | 19 October bucket | Share of the final 42,580 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 14:04 | 8,717 | **20.5%** |
| 14:11 | 12,706 | 29.8% |
| 14:21 | 18,695 | 43.9% |
| 14:26 | 21,088 | **49.5%** |
| final | **42,580** | 100% |

**More than half the day's records arrived after 14:26.** Against that, **Saturday 17 October read 3,650 at
14:08 and finished at 3,631** — complete by early afternoon, and slightly *lower* now through churn. **So the
same-day index-date count is an unreliable fraction of the day's eventual total, somewhere between a fifth and
all of it, and which one depends on whether a catch-up batch is coming.**

**That settles two things.** **Today's reading of 847 for 20 October cannot be read as low** — it is a
fifth-to-all of an unknown total, measured at the hour when that ratio is least stable. **And it
retrospectively explains the Sunday zero without needing the weekly cycle at all**: 18 October's records, had
there been any, would simply have arrived after 14:00. **The instrument for "is ingest running" is the previous
day's final count and the whole-database delta, not today's bucket.** 19 October's final 42,580 and a
whole-database rise of **34,271** since yesterday's 14:04 reading both say yes.

**And a baseline-mismatch error, caught before publishing, which is a direct cost of the twice-per-run check
adopted on 16 October.** The whole database read **49,079,047** at 14:26 yesterday and the eleven source counts
were last read at **14:04**, when the total was 49,066,705. **I differenced today's source counts against the
14:26 whole-database figure, got a residual of more than twelve thousand records, and briefly had an anomaly.**
There is none: against the matching 14:04 baseline the per-source gains — `MED` +28,798, `PMC` +4,635,
`PPR` +836, `CTX` +2 — sum to **34,271**, exactly the whole-database change. **Measuring twice per run creates
two baselines, and every number has to be differenced against the one it belongs to.** Both reference windows
grew substantially: 1–30 September by **6,152** to 163,125, and 1–7 October by **5,839** to 42,533.

## 1. Which oxygen target you pick after cardiac arrest does not change the outcome

**Journal · Published:** *Academic Emergency Medicine*, **1 October 2026** — **nineteen days old**,
0 citations. **Systematic review, meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis**, PRISMA 2020, searched to
**June 2026**, RoB 2 and GRADE. **Eleven randomised trials (12 reports), 4,136 adults** after cardiac arrest
on invasive mechanical ventilation, restrictive versus higher oxygenation targets.

> **Restrictive oxygenation showed no significant difference in all-cause mortality (RR 0.96, 95% CI
> 0.88-1.06) or unfavorable neurological outcomes (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.88-1.04).** Most subgroup analyses were
> non-significant, **except for reduced mortality in the strict hypothermia subgroup (33°C-34°C), although TSA
> showed unstable evidence.** No significant differences were observed in secondary outcomes. **Restrictive
> oxygenation increased hypoxemia risk (RR 1.65) but improved ventilator-free days (MD 2.14).**
>
> **Conclusion** … **Under current normothermia (36°C-37.5°C) protocols, the accumulated evidence trends
> toward futility for a restrictive approach, though the definitive information size has not yet been entirely
> accrued.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the patient outcome that was missing from a review published three days ago, and
it is a null.** On 17 October this archive reported a meta-analysis of
[closed-loop oxygen control](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-17/) showing **21 percentage points more time
inside a target oxygen saturation range** — with no mortality, no length of stay, no intubation and no harm
outcome measured anywhere. **The question it left open was whether hitting the oxygen target helps. For adults
after cardiac arrest, across eleven trials and 4,136 patients, the answer is that which target you aim at does
not change mortality or neurological outcome.**

**Put the two together and the monitoring thread's first condition fails.** That condition is that the
measurement be closer to the pathology than the one it replaces — and more fundamentally, that the target sit
on a causal path to the outcome. **A device that hits an oxygen target with 21 points more precision cannot
improve an outcome that does not depend on which target is chosen.** **This is not an argument against
closed-loop control**, which may well be worth having for the hypoxaemia it prevents at the extremes; **it is
an argument against expecting outcome benefit from precision in a variable whose set-point does not matter.**

**The trade-off the review does find is real and is the clinically usable part: hypoxaemia risk up (RR 1.65)
against ventilator-free days up (MD 2.14).** A restrictive strategy buys about two ventilator-free days and
costs a 65% relative increase in hypoxaemic episodes, with no mortality or neurological difference either way.
**That is a choice between a measurable harm and a measurable process benefit, neither of which moves the
outcome** — and stated that plainly, it is a reasonable basis for preferring whichever a unit finds easier to
deliver safely.

**Two methodological notes, and the first is a credit.** **The authors found a mortality reduction in the
strict hypothermia (33–34 °C) subgroup and immediately reported that their own trial sequential analysis showed
the evidence unstable.** This is the third time in a week this archive has met the same situation — a null
primary with a positive subgroup — and the handling has differed each time: MODEST led its conclusion with the
subgroup, the lidocaine rebound-pain trial labelled its secondaries exploratory and refused to recommend, and
**this review flags its own subgroup as unstable in the same sentence that reports it.** **Second, the
conclusion's phrasing is exact and worth copying: "trends toward futility … though the definitive information
size has not yet been entirely accrued."** That is a trial sequential analysis saying "probably nothing, and
not yet certain" without pretending to either certainty.

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1111/acem.70422) · *Academic Emergency Medicine*

## 2. Remimazolam wakes patients nineteen minutes slower than propofol — the opposite of yesterday, and the pair isolates flumazenil

**Journal · Published:** *PLOS One*, **15 October 2026** — **five days old**, 0 citations. **Randomised
controlled trial, 80 adults** for elective arthroscopic meniscectomy, **propofol-based versus
remimazolam-based total intravenous anaesthesia**; **71 completed the QoR-40 and were analysed** (36 propofol,
35 remimazolam). **Primary outcome: global QoR-40 on postoperative day 1.**

> **Global QoR-40 scores on POD 1 did not differ significantly** between the Propofol and Remimazolam groups
> (**166.1 ± 16.8 vs. 170.6 ± 13.6; mean difference −4.5, 95% CI −11.8 to 2.7; P = 0.218**). In an
> **exploratory** analysis, the emotional-state domain scores were higher in the Remimazolam group (**mean
> difference −2.2, 95% CI −3.9 to −0.6; P = 0.009**). **The Remimazolam group had a longer time to full
> alertness (18 [15-24] vs. 37 [31-45] min; P < 0.001) and longer post-anesthesia care unit stay (30 [30-39]
> vs. 38 [30-50] min; P = 0.008). Intraoperative hypotension requiring ephedrine occurred more frequently in
> the Propofol group (44.4% vs. 17.1%; P = 0.013).**

**Interpretation.** **Yesterday this archive reported a trial in which remimazolam produced faster eye-opening
and faster extubation than propofol. Today's trial reports the reverse: nineteen minutes slower to full
alertness, 37 against 18.** Taken alone, each looks like a result about remimazolam. **Taken together they
isolate the variable, and the variable is flumazenil.**

**[Yesterday's trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-19/) gave the remimazolam arm 200 μg of flumazenil at
the end of surgery. Today's trial does not mention flumazenil at all.** So: **remimazolam reversed with its
antagonist emerges faster than propofol; remimazolam left to wear off emerges considerably slower.** That is
mechanistically unsurprising — remimazolam is ultra-short-acting but it is still a benzodiazepine, and
propofol's offset is fast — **and it is a practical point neither paper could have made on its own.** **The
drug's advertised emergence advantage is conditional on giving the antagonist.**

**It also refines, without retracting, what I wrote yesterday.** Yesterday's trial found a *longer* PACU stay
in the remimazolam arm and its authors attributed that to **institutional safety protocols** — presumably
mandated observation after flumazenil. **Today's trial also finds a longer PACU stay in the remimazolam arm,
without flumazenil.** So the protocol explanation was the authors' own and remains theirs; **but a longer PACU
stay after remimazolam evidently does not require a flumazenil protocol to produce it — slower emergence will
do it.** **Yesterday's reading stands as reported; its sufficiency does not.**

**The primary outcome is a null and the exploratory finding should be left where the authors left it.** QoR-40
on day 1 did not differ — mean difference 4.5 points on a scale running to 200, interval −11.8 to 2.7. **The
emotional-state domain was better with remimazolam at P = 0.009 and is labelled exploratory**, which is
correct: one domain of a multi-domain instrument, in a trial powered for the global score. **Worth remembering
next time rather than quoting now.**

**The clinically substantial finding is haemodynamic and it is large: intraoperative hypotension requiring
ephedrine in 44.4% of the propofol arm against 17.1% of the remimazolam arm** — 16 of 36 against 6 of 35.
**That is the trade the two trials jointly describe: remimazolam buys haemodynamic stability and, without
flumazenil, pays for it in emergence time.** For a short day-case operation that is probably a bad trade; for
a frail patient in whom hypotension is the hazard, it may be a good one.

**Denominator note: 80 randomised, 71 analysed — nine patients, 11%, absent with no reason given.**

## 3. Erector spinae block in coronary surgery: a high-certainty positive, which this archive has not seen in a month

**Journal · Published:** *Annals of Cardiac Anaesthesia*, **12 October 2026** — **eight days old**,
0 citations. **Systematic review, meta-analysis, meta-regression and trial sequential analysis**
(CRD42025645045), **12 studies, 704 patients** undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting, erector spinae
plane block versus control. **GRADE certainty reported per outcome.**

> **ESPB significantly reduced the morphine milligram equivalent consumption at 24 h (mean difference −19.05
> mg; 95% CI −22.50 to −15.61; P < 0.001; high certainty) and 48 h (MD −19.42 mg; 95% CI −28.75 to −10.09;
> P < 0.001; moderate certainty).** Pain scores … were lower within 12 h (MD −0.93; moderate certainty) and at
> 24 h (MD −0.58; low certainty). **ESPB shortened extubation time (MD −2.50 h; high certainty) and ICU stay
> (MD −12.55 h; moderate certainty). TSA confirmed robust evidence for opioid reduction, early pain relief,
> and extubation benefits.**

**Interpretation.** **Two outcomes at high certainty is the thing to notice, because this archive has spent a
month on guidelines reporting none.** The ACCP found no high-certainty evidence for inpatient tobacco
treatment; the AAP's paediatric UTI certainty ran low to very low; EAN and ESCMID reached two graded
recommendations from sixteen questions; SFAR and SOFMER issued six expert opinions out of nine
recommendations. **Here a meta-analysis of twelve small trials awards high certainty to a 19-milligram
morphine-equivalent reduction at 24 hours and to extubation 2.5 hours earlier, with trial sequential analysis
confirming both.** **The contrast is not that regional anaesthesia is better evidenced than end-of-life care —
it is that a narrow, repeatedly-randomised, short-horizon question can reach high certainty, and the questions
guidelines must answer usually cannot.**

**On the clinical substance: 19 milligrams of morphine equivalent at 24 hours is a large reduction after CABG**
— enough to matter for ileus, nausea, sedation and the respiratory depression that keeps a sternotomy patient
intubated. **And the extubation and ICU-stay findings connect analgesia to the thing a cardiac unit actually
manages: 2.5 hours earlier extubation at high certainty and 12.55 hours less ICU stay at moderate.** That is a
plausible chain — less opioid, earlier extubation, shorter ICU stay — and the certainty grades fall exactly as
one would expect along it, highest at the opioid end and lower further downstream.

**The pain scores are where the honesty sits and they are small.** **−0.93 on a numerical rating scale within
12 hours (moderate certainty) and −0.58 at 24 hours (low certainty).** Under a point on an 11-point scale is
below most estimates of a minimally important difference. **So the block reduces opioid consumption
substantially and pain scores barely** — the usual signature of effective regional analgesia, because patients
titrate themselves to a tolerable score and the block shows up in the drug they did not need. **Quoting the
pain difference as the benefit would understate it; quoting it as clinically meaningful would overstate it.**

**Limitations: 12 studies and 704 patients is a small evidence base to award high certainty from**, the trials
are heterogeneous enough that meta-regression was needed, and the authors name the open question themselves —
**continuous rather than single-shot techniques.** This clears a queued item.

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.4103/aca.aca_17_26) · CRD42025645045

## 4. Spinal anaesthesia is cheaper and less wasteful, and occupies a recovery bed half an hour longer

**Journal · Published:** *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica*, **online ahead of a 1 November 2026 issue
date** — stated plainly because this archive does not blur dates. 0 citations. **Randomised trial, 395
patients** having total knee arthroplasty, **spinal versus general (total intravenous) anaesthesia**, with
outcomes chosen for **time, cost and disposable materials** rather than for the patient.

> **Anaesthesia method did not affect operating room time** (median 152 min [136-168] vs. 150 min [137-165],
> Hodges-Lehmann estimate 2 min [95% CI −3 to 6], p = 0.49). **However, after spinal anaesthesia, patients
> appeared to spend more time in the postanaesthesia care unit** (median **104 min [71-148] vs. 73 min
> [49-98]**, Hodges-Lehmann estimate **32 min [95% CI 23-42]**, p < 0.001). **Medication for hypotension was
> more common in the general anaesthesia group, whereas medication for hypertension was more common in the
> spinal anaesthesia group. General anaesthesia generated more disposable material (1252 vs. 611 g), and both
> medicine and equipment expenses were higher (total costs €83 vs. €36).**

**Interpretation.** **This is a randomised trial whose outcomes are cost, waste and minutes, and the archive
has not reported one like it.** Current guidance recommends regional anaesthesia and total intravenous
anaesthesia partly on environmental grounds, and the honest version of that argument needs numbers. **Here they
are: general anaesthesia produced 2.05 times the disposable waste by mass and cost 2.31 times as much in
medicines and equipment — €83 against €36.**

**And the paper reports, without pricing, a resource cost that runs the other way.** **Spinal anaesthesia kept
patients in the postanaesthesia care unit 32 minutes longer** — a Hodges-Lehmann estimate with an interval from
23 to 42 minutes, so the effect is solid. **Across the 395 patients randomised that is about 210 hours of
recovery-unit occupancy**, and a PACU bed is a staffed bed. **The €47 saved per patient on materials is real;
so is the half-hour of nursing time, and only one of the two appears in the cost comparison.** The conclusion
says spinal "appeared to be more economical and material-sparing", which is true of the materials and is not
the whole ledger. **The paper measures the offset and leaves it unmonetised, which is better than not measuring
it — but a reader comparing the two methods on cost needs to put a price on PACU minutes, and this trial does
not.**

**The haemodynamic finding is clean and matches today's second item.** Hypotension needing treatment was more
common under general anaesthesia; hypertension needing treatment more common under spinal. **Compare item 2,
where propofol caused hypotension requiring ephedrine in 44.4% against remimazolam's 17.1%** — **two
independent trials in today's reading in which the propofol-based general anaesthetic is the one that drops the
blood pressure.** Not a new finding, but worth noting that it arrived twice in one day.

**And a pattern across three trials in two days: the postanaesthesia care unit is where the choice shows up.**
Remimazolam with flumazenil stayed longer by protocol; remimazolam alone stayed longer by slower emergence;
spinal anaesthesia stays longer than general by 32 minutes. **Operating-room time, the resource everyone
measures, was identical in this trial. The recovery unit, which few trials report, moved every time.**
