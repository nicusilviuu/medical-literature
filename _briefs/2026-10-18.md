## A zero that cannot be interpreted, and a near-finding discarded

**Every figure is identical to yesterday.** Whole database **49,058,472**, all eleven source counts unchanged,
both reference windows unchanged, and **today's index-date count is zero** on three reads. After a pipeline
that restarted on 16 October with 37,260 records and delivered 3,650 more on the 17th, **nothing has entered
in twenty-two hours.**

**That looks like a renewed stop and it is not interpretable, which is the honest reading.** The archive has
never characterised this database's weekly cycle. Doing it now:

| 26 September | Saturday | 9,581 |

| **18 October** | **Sunday** | **0 so far** |

**Two of the three previous Sundays finished on 189 and 187 records.** A near-zero Sunday is this database's
ordinary behaviour, not an outage signature. **And every one of those figures is a final value read on a later
day: this archive has no idea whether a Sunday's 187 records arrive before or after 14:00 UTC.** So a zero at
14:04 on a Sunday is compatible with a normal weekend and with a renewed stop, and **the measurement design —
once daily at a fixed time — cannot separate a one-day signal from a weekly cycle it has eight data points on.**
**The discriminating test is tomorrow: a normal Monday delivers 2,500 to 6,500.**

**And a near-finding of my own, discarded before it was published.** I first noted that the 17 October
index-date bucket had not grown by a single record in twenty-two hours and began writing that up as evidence of
a stop. **It is not evidence of anything. `FIRST_IDATE` is the date a record was *first* indexed, so once
17 October ended no record could acquire that date — a past bucket is complete by construction and cannot
grow.** That is the third time this month an instrument's own definition has refuted a reading taken from it,
and it belongs in the record alongside the other two.

**What is genuinely informative is narrower: zero records entered the whole database between 14:08 yesterday
and 14:04 today.** On previous weekends the corresponding window would have contained Sunday's one or two
hundred. **One or two hundred against zero is a difference this archive cannot resolve against its own noise.**

## 1. High-dose rocuronium helps in the ICU and not in the emergency department

**Journal · Published:** *American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine*, **1 August 2026** —
**eleven weeks old**, 0 citations. **Secondary analysis of two multicentre randomised airway trials**, **9
emergency departments and 24 ICUs** in the United States. Of 2,440 trial patients, **1,822 (74.7%) received
rocuronium**: **720 (39.5%) high-dose (>1.2 mg/kg)** and **1,102 (60.5%) standard-dose (≤1.2 mg/kg)**.
**Propensity matching, stratified by setting. Primary outcome: successful intubation on the first attempt.**

> **Participants receiving high-dose rocuronium had a higher probability of successful intubation on the first
> attempt (adjusted relative risk 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12). This difference in the primary outcome was not
> present for ED intubations (adjusted RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.89–1.04) but was present for ICU intubations
> (adjusted RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06–1.20).** … **Conclusions** … **Given the potential for unmeasured
> confounding, these findings warrant evaluation in a randomized controlled trial.**

**Interpretation.** **The setting split is the result, and it is a specificity finding rather than a subgroup
fishing expedition.** The overall estimate — 1.07 — is the average of a clean null in the emergency department
(0.97, interval 0.89 to 1.04) and a clear effect in intensive care (1.13, interval 1.06 to 1.20). **Those two
intervals barely overlap, and the pooled figure describes neither population.** This archive uses replication
or specificity as the two controls on an interaction; here the specificity is sharp enough to carry it.

**And there is a mechanism that predicts exactly this, which is why it is worth more than a P value.** Rocuronium
has to reach the neuromuscular junction, and in a shocked patient with a low cardiac output it arrives slowly
and incompletely — **so a higher dose compensates for poor delivery rather than for pharmacodynamic
resistance.** ICU patients awaiting emergency intubation are more often vasopressor-dependent and
low-output than emergency department patients, who are more often normotensive and hypoxic. **If the mechanism
is circulatory delivery, the dose should matter where the circulation is bad, and it does.** **That is a
hypothesis the data are consistent with, not a mechanism this paper tested**, and it is offered as such.

**The honest limits are the ones the authors state.** This is **observational within randomised datasets**:
the trials randomised other things, and dose was chosen by clinicians. **Propensity matching handles measured
confounders and the choice of a high dose is plausibly driven by exactly the unmeasured ones that predict a
difficult airway** — a clinician who expects trouble gives more relaxant and may also do several other things
differently. The authors call for a randomised trial, which is the right call. **One figure the abstract does
not give: the absolute first-attempt success rates.** A relative risk of 1.13 on a base rate somewhere around
80% is roughly ten percentage points, but that is my arithmetic on an assumed baseline, not the paper's, **and
it is the number a clinician would actually want.**

**A cross-reference worth noting**, because the dose boundary is the same: the trial of
[omitting face-mask ventilation](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-17/) reported yesterday used **1.2 mg/kg**
in its no-mask arm against 0.6 in the mask arm, and called that a doubled dose. **By this paper's
classification, 1.2 mg/kg is the top of "standard" and not "high" at all.** The two papers are using the same
number as a ceiling and as a baseline.

[Read the analysis](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajrccm/aamag203) · *AJRCCM*

## 2. MODEST: null on both primary comparisons, and the conclusion leads with a subgroup

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA Network Open*, **3 August 2026** — **eleven weeks old**, 0 citations, open
access. **Double-blind, sham-controlled, three-arm randomised trial**, University Medicine Greifswald,
February 2024 to January 2025. **225 patients aged 65 or over** having major elective surgery, randomised to
**α-transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS, n = 68), transcranial direct current stimulation
(tDCS, n = 83) or sham (n = 74)** — **20 minutes at 2 mA in the postanaesthesia care unit.** **Primary
outcome: postoperative delirium on the ward over up to five days, tACS versus sham.**

> **No substantial between group differences were observed for POD incidence in ward (tACS, 11.5% [6 of 52];
> tDCS, 14.3% [9 of 63]; sham, 9.2% [6 of 65]; tACS vs sham: OR, 1.60 [95% CI, 0.47-5.46]; P = .45; tDCS vs
> sham: OR, 1.51 [0.49-4.68]; P = .47) or in PACU** (tACS, 14.6% [7 of 48]; tDCS, 21.4% [12 of 56]; sham,
> 21.7% [13 of 60]; ORs 0.95 and 0.91). … **Prespecified subgroup analyses revealed different modulation of POD
> incidence in PACU depending on duration of surgery (OR, 0.15 [95% CI, 0.02-0.97]; P = .046), indicating that
> tACS may reduce POD incidence for patients with longer surgery (absolute risk difference … for a surgical
> duration of 3 to 5 hours of −26.6% to −46.5%).** …
>
> **Conclusions** … **there was no evidence for a uniform effect of postoperative tES on the incidence of POD,
> but it suggested a benefit of parietal α oscillatory enhancement for patients with longer surgery.**

**Interpretation.** **The trial is null and both active arms point the wrong way on the primary outcome** —
odds ratios of 1.60 and 1.51 for more delirium, not less. Then the conclusion's second clause offers a benefit.
**Three things need stating before anyone acts on that clause, and all three are arithmetic.**

**First, the denominators.** 68 plus 83 plus 74 is **225 randomised**. The ward analysis uses **52, 63 and
65 — 180 patients, missing 45, or 20%.** The PACU analysis uses **48, 56 and 60 — 164 patients, missing 61, or
27%.** **The abstract does not say why a fifth to a quarter of the randomised patients are absent**, and in a
delirium trial the reasons for missing delirium assessments are rarely independent of delirium. That is the
single largest threat to everything below it.

**Second, the event counts.** The primary comparison is **six delirium events against six** — 6 of 52 against
6 of 65. **Twelve events.** The confidence interval 0.47 to 5.46 is the honest expression of that: this trial
could not have detected anything short of an enormous effect. **The PACU subgroup claim rests on 7 events
among 48 tACS patients against 13 among 60 shams, and the interaction with surgical duration is estimated
inside that.**

**Third, the subgroup arithmetic.** **Six factors were prespecified** — age, sex, duration of surgery, type of
surgery, baseline cognitive function, preoperative pain. **One reached P = .046, with an odds ratio of 0.15
whose upper bound is 0.97.** Across six independent tests at α = 0.05 the expected number of false positives is
0.3, so one marginal hit is close to what chance supplies; prespecification protects against choosing the
subgroup after the fact, not against multiplicity. **And the quoted "absolute risk difference of −26.6% to
−46.5%" is not an observed difference — it is a range read off a model at a specified surgical duration.** A
26-to-46-percentage-point effect is not estimable from 7 events in 48 patients, however the model expresses it.

**What the trial does establish is worth having and is the opposite of exciting: twenty minutes of
transcranial stimulation in recovery does not reduce postoperative delirium, and is safe.** No difference in
adverse events, no serious adverse events, and a clean sham comparison in an older surgical population with
neurological and psychiatric disease excluded. **That is a useful negative result**, and the authors' own
closing sentence — future work should test vulnerable groups, optimal timing, and personalised parameters — is
the correct reading of their own data. **"Suggested a benefit" is the part that will be quoted, and it is the
part the numbers cannot support.**

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.28284) · *JAMA Network Open*, DRKS00033703

## 3. Disease-specific glucose targets, where the two methods disagree exactly where it matters

**Journal · Published:** *Nursing in Critical Care*, **online ahead of a 1 November 2026 issue date** — so
a fortnight ahead of its nominal publication date, stated plainly because this archive does not blur dates.
0 citations. **Two designs in one paper: a retrospective cohort from MIMIC-IV and a frequentist network
meta-analysis of 35 randomised trials.** Exposure is **time-weighted average glucose** in four strata: strict
(80–110 mg/dL), **intermediate strict (110–150, the reference)**, liberal (150–180) and very liberal. **Cohort:
21,119 sepsis, 16,957 cardiac, 4,708 neurological patients.**

> **In both sepsis and cardiac patients, compared with the intermediate strict control group … both the strict
> (80–110 mg/dL: HR 1.10/1.26, both p < 0.05) and liberal (150–180 mg/dL: HR 1.38/1.78, both p < 0.05) control
> groups showed significantly higher 28-day mortality. In contrast, among neurological patients, strict control
> conferred a survival benefit (HR 0.76, p = 0.013).** **The NMA of 35 RCTs corroborated these findings for
> sepsis and neurological disorders**, with intermediate strict ranking highest for sepsis (P-score 74.9%) and
> strict control highest for neurological disorders (71.2%), **whereas liberal control ranked highest for
> cardiac disease (P-score 83.5%); no pairwise differences were statistically significant.** …
> **Conclusions** … **for cardiac disease, the NMA ranked liberal control highest, leaving the optimal target
> uncertain.**

**Interpretation.** **The paper's own honesty is the best thing in it: the two methods agree on sepsis and
neurology and flatly contradict each other on cardiac disease, and the conclusion says so.** In the cohort,
liberal control in cardiac patients carried a hazard ratio of **1.78** — the largest harm estimate in the
table. In the network meta-analysis, liberal control **ranked best** for the same group. **Those are not
reconcilable, and a paper that reports both rather than the convenient one deserves credit.**

**But the network meta-analysis cannot adjudicate anything, for a reason the abstract states and then rides
over: "no pairwise differences were statistically significant."** **P-scores are a ranking of estimates that
are indistinguishable from one another.** A P-score of 83.5% for liberal control in cardiac disease, with every
pairwise comparison non-significant, is the ordering of overlapping intervals — it conveys precision the
underlying data do not contain. **This is a well-known failure of ranking metrics in network meta-analysis and
it is the reason the contradiction exists at all: one side of it is noise with a percentage attached.**
**The cohort is where the cardiac signal lives, and the cohort has its own problem.**

**Which is the shape of the result.** In sepsis and cardiac patients the cohort finds a **U-shape** — worse at
80–110, worse at 150–180, best in the middle. **A U-shape is exactly what confounding by illness severity
produces in an observational glucose study**, and in both directions: a patient whose time-weighted average
runs low is often either receiving aggressive insulin or failing to mount a stress response, and a patient
running high is often sicker. **Time-weighted average glucose over an ICU stay is as much an outcome of
illness as a treatment decision.** The abstract reports hazard ratios without saying what they are adjusted
for. **So the U-shape is consistent with disease-specific optimal targets and equally consistent with sicker
patients sitting in both tails**, and nothing in the abstract separates those.

**The neurological finding is the one that survives best and is the most interesting.** Strict control
benefited neurological patients in the cohort (**HR 0.76**) *and* ranked highest in the network meta-analysis
(71.2%) — **the only stratum where both designs point the same way against the overall literature**, which
since [the 70-trial meta-analysis reported on 14 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-14/) has said
tight control does not improve survival. **A brain-injured population plausibly differs: cerebral glucose
metabolism, the vulnerability of injured brain to hyperglycaemia, and a smaller cohort (4,708) in which
confounding patterns differ.** **It is a hypothesis with two consistent lines of support and no trial.**

## The monitoring thread, written up as one piece

**This archive has been collecting, since late September, trials in which someone measured something and then
asked whether the measuring helped. There are now ten of them and the pattern is stable enough to state.**

| Document | What happened to the number | What happened to the patient |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Videolaryngoscopy | the view improved | first-pass success improved; patient outcomes unestablished |
| Remimazolam BIS crossover ([11 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-11/)) | the index read differently at equal depth | not measured — the calibration moved under the drug |
| AlphaMax, EEG alpha power ([13 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-13/)) | pushed up in one arm, down in the other | delirium 33–37% in all four groups |
| qCRT in endotoxaemia ([14 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-14/)) | crossed its 3-second threshold in both directions | inflammation continued throughout |
| STOP-Vanc ([14 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-14/)) | a swab with 98.9% negative predictive value | vancomycin-free hours moved 4 of ~108 |
| Dexamethasone satisfaction ([15 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-15/)) | the instrument was saturated at maximum | no difference detectable, by construction |
| NeoBeat ([15 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-15/)) | the measurement changed who was measured | trial terminated without power |
| BOX calculated PVR ([16 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-16/)) | fell because cardiac output rose | lactate and mortality unchanged |
| Closed-loop oxygen ([17 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-17/)) | 21 points more time in target range | **not measured at all** |
| **Lung ultrasound fluid management ([17 Oct](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-17/))** | **a better measurement replaced a worse one** | **postoperative hypoxaemia fell** |

**Nine of the ten are failure modes and they are not the same failure.** Four concern the instrument — it
changes, it is recalibrated by the drug, it saturates, it is a ratio whose denominator moved. Three concern the
causal chain — the number is not on the path, it reverses while the disease continues, an accurate result
changes no behaviour. One concerns the act of measuring altering the sample. **One, closed-loop oxygen, is the
limiting case: the outcome was the target, so the study could not fail and could not inform.**

**What distinguishes the single positive case is worth naming, because it is not enthusiasm.** The lung
ultrasound trial replaced central venous pressure with a measurement **of the organ that was actually getting
wet**, attached a protocol with predefined thresholds, and reported an outcome — postoperative hypoxaemia —
**that did not share the instrument.** Three properties: **a measurement closer to the pathology than the one
it replaced; a protocol that converts the number into an action; and an outcome measured by something else.**
**Every one of the nine failures lacks at least one of the three.** Closed-loop oxygen has the first two and
not the third. STOP-Vanc has the first and third and not the second — the result was accurate and nobody acted
on it. AlphaMax has the second and third and not the first, because frontal alpha power was never close to the
pathology.

**The honest statement of what the thread shows is therefore narrower than "monitoring does not work".** It is
that **a monitored variable earns its keep only when all three hold**, and that most monitoring research
establishes one of the three and reports it as though it had established all of them. **The commonest single
failure is the third: an outcome that shares the instrument.** It appears in the closed-loop oxygen review as
the entire design, and in the lung ultrasound trial as one of two primary outcomes — which is why that trial is
quoted here on hypoxaemia and not on lung-water score.

**And the one thing that would most change the picture is a trial this archive has not found: a randomised
comparison in which a monitored physiological target was controlled more tightly, and a patient outcome
improved, with nothing cancelling it.** [Intensive glucose control](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-14/)
came closest and then cancelled itself — fewer infections, three times the severe hypoglycaemia, mortality
0.99. **Today's disease-specific glucose paper is an attempt to escape that cancellation by stratifying**, and
its two methods disagree. **The pressure-targeting thread, seven documents deep, has been waiting five years
for the same kind of trial and has not had it either.** **Until one appears, the defensible position is that
tighter control of a monitored number is a hypothesis about a causal path, and the path has to be demonstrated
separately from the measurement.**
