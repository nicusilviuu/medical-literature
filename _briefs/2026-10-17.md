## 1. Cochrane: daily sedation interruption probably reduces mortality, and probably increases opioid dose

**Journal · Published:** *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, **7 October 2026** — **ten days old**,
0 citations. **An update of the 2014 review**; latest search **8 October 2025**. **23 trials, 5,987
participants** (18 adult trials, 5 paediatric; 3,238 adults, 2,750 children, 4,910 after cluster adjustment).
**Random-effects meta-analysis, RoB 1, GRADE.** Comparators were deliberately mixed: **nine trials against a
sedation protocol, nine against clinician discretion, five against analgesia-first or no sedation.**

> **Daily sedation interruption may reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation** (MD **−0.15 log days**,
> 95% CI −0.29 to −0.01; **I² = 88%**; 18 studies, 3,815 participants; **low-certainty** evidence) …
> **a relative reduction of 14% (1% to 25%).** **Daily sedation interruption probably reduces mortality**
> (**RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.99; I² = 0%**; 18 studies, 4,057 participants; **725 events**;
> **moderate-certainty**). **Probably reduces ICU LOS** (−0.12 log days, −0.22 to −0.02; I² = 72%;
> moderate) … **11% (2% to 20%)**, and **hospital LOS** likewise. … **DSI probably reduces rates of
> tracheostomy** (**RR 0.68, 0.55 to 0.85**; I² = 0%; 8 studies, 2,503 participants; moderate). **Little to no
> difference** in accidental removal of the endotracheal tube (RR 0.88, 0.66–1.19) or of lines or catheters
> (RR 1.44, 0.80–2.57), **and in delirium occurrence, but the evidence is very uncertain** (RR 0.92,
> 0.74–1.15; I² = 59%). … **Daily sedation interruption probably increases the total dose of morphine
> administered** (MD **+0.07 log mg**, 0 to 0.13; moderate) … **a relative increase of 7% (0% to 14%).**

**Interpretation.** **The headline is that a practice which has been quietly abandoned in a lot of ICUs comes
back with a mortality signal, at moderate certainty, with no heterogeneity.** RR 0.88, interval 0.77 to 0.99,
**I² = 0% across eighteen trials and 725 deaths among 4,057 participants.** Daily interruption spread after
the 1990s, then lost ground to protocolised light sedation and the "analgesia first" approach, on the
reasonable view that if you never deepen sedation you need not interrupt it. **This review says the
interruption still buys something.**

**The sharpest finding is one the review does not frame as a finding at all: the stated rationale is not what
the data support.** DSI was built on limiting drug accumulation — "aims to limit drug accumulation and promote
a more awake state". **There is little to no difference in total benzodiazepine, fentanyl or propofol dose,
and morphine dose probably goes up by about 7%.** So whatever is producing the mortality and ventilation
effects, **it is not less sedative drug.** The plausible alternatives are the daily neurological examination
itself, earlier recognition of readiness to wean, or the organisational discipline a scheduled interruption
imposes on a unit — **and the review cannot distinguish them.** **A practice that works for a reason other
than its stated mechanism is a practice whose boundaries nobody knows**, which matters when deciding whether
it adds anything on top of a modern light-sedation protocol.

**Which is the limitation that governs how far to take this.** **The comparator is three different things
pooled**: nine trials against protocolised sedation, nine against clinician discretion, five against
analgesia-first or no sedation. **Against clinician discretion, DSI is a protocol and most of the benefit could
be the protocol. Against analgesia-first, DSI is the *deeper* strategy.** The abstract gives the breakdown and
not the subgroup results, so **the question a 2026 intensivist actually has — does DSI add anything to light
protocolised sedation — is answerable from the full review and not from the abstract.** **That is where to
look next, and I am not going to guess the answer.**

**Two more things worth flagging.** The **I² = 0% on mortality against I² = 88% on duration of ventilation** is
an odd pair: the process outcome is wildly inconsistent across trials and the hard outcome is perfectly
consistent. This archive noted on 11 October that **I² = 0 is itself a warning** — it is the value you also
get when individual trials carry too little information to disagree. **With 725 events it is less fragile than
that, but the contrast between 0% and 88% in the same review deserves a reader's attention rather than a
reader's confidence.** And the arithmetic: **all four log-scale translations check out** — exp(−0.15) = 0.861,
exp(−0.12) = 0.887, exp(0.07) = 1.073 — **but 3,238 adults plus 2,750 children is 5,988, and the review says
5,987.** One participant, almost certainly the cluster adjustment, and noted because the archive checks.

## 2. Closed-loop oxygen control hits the target 21 points more often, and the review reports no patient outcome at all

**Journal · Published:** *European Respiratory Review*, **7 October 2026** — **ten days old**, 0 citations.
**Systematic review and meta-analysis**, four databases, **3,777 papers screened, seven included, 635
participants.** Randomised trials of **automated closed-loop oxygen control (CLOC) versus manual adjustment**
in hospitalised adults; mechanical ventilation and outpatients excluded. **Primary outcome: proportion of time
spent within a target SpO₂ range.**

> **The mean proportion of time spent within the target S pO₂ range was between 81.2 and 94.9% for CLOC and
> 51.3–84.0% for manual oxygen control. The mean difference was 21.2% (95% CI 18.5–23.8, p < 0.0001),
> favouring CLOC.** Evidence of heterogeneity (**I² = 87.9**) was explained by **three studies using low-flow
> oxygen therapy, mean difference 30.9% (27.0–34.8)**, compared with **four studies using high-flow nasal
> oxygen, mean difference 12.7% (9.1–16.4).** … **Conclusions** CLOC **improves adherence to a target SpO₂
> range** compared to manual oxygen control **and is an effective approach for achieving a target SpO₂
> range.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the purest case this archive's monitoring thread has found, and it is pure
because nothing is hidden: the intervention's purpose is to hit a number, the primary outcome is hitting the
number, and the conclusion is that it hits the number.** Twenty-one percentage points more time in range is a
large, consistent and entirely believable effect. **There is not one patient outcome in the abstract** — no
mortality, no length of stay, no intubation, no hypercapnia, no hyperoxia-related harm, no desaturation
events, not even oxygen consumed. **"Effective approach for achieving a target SpO₂ range" is a conclusion
about the device's accuracy, and the review says exactly that and no more**, which is honest and is also the
whole problem.

**Whether that matters depends on a prior the review does not test: that time inside the target range is
itself worth having.** For oxygen there is a genuine case — both hypoxaemia and hyperoxaemia carry outcome
signals, and manual control spending up to half its time outside the range is a real deviation from intent.
**But "the target is correct" and "hitting the target helps" are two assumptions stacked on each other**, and
a 21-point improvement in adherence is only as valuable as the weaker of the two. **This archive has spent two weeks on
[cases where a number moved and the patient did not](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-14/)** — a processed-EEG target pushed in both
directions with identical delirium, an accurate MRSA swab that changed no antibiotic, a mean arterial pressure
raised until every catheter reading moved and lactate did not. **Closed-loop oxygen control may well be the
exception. The evidence assembled here cannot say, because it did not look.**

**The heterogeneity resolution is the best methodological part and is worth copying.** **I² = 87.9% is fully
explained by delivery mode**: low-flow **30.9 points**, high-flow nasal oxygen **12.7 points**. That is a
ceiling effect doing exactly what a ceiling effect should — **high-flow manual control already keeps patients
in range most of the time, so there is less room to improve**, and the automation's value is largest where
manual control is worst. **It is also a clean instance of the
[saturation problem logged on 15 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-15/)**: an instrument with little headroom shows a small difference, and the small difference is about
the headroom, not the device.

**Scope note: seven trials and 635 participants** is a small evidence base for a strong conclusion, and
**mechanically ventilated patients were excluded**, so none of this speaks to closed-loop FiO₂ control in the
ICU — which is where the outcome question would bite hardest.

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1183/16000617.0167-2026) · *European Respiratory Review*

## 3. Lung ultrasound beats central venous pressure for fluid management — on an outcome that is partly the ultrasound

**Journal · Published:** *Annals of Medicine*, **9 October 2026** — **eight days old**, 0 citations, open
access. **Randomised controlled trial, 181 patients** undergoing elective **percutaneous nephrolithotomy**, a
procedure in which irrigation fluid is absorbed and can raise **extravascular lung water**. **Conventional
CVP-based fluid management versus lung-ultrasound-guided protocolised management** with predefined
intervention thresholds.

> **Primary outcomes included increased incidence of EVLW and severity within 30 min postoperatively and
> postoperative hypoxemia** … **Results** Compared with Group C, **Group LUS demonstrated significantly lower
> incidence and severity of EVLW increase within 30 min postoperatively, as well as reduced postoperative
> hypoxemia rate (all p < 0.001). Group LUS displayed evidently lower LUS scores and CVP values** at T1, T2
> and T3 … **extubation time and PACU stay in Group LUS were significantly reduced** (p < 0.05).
> **Conclusions** … **reduced postoperative hypoxemia incidence, shortened extubation time and PACU stay
> duration, and improved short-term perioperative recovery.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the positive case the monitoring thread has been asking for, and it comes with a
caveat that has to be stated first.** One of the two primary outcomes is **"increased incidence of EVLW and
severity"**, and extravascular lung water here is assessed by **lung ultrasound score** — the same measurement
the intervention arm was managed to minimise. **An arm titrated to keep a score low, judged on whether the
score is low, will win.** That half of the result is close to tautological and should not be quoted as
evidence of benefit.

**The other half is not, and it is the finding: postoperative hypoxaemia fell, at p < 0.001.** Hypoxaemia is
measured independently of the guiding instrument, it is what EVLW is supposed to cause, and it moved. **So:
a measurement replaced another measurement, a protocol was attached to it, and a patient-level outcome
improved.** That is the structure the thread has lacked — against videolaryngoscopy, the EEG target, the MRSA swab, the
[blood-pressure target](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-16/) and now closed-loop oxygen, **every one of which moved its number and left the
patient where it found them.** **Extubation time and PACU stay moved too, at the weaker p < 0.05.**

**And the mechanism is specific enough to be believable.** CVP is a poor proxy for pulmonary extravascular
water at the best of times; in PCNL the insult is **absorbed irrigation fluid**, which raises lung water
without necessarily raising central venous pressure in a way that triggers a CVP-based protocol. **Lung
ultrasound looks at the organ that is actually getting wet.** That is a case where one would expect the more
direct measurement to win, which is the reverse of the usual monitoring story and is why it is worth having.

**The limitations are real and some of them are severe.** **Single centre, 181 patients, one operation** whose
fluid physiology is unusual — this does not generalise to perioperative fluid management broadly, and nobody
should read it as CVP being retired. **And the abstract reports "all p < 0.001" with no incidence figures at
all**: not the hypoxaemia rate in either arm, not the absolute difference, not a confidence interval. **So I
can tell you postoperative hypoxaemia was lower and I cannot tell you by how much**, which for a result this
archive wants to lean on is a genuine problem and the reason it is reported here with the gap named rather
than smoothed over.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1080/07853890.2026.2741437) · *Annals of Medicine*

## 4. Zero desaturations in both arms, and a careful explanation of why that proves nothing

**Journal · Published:** *BMC Anesthesiology*, **19 September 2026** — **four weeks old**, 0 citations.
**Single-centre, randomised, group-sequential non-inferiority trial**, registered **March 2015**. Low-risk
adults for elective thyroid surgery, intubation **with face-mask ventilation (rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg) or without
(rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg)**. **Primary outcome: desaturation (SpO₂ ≤ 95%) from induction to first intubation
attempt, non-inferiority margin 2% absolute.** An interim analysis was **added by protocol amendment after
enrolment had begun.**

> **The trial was stopped at the interim analysis with 288 participants (144 per group), a decision made in
> the absence of any events rather than by a stopping rule. No patient in either group reached the
> desaturation threshold (0% vs. 0%; risk difference 0%, 95% confidence interval −2.6% to 2.6%).** The gastric
> antral CSA after intubation did not differ (median 3.7 cm² in both groups; P = 0.99). **In post hoc
> analyses, gastric insufflation occurred only in the FMV group**, both by the qualitative sign (**8/144
> [5.6%] vs. 0/144; P = 0.007**) and by the antral CSA threshold (**9/144 [6.2%] vs. 0/144; P = 0.003**).
>
> **Conclusions** … **Because the trial was stopped at the interim analysis with zero events, the upper limit
> of the confidence interval for the risk difference exceeded the pre-specified 2% margin, and formal
> non-inferiority was not established.** … **These hypothesis-generating findings warrant confirmation in
> adequately powered trials before routine FMV is omitted in low-risk patients.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the most honest trial report in the archive and it is worth reading for that
alone.** The authors declare that they stopped **"in the absence of any events rather than by a stopping
rule"**; that a perfect result — zero desaturations in 288 patients — **does not establish non-inferiority**;
that their only positive finding is **post hoc**; and that practice should not change until someone runs an
adequately powered trial. **Each of those four sentences is one that a differently inclined author would have
omitted.**

**And there is a structural point behind it that generalises, which I checked rather than assumed.** With
**zero events in 144 patients**, the upper bound on the event rate is about **2.6%** — that is simply what 144
observations can exclude, and it is exactly the confidence limit reported. **So a 2% non-inferiority margin was
unachievable at this sample size even if the trial had run to completion with a perfect result.** The margin
was narrower than the best precision the design could deliver. **Stopping early did not cause the failure; it
revealed it.** **The rule worth keeping: before accepting a non-inferiority margin, check that the planned
sample could demonstrate it in the best case — the maximum achievable precision is a property of n, not of the
results.**

**On the clinical substance, such as it is.** The post-hoc finding is coherent and plausible: **face-mask
ventilation insufflates the stomach and omitting it did not** — 8 of 144 against none by the qualitative sign,
9 of 144 against none by the area threshold. Gastric insufflation is the stated reason for asking the question
at all, and finding it only where the mask was used is what the mechanism predicts. **The price is doubled
rocuronium, 1.2 against 0.6 mg/kg**, which buys faster onset and buys it with a longer block — a real trade in
a short thyroid operation and not one the trial measured. **So the readable conclusion is narrow: in low-risk
adults for elective thyroid surgery, neither approach desaturated anyone, and the mask may be putting air in
the stomach for no benefit.** The authors are right that this is a hypothesis.

**One archival note: registered 23 March 2015 and published September 2026** — eleven and a half years, with a
protocol amended mid-enrolment and a stop for want of events. **The gap is part of the story of a trial that
asked a question whose answer turned out to be "nothing happens either way".**

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04276-y) · *BMC Anesthesiology*, NCT02400931
