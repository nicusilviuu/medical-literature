## 1. GLIMPSE — 47,039 patients, and the aspiration odds ratio is 11.39

**Journal · Published:** Anaesthesia, **16 September 2026** — **published yesterday**
Potter, Cronin, Kua, Bloomfield, Murali, Beatty, McKechnie, Wong, **El-Boghdadly**, and the
GLIMPSE Resident Research Networks and Collaborators

National prospective multicentre cohort of adults undergoing **elective or emergency procedures
under the care of an anaesthetist**, screened for GLP-1 receptor agonist use in the **three months
before** the index procedure. Primary outcome the **proportion of patients receiving GLP-1 RAs**;
secondary outcomes their preoperative management, anaesthetic and airway management, and the
**incidence of pulmonary aspiration and/or regurgitation**.

**47,039 patients from 119 sites across the UK.**

- **1,348 (2.9%) reported receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists — 1 in 36 patients**
- **Peri-operative management was variable** regarding drug cessation, anaesthesia and airway
  management
- **Aspiration and/or regurgitation, non-GLP-1 cohort: 57/45,691 — 0.12% (0.09–0.16%)**
- **GLP-1 cohort: 19/1,348 — 1.41% (0.85–2.19%)**
- **Odds ratio 11.39 (6.47–20.05), P < 0.001**
- **In GLP-1 patients, events occurred most frequently during emergence from anaesthesia**
- Despite a range of airway techniques aimed at reducing risk, the incidence was **1 in 71**

**Interpretation.** This is the paper the perioperative GLP-1 question has been waiting for, and
it converts a physiological worry into a measured risk.

**An odds ratio of 11.39 with a lower confidence bound of 6.47 is not a marginal signal.** The
[BJA adolescent gastric ultrasound study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-09/) sent on
9 September found **80% of GLP-1-treated adolescents had solid gastric contents after a median
13-hour fast** — a sonographic surrogate, and I noted at the time that the leap from "solids
visible" to "will aspirate" was exactly the leap the adult literature had been arguing over.
**GLIMPSE closes that gap with the clinical event itself, in 47,039 patients.** The surrogate was
telling the truth.

**The two numbers to carry are 1 in 36 and 1 in 71.** One in thirty-six of your list is on one of
these drugs — and that is a UK figure from a period of rapid uptake, so it is a floor, not a
ceiling. One in seventy-one of those patients regurgitated or aspirated. Against a background rate
of roughly 1 in 800, that is a different operation.

**"Events occurred most frequently during emergence" is the finding that should change practice,
and it cuts against the instinct.** Most of the anxiety about GLP-1 agonists has been directed at
induction — rapid sequence, cricoid pressure, awake technique. This says the risk concentrates at
the other end, where the airway is being removed, protective reflexes are returning unevenly, and
a full stomach is still full. **Extubation planning, not just induction planning.**

**The honest caveats.** GLP-1 use was **self-reported**, so misclassification is certain and would
bias toward the null in the comparator group. The GLP-1 cohort differs systematically — obesity,
diabetes, and their own aspiration risks — and the abstract reports an **unadjusted odds ratio**
with no mention of confounder adjustment. An effect this large is unlikely to be confounding
alone, but the 11.39 is not a causal estimate. And **19 events** is a small numerator: the
confidence interval, 6.47 to 20.05, is correspondingly wide.

**What it does not tell you is what to do**, and the authors are careful about that: management
*"was variable"*, a **range of airway techniques was observed**, and **the incidence was still 1 in
71 despite them**. That is a null on mitigation, not an endorsement of any particular strategy —
and it is the part that should unsettle anyone following existing GLP-1 guidance, because the
strategies already in use did not bring the rate down to baseline.

*Note on a gap: this archive has never covered a society guideline on perioperative GLP-1
management — not ASA's, not any multisociety statement. Given a national incidence of 1 in 36 and
an eleven-fold risk, that is a hole worth filling, and it has gone to the guidelines watch as a
targeted search.*

[Read the study](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.70380) · PMID 42748560

## 2 and 3. Rebound pain after brachial plexus block — an RCT and a network meta-analysis, two days apart

Two papers on the same problem landed in different journals within 48 hours, and they answer
different halves of it. Rebound pain — the transient severe pain as a single-shot block wears
off — is one of the genuine costs of regional anaesthesia, and until recently had no agreed
prevention.

### The trial: transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation

**Journal · Published:** Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine, **15 September 2026** — **two days
old** · ChiCTR2500107694
Zhang, Lu, Lin, Yang, Zhao, Qian, Xu, Han, Li

Single-centre, **randomised, participant- and assessor-blinded, sham-controlled** trial.
**120 adults** for elective arthroscopic shoulder surgery under ultrasound-guided **single-shot
interscalene block**, randomised 1:1 to **active taVNS or sham**, applied perioperatively and
continued on postoperative days 1 and 2. Primary outcome **incidence of rebound pain within 24
hours**.

- **Rebound pain: 12/60 (20.0%) with taVNS vs 26/60 (43.3%) with sham**
- **Absolute risk reduction 23.3% (7.2–39.5%); NNT 4.3 (2.5–13.9); RR 0.46 (0.26–0.82), P = 0.006**
- **Resting pain −0.50 (−0.63 to −0.37)**; **movement-evoked pain −0.39 (−0.65 to −0.14)**
- **Higher QoR-15 on days 1 and 2**, better sleep quality on the night of surgery and night 1
  (all P < 0.01)
- **Cumulative sufentanil: GMR 0.67 (0.44–1.01), P = 0.056** — not significant
- **No stimulation-related adverse events**

### The synthesis: Bayesian network meta-analysis of six interventions

**Journal · Published:** European Journal of Anaesthesiology, **17 September 2026** — **published
today** · PROSPERO CRD420251251811
Ma, Chen, Zhou, Jin, Han

**22 RCTs, 1,699 patients**, comparing **dexamethasone, esketamine, dexmedetomidine, liposomal
bupivacaine, continuous nerve block and combined (multi-approach) nerve block** against placebo.

- **Reduced rebound pain incidence: dexamethasone OR 0.22 (0.11–0.42); dexmedetomidine OR 0.23
  (0.08–0.68); continuous nerve block OR 0.04 (0.01–0.33)**
- **Lower 12-hour pain: dexamethasone MD −2.54 (−4.76 to −0.26); continuous nerve block MD −3.10
  (−5.86 to −0.23)**
- **Lower 24-hour pain: combined nerve block MD −0.86 (−1.59 to −0.13)**
- **SUCRA ranked continuous nerve block first** for rebound pain and for 12- and 24-hour scores
- The authors' own framing: findings are **"exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than
  definitive"**, and SUCRA rankings **"reflect relative probabilities rather than absolute
  efficacy"**

**Interpretation.** Read together, these describe a problem with several working answers and no
comparison between them.

**The network meta-analysis says the boring answer wins.** Continuous nerve block — a catheter
instead of a single shot — has by far the largest effect, **OR 0.04**, and ranks first on every
outcome. That is not surprising: rebound pain is what happens when a block wears off, and a
catheter is the intervention that stops it wearing off. The interesting part is that
**dexamethasone, at OR 0.22, gets most of the way there for the cost of a single intravenous
dose**. For most practices that is the pragmatic choice, and it is well supported.

**The taVNS trial is the more surprising result and the weaker evidence.** An NNT of 4.3 is large
for any perioperative intervention, and the trial is properly conducted — sham-controlled,
participant- and assessor-blinded, intention-to-treat. But it is **120 patients at a single
centre**, and three features invite caution. The **pain score differences are tiny** — 0.50 and
0.39 points on presumably an 11-point scale, statistically significant and clinically
imperceptible — which sits oddly beside a 23-point absolute reduction in a dichotomous outcome
defined by a pain threshold. **Opioid consumption did not differ** (P = 0.056). And a
**43.3% sham rebound pain rate** is at the high end of reported incidence, which makes the
absolute risk reduction easier to achieve.

**The gap neither paper fills is the comparison.** The network meta-analysis did not include
taVNS; the taVNS trial compared against sham, not against dexamethasone. Nobody has asked whether
a neuromodulation device beats 8 mg of dexamethasone, and the burden is on the device.

**One methodological note worth flagging.** The network meta-analysis reports **OR 0.04 with a
confidence interval of 0.01 to 0.33** for continuous nerve block — a 33-fold span, the same shape
as estimates I have declined to quote twice this month. Here it is more defensible because the
mechanism is obvious and the direction is certain, but **the magnitude should not be quoted as a
number.** "Continuous block substantially reduces rebound pain" is supported; "continuous block
reduces rebound pain by 96%" is not.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2026-108256) · PMID 42744405 ·
[Read the network meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1097/eja.0000000000002486) · PMID 42750605

## 4. Readmission after CABG in women and men — the difference is risk, not sex

**Journal · Published:** Interdisciplinary CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery, **16 September
2026** — **published yesterday**
Arnreiter, Kaider, Angleitner, Pljakova, Rizvanovic, Osipenko, Klaus, Riebandt, Bergmair,
Zuckermann, **Gaudino**, Sandner (Vienna, with Weill Cornell)

Nationwide **Austrian Cardiac Surgery Registry** data for CABG **2015–2021**, linked to the federal
social insurance claims database. **Cumulative incidence functions accounting for the competing
risk of death**, plus cause-specific Cox models.

**12,169 patients, 2,192 (18%) women.**

- **Cardiovascular readmission, women vs men: 1 year 19.1% vs 17.3%; 4 years 37.8% vs 34.9%;
  6 years 46.9% vs 43.4% (P = 0.003)**
- **All-cause readmission: 1 year 42.2% vs 37.6%; 4 years 69.6% vs 66.7%; 6 years 79.1% vs 75.9%
  (P = 0.0004)**
- **After adjustment, female sex was NOT independently associated with cardiovascular readmission
  — HR 1.01 (0.94–1.10), P = 0.73 — or all-cause readmission — HR 0.99 (0.94–1.05), P = 0.74**
- **Earlier readmission correlated with earlier cardiovascular death (ρ = 0.36) and all-cause
  death (ρ = 0.39)**

**Interpretation.** The unadjusted and adjusted results point in different directions, and the
paper is explicit about which one to believe: *"Long-term readmission after CABG is driven
primarily by clinical risk rather than biological sex."*

**A hazard ratio of 1.01 with a confidence interval of 0.94 to 1.10 is about as null as
epidemiology gets.** Women are readmitted more often after CABG — that part is real and visible
at every timepoint — but once comorbidity is accounted for, **being female carries no independent
risk.** The excess is explained by what women having CABG bring to the operation: older age,
more diabetes, more hypertension, smaller vessels, later presentation.

**That is a finding with a genuine practical edge and an easy misreading.** The misreading is
"sex doesn't matter, so stop looking" — wrong, because the higher event rate is real and the
patients in front of you are the ones with it. The correct reading is the authors': **target the
comorbidities, not the demographic**, because the demographic is a marker for them rather than a
mechanism.

**The competing-risk methodology deserves specific credit**, and it connects to the sharpest paper
this archive has carried. The [ventilator liberation benchmarking study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-14/)
sent on 14 September showed that ignoring death as a competing event **inverted which hospitals
were outliers** — weighted κ of 0.00. Readmission has exactly the same structure: a patient who
dies cannot be readmitted, and treating death as censoring silently flatters the sicker group.
**Doing this correctly is not optional in a population with 6-year mortality this high**, and it
is still not universal.

**The correlation between early readmission and early death** — ρ around 0.36 to 0.39 — is modest
but points at readmission as a marker of trajectory rather than a discrete event. Nothing in this
paper suggests preventing the readmission would change the trajectory.

Limits: registry and claims data, so readmission reason is coded rather than adjudicated; a single
national health system with its own admission thresholds; and **18% women** is the usual CABG sex
ratio, which means the female estimates rest on 2,192 patients.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/icvts/ivag260) · PMID 42750126
