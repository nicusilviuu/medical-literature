## The instrument was there the whole time

**Europe PMC indexes a `FIRST_IDATE` field — the date a record was first indexed. Querying it answers in one
pass the question four days of publication-date arithmetic could only approach.**

| `FIRST_IDATE` | Records indexed |
| --- | --- |
| 1–10 October (daily range) | 2,503 – 6,531 |
| 11 October | **188** |
| 12 October | 2,877 |
| **13 October** | **63,473** |
| **14 October** | **2** |
| **15 October** | **1** |

**Three things fall out of that column, and all three are measurements rather than inferences.**

**First, ingest has stopped, and this is now a direct observation.** Two records on 14 October, one on
15 October, against a normal 2,500 to 6,500. **And the three records are not new literature at all**: an
*American Journal of Nursing* news item from March 2025, a 1996 piece in the *British Journal of General
Practice* titled "Anecdotes and empiricism", and a 1938 *Journal of Anatomy* review of an "Atlas of Skeletal
Maturation". **Nothing published recently has entered the database in two days.**

**Second, that single `MED` record is the whole of yesterday's standing action, and it is not a restart.**
`MED` moved from 41,178,083 to 41,178,084 — its first movement in four days, which is exactly what I said to
watch for. **The record is nineteen months old and is a news item about food labelling.** Reporting "`MED`
moved" without identifying what moved would have been technically true and substantively misleading.

**Third, the restore was one batch, not a two-day process.** **63,473 records carry an index date of
13 October, and every single one is `PMC` — zero `MED`, zero preprints.** I described it on 13 and 14 October
as a restoration unfolding across two days, because that is when the counts moved. **The counts lagged the
batch.** The event was a single ingest of 63,473 PubMed Central records on 13 October, against the 61,746 lost
on the 12th, which also explains the overshoot.

## And the reference window I adopted four days ago is not an ingest detector

**This is the part worth recording against myself.** On [11 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-11/) I retracted the rolling trailing-window
decomposition and replaced it with **fixed-boundary reference windows**, on the reasoning that a window with
fixed dates cannot be distorted by its own boundaries moving. **That reasoning was correct and incomplete.**
Today:

| | 14 Oct | 15 Oct | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole database | 49,018,570 | 49,018,571 | **+1** |
| 1–30 September | 148,993 | **148,918** | **−75** |
| 1–7 October | 20,218 | **20,584** | **+366** |

**The database gained one record and the two reference windows moved by 441 records between them, in opposite
directions.** Both numbers were read three times and are stable. **With three records indexed in two days,
neither movement can be ingest.** The records were already in the database; **their first-publication dates
were rewritten.**

**So the fixed window measures window membership, not ingest**, and membership changes when the dates change —
which for a bibliographic database is an ordinary housekeeping operation, probably a correction pass after the
reload. **A September-to-October reassignment is exactly what fixing online-first versus issue dates looks
like.** The 291-record net gain across the two windows means roughly 290 more came from somewhere I had no
baseline for, which is why **today's measurement establishes the full 2026 decomposition** — 1 January to
31 July 1,195,756; August 150,675; September 148,918; 1–7 October 20,584; 8 October to year end 5,844;
**summing to the 2026 total of 1,521,777 exactly.**

**That is the third revision of this instrument in five days** — whole-database total retired as a primary
metric on 9 October, rolling decomposition retracted on 11 October, fixed windows demoted today. **Each
revision came from the instrument being contradicted by data, not from foresight, and the honest summary is
that I was measuring a proxy for ingest for four days while the database exposed the thing itself.** **The
reference windows stay in the record — they are a genuine signal about dates — but `FIRST_IDATE` is the ingest
measure from now on, and the test for a restart is a day with thousands of records indexed, not a day when a
window moves.**

## 1. Cefepime: a 94% posterior probability of harm that rises to 99% when you use only published trials

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA Network Open*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**, 0 citations, open
access. **Systematic review and hierarchical Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis**, searched to
**25 May 2026** across seven databases and two trial registries. **110 randomised trials, 22,608 patients**
(cefepime 11,726, comparator β-lactam 10,882), monotherapy against any β-lactam monotherapy or identical
combinations. **GRADE applied.**

> **Results** Synthesis of 110 trials including 22 608 patients … showed a **94.4% posterior probability that
> the pooled OR mortality exceeded 1 with cefepime** use compared with other β-lactams (**778 [6.6%] vs 674
> [6.2%]; OR, 1.10, 95% credible interval, 0.98-1.24**). **Meta-analysis of published peer-reviewed RCTs (73
> trials, 15 411 patients) showed that cefepime was associated with a 98.6% posterior probability of higher
> mortality** compared with other β-lactams (**OR, 1.17; 95% credible interval, 1.02-1.34**). **Posterior
> distributions generally favored higher odds of mortality across all comparator β-lactams, across all clinical
> indications, and with doses of 2 g or more every 12 hours.**
>
> **Conclusions** … cefepime **was associated with higher odds of all-cause mortality** compared with other
> β-lactams. These findings **support a nuanced discussion of cefepime's safety in guidance statements.**

**Interpretation.** **The headline and the interval say different things, and both are correct — which is the
whole lesson of this paper.** The pooled credible interval is **0.98 to 1.24**: it includes no effect. Under
frequentist reporting this is a null result and the paper would read "no significant difference in mortality."
Under Bayesian reporting the same posterior says there is a **94.4% probability that cefepime is worse.** **The
data did not change. The question did.** A frequentist test asks whether the data would be surprising if there
were no difference; the posterior asks how much of the plausible range sits on the harm side. **With 1,452
deaths across 22,608 patients and a point estimate of 1.10, the answer to the first is "not quite" and to the
second is "almost all of it."**

**And the sensitivity analysis runs the wrong way, which is the finding I would not have predicted.** Dropping
to the **73 published peer-reviewed trials** — excluding 37 trials and 7,197 patients from registries and grey
literature — **strengthens the signal**: posterior probability 94.4% to **98.6%**, odds ratio 1.10 to **1.17**,
credible interval now **1.02 to 1.34**, excluding no effect. **Publication bias is normally feared in the
other direction**: the published record is expected to flatter the drug, and adding unpublished data is
expected to reveal harm. **Here the unpublished and registry data attenuated a harm signal.** Two readings
are available and the abstract does not settle between them — the unpublished trials may genuinely be
reassuring, or they may be smaller and noisier and pull the pooled estimate toward the null. **Either way,
anyone quoting this paper must say which analysis they are quoting**, because the two give different answers
to "does the interval exclude no effect."

**Clinically, the consistency is what makes it hard to dismiss.** The direction held **across every comparator
β-lactam, across every clinical indication, and at doses of 2 g or more every 12 hours.** A signal that
survives that much subgrouping is not an artefact of one indication or one comparator. **Cefepime is a
first-line choice in febrile neutropenia and in gram-negative infection at moderate risk of AmpC β-lactamase
production**, so this is not a marginal agent. **The authors' own framing — "support a nuanced discussion of
cefepime's safety in guidance statements" — is the right strength**: this is not evidence to stop using
cefepime, it is evidence that the guidance documents recommending it should say something about mortality and
currently do not.

**The honest caveat, and it matters: 6.6% against 6.2% is an absolute difference of 0.4 percentage points.**
Even taking the harm as real, the number needed to harm is in the hundreds. **A 94% probability of a small
effect is not the same as a large effect**, and the posterior probability is a statement about direction, not
magnitude.

[Read the meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.33017) · *JAMA Network Open*

## 2. TRAIN in traumatic brain injury: a fragility index of 1, and an odds ratio that is mostly a scale change

**Journal · Published:** *JAMA Network Open*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**, 0 citations, open
access. **Preplanned secondary analysis of the TRAIN randomised trial**, 72 ICUs in 22 countries,
September 2017 to December 2022. **The TBI subset: 486 patients, 475 in the primary analysis** (mean age 46.8,
71.4% male). **Liberal transfusion at Hb < 9 g/dL versus restrictive at Hb < 7 g/dL** for up to 28 days.
**Primary outcome: unfavourable neurological outcome — Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended 1 to 5 — at 180 days.**

> **Results** … **534 RBCTs were administered in the liberal transfusion strategy group, compared with 246
> RBCTs in the restrictive group.** At 180 days … **138 patients (58.5%) in the liberal group had unfavorable
> neurological outcome compared with 161 patients (67.4%) in the restrictive group (relative risk, 0.86 [95%
> CI, 0.75-1.00]; P = .047; fragility index = 1).** There were no significant differences in … 28-day
> mortality: 42 of 240 (17.5%) vs 51 of 244 (20.9%); RR, 0.84 (0.58-1.21); P = .34 … **After adjustment for
> several confounders, being randomized to the liberal group was associated with a lower observed probability
> of unfavorable neurological outcome (odds ratio, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.38-0.94]; P = .03).**
>
> **Conclusions** … a liberal strategy **was associated with a lower risk** … **These findings should be
> interpreted with caution in light of the inherent uncertainty of the estimate.**

**Interpretation.** **The investigators report their own fragility index, which is unusual and should be
copied.** A fragility index of 1 means **one patient moving between outcome categories would make the primary
result non-significant**, and the upper confidence bound is **exactly 1.00**. They then say the estimate
carries "inherent uncertainty," which is a plain statement rather than a hedge. **This archive has spent the
week on results whose significance depended on an analysis choice; here the authors flag the dependency
themselves, in the abstract, with a number.**

**The adjusted odds ratio needs unpacking, because it looks like a much bigger effect than it is.** An
**OR of 0.60** against an **RR of 0.86** invites the reading that adjustment revealed a 40% reduction.
**It did not — most of that gap is the odds scale.** The restrictive arm's event rate is 67.4%, which is far
from rare, so odds and risk diverge sharply. Converting the adjusted OR back at that baseline:

| Adjusted OR | Implied liberal-arm risk | Implied risk ratio |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.60 (point) | 55.3% | **0.82** |
| 0.38 (lower bound) | 44.0% | 0.65 |
| 0.94 (upper bound) | 66.0% | **0.98** |

**On the risk scale the adjusted estimate is about 0.82 against an unadjusted 0.87** — a modest shift, not a
transformation. **With a common outcome, an odds ratio is not a risk ratio and quoting "0.60" as a 40%
reduction overstates the result by roughly double.** That is a routine error and this paper is a clean case
for checking it.

**Clinically, what the trial actually shows is an absolute difference of about 9 percentage points in
unfavourable neurological outcome at 180 days, bought with 534 transfusions instead of 246** — more than
double the blood for a result whose confidence interval touches no effect and whose fragility index is one
patient. **And mortality did not move** (17.5% vs 20.9%, P = .34). **So the signal is in function among
survivors, not survival**, which is both biologically plausible in TBI — an injured brain is unusually
sensitive to oxygen delivery — and exactly the kind of outcome where a 180-day Glasgow Outcome Scale
dichotomised at 5/6 can shift on judgement.

**One detail for this archive's mismatched-denominator tally, now at five trials in two weeks.** The primary
outcome uses **236 and 239 patients (475)**; 28-day mortality uses **240 and 244 (484)**. The nine-patient
difference is almost certainly differential missingness at 180 days versus 28 days, which is legitimate —
**but the two results are reported side by side with different denominators and the abstract does not say
so.** Reconstructing percentages catches it in one pass.

## 3. Dexamethasone halves rebound pain after nerve blocks — and the satisfaction measure had no room to move

**Journal · Published:** *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**,
0 citations, open access. **Substudy pooling two randomised, placebo-controlled three-arm trials**, June 2022
to May 2023, **159 participants** having osseous surgery of the upper or lower limb under peripheral nerve
block. **Dexamethasone 12 mg oral, 24 mg oral or 12 mg intravenous (103 participants) versus oral or
intravenous placebo (56).**

> **Results** … **The median pain score after block cessation was 5.0 points (IQR 3.0 to 8.0) in the placebo
> group and 3.0 points (IQR 1.0 to 6.0) in the dexamethasone group. Rebound pain occurred in 20 of 56
> participants (35.7%) in the placebo group and in 19 out of 103 participants (18.4%) in the dexamethasone
> group.** Dexamethasone significantly reduced pain after block cessation (**mean difference −1.7 points, 95%
> CI −2.6 to −0.8, p < 0.001**) and the risk of rebound pain (**risk ratio 0.51, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.88,
> p = 0.014**). **We found no association between patient satisfaction and rebound pain. However, the
> satisfaction measure exhibited a ceiling effect with most participants reporting maximum satisfaction and
> therefore the ability to detect a difference may have been limited.**

**Interpretation.** **The clinical result is clean and useful: rebound pain nearly halved, 35.7% to 18.4%, and
pain at block cessation 1.7 NRS points lower.** Rebound pain — the surge when a block wears off, often at home
and at night — is one of the real costs of regional anaesthesia and one of the commonest reasons a patient
remembers an otherwise excellent block badly. **A risk ratio of 0.51 with an interval from 0.30 to 0.88 is a
substantial effect from a cheap, familiar drug**, and the trials used three different dexamethasone regimens,
oral and intravenous, pooled together. **That pooling is the main limitation to state**: it answers "does
systemic dexamethasone help" and not "which route or dose," and the substudy is a post-hoc pooling of two
trials rather than a prespecified comparison.

**The satisfaction finding is the methodologically interesting half, and it is a fifth distinct way a
measurement fails.** The authors looked for an association between satisfaction and rebound pain, found none,
and then said why their own null may be uninformative: **"the satisfaction measure exhibited a ceiling effect
with most participants reporting maximum satisfaction."** **The instrument had no headroom.** Everyone was
maximally satisfied, so there was nowhere for dissatisfaction to register, so the question could not be
answered by this measure in this population.

**Set against this archive's monitoring thread, that is new.** The thread holds: the measurement changes
(videolaryngoscopy); the calibration changes (remimazolam and BIS); the number is not on the causal path
(AlphaMax); the number reverses while the disease continues ([capillary refill,
yesterday](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-14/)); an accurate measurement is not an intervention
(STOP-Vanc, yesterday). **This one is: the instrument is saturated, so a
real difference cannot appear.** It is the most ordinary of the six and probably the most common in practice —
**any outcome scored on a bounded scale where the modal response is the maximum is at risk of it**, and
patient-satisfaction instruments are notoriously so. **A null on a saturated measure is not evidence of no
difference, and the authors deserve credit for saying so rather than reporting the null flat.**

[Read the substudy](https://doi.org/10.1111/aas.70308) · *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica*

## 4. A trial destroyed by giving the filmed staff an anonymous delete button

**Journal · Published:** *Resuscitation*, **1 September 2026** — **six weeks old**, 0 citations, **not open
access; the abstract below was read via Europe PMC.** **A post-mortem of the NeoBeat randomised trial**,
Stavanger University Hospital, Norway. **This was yesterday's queued lead** and is published here fourth
because the three papers above carry more direct clinical weight; it is the most unusual document of the four.

> **Objective** Evaluating if **employee instigated deletion of newborn resuscitation videos** resulted in
> selection bias compromising the validity of the NeoBeat trial.
>
> **Method** The NeoBeat randomized clinical trial … evaluate if **continuously displayed ECG heart rate
> starting from birth** resulted in a higher proportion of treated newborns receiving **ventilation within
> 60 s. Videos of resuscitations were used to evaluate time to ventilation. Employees could request deletion
> of videos by e-mail.** Halfway through the trial, **protests over video recordings led to 12-month pause in
> data collection. An anonymous solution for employee video deletion request was implemented, resulting in a
> surge of video deletions.**
>
> **Result In total, 7343/10,362 (71%) newborns** of gestational age ≥34 weeks **were enrolled before and
> 2566/4417 (58%) after** implementation of the anonymous opt-out solution. **Time of birth and video were
> available for 171 and 80 newborns** … **loss of 0 and 14 cases before and after, leaving 171 and 66 cases
> for comparison. There were no differences in participant characteristics, but significantly higher Apgar
> scores, shorter duration of ventilation, and fewer admissions after** implementation.
>
> **Conclusion** … **systematic differences among included newborns, strongly suggesting a selection bias. The
> trial was terminated halfway without power to conclude on outcomes.**

**Interpretation.** **This is a published account of a trial's own destruction, written by its investigators,
and it deserves to be read by anyone who collects video or audio as an outcome measure.** The mechanism is
simple and was not foreseeable as a bias: **the people who could delete the data were the people being
evaluated by it.** While deletion requests went by e-mail and were therefore attributable, nothing much
happened. **When deletion was made anonymous, deletions surged** — and the cases that survived had
**higher Apgar scores, shorter ventilation and fewer admissions.**

**The direction is the finding, and I want to state carefully what it does and does not establish.** What the
data show is that **the remaining cases after the anonymous route opened were systematically less severe.**
The natural inference — that staff were preferentially deleting recordings of the harder resuscitations — is
not something the paper demonstrates and not something it claims; **it reports the systematic difference and
calls it selection bias, which is the defensible statement.** The distinction matters because the alternative
explanations are real: the 12-month pause, a changed case mix, or changed practice could each contribute.
**But "no differences in participant characteristics" cuts against a case-mix explanation**, and that is the
comparison the authors made.

**Note what this trial was testing, because it sharpens the irony without being the point: whether
continuously displaying a measured heart rate from birth gets newborns ventilated faster.** A trial about
making a measurement visible was ended by the measurement of the measurers becoming visible. **For this
archive's monitoring thread, this is a distinct failure mode from the other five — not a number that misleads,
but the act of measuring changing who ends up in the sample.** Observation altering the observed is familiar
in principle; a concrete, quantified, published instance in a clinical trial is not.

**Two practical lessons, and they are both design lessons rather than findings.** First, **an opt-out from
data collection that is both anonymous and retroactive is not a privacy feature bolted onto a trial — it is a
missingness mechanism, and it can be correlated with the outcome.** Second, **the enrolment figures show the
damage before the outcome analysis does**: 71% of eligible newborns enrolled before, 58% after. **A drop in
enrolment fraction after a protocol change is a warning that something has become selective**, and it was
visible without waiting for the videos.
