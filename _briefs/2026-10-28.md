## 1. Eight points more first-attempt success, and not one complication fewer

**[Videolaryngoscopy versus direct laryngoscopy for nasotracheal intubation in infants undergoing cardiac
procedures (NasoVISI): a multicentre, randomised controlled
trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2352-4642(26)00239-7)** — *The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health*, **first
published 27 October 2026**. Paywalled; abstract read through the Europe PMC index. Funded by the Anesthesia
Patient Safety Foundation and The Children's Hospital of Philadelphia. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05433155.

**A proper trial, and the design deserves saying in full.** Eight US paediatric cardiac centres; infants
365 days or younger **without anticipated difficult airways** needing nasotracheal intubation for elective
cardiothoracic surgery or cardiac catheterisation; **randomised 1:1 by permuted blocks stratified by site**;
**684 randomised, 673 analysed** by modified intention to treat, **340 videolaryngoscopy and 333 direct
laryngoscopy**; **generalised estimating equations to account for clustering within intubating clinicians**,
with complications compared by the same marginal models. Median age 14.04 weeks, mean weight 4.77 kg.

**The primary result:**

> First-attempt success was higher in the videolaryngoscopy group than in the direct laryngoscopy group
> (**281 [83%] vs 249 [75%]; adjusted risk difference 8.2% [95% CI 2.0–14.3], p = 0.0093**).

The crude difference is 7.9 points (82.6% against 74.8%) and the adjusted 8.2, with an exactly symmetric
interval. **An 8.2-point absolute gain is one additional first-attempt success for about every twelve infants
intubated.**

**And the complications:**

> First-attempt complications occurred in 85 (13%) participants overall, with similar incidences between
> groups (45 [13%] vs 40 [12%]; **0.4% [−3.5 to 4.3], p = 0.83**). Considering all attempts cumulatively, any
> complication occurred in 94 (14%) participants overall (46 [14%] vs 48 [14%]; **−1.9% [−6.0 to 2.3],
> p = 0.38**). **No cases of cardiac arrest or death occurred in either group.**

**Read the opening sentence of the abstract against that.** It states the premise as *"successful intubation
at first attempt reduces the risk of complications."* **The trial improved first-attempt success by 8.2 points
and found no difference in complications on either analysis.** That is not a contradiction — the trial was
powered for the process measure, not the outcome — **but it does mean the thing that moved is the proxy and
not the thing the proxy stands for, and the abstract does not say so.**

**The arithmetic shows why no difference was detectable, and it also shows the benefit is probably real.**
**143 of 673 infants failed at the first attempt — 59 in the videolaryngoscopy arm and 84 in the direct
laryngoscopy arm.** Across both arms, moving from first-attempt complications (85) to all-attempt
complications (94) adds only **nine** patients. **Eight of those nine are in the direct laryngoscopy arm
(40 → 48) and one in the videolaryngoscopy arm (45 → 46).** So the extra complications that arise from
repeated attempts fall almost entirely where the repeated attempts were — **which is the mechanism the trial's
premise proposes, visible in the counts and far too small to reach significance.** The all-attempts adjusted
difference of −1.9 points points the same way.

**My reading.** **This is a well-conducted trial that answers its question and should be read for what it
measured.** First-attempt nasotracheal intubation is easier with a videolaryngoscope in infants with normal
airways at specialist centres, by about eight points. **The complication benefit is a nine-patient signal in
the expected direction, and nobody should quote it as a finding.** The conclusion's own framing is right —
this *"adds to growing evidence favouring videolaryngoscopy as the first-line technique"* — and its list of
remaining questions is the honest one: **difficult airways, non-specialist centres, urgent settings, and how
training should change as direct laryngoscopy becomes the technique nobody practises.** That last question is
the one that will matter in ten years and the one no trial is designed to answer.

## 2. A review of a worry that happened once in 714 patients — and which, again, does not report device failure

**[Supraglottic airway vs. endotracheal tube for sinonasal surgery: a systematic review and
meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00405-026-10650-w)** — *European Archives of Oto-Rhino-Laryngology*,
**first published 26 October 2026**. **Nine studies, 714 patients, spanning 1995 to 2024.**

The question is whether a supraglottic airway is safe in endoscopic sinus surgery, where blood runs
continuously towards an unprotected larynx. **The answer on the outcome everyone worries about:**

> **Aspiration was rare (one asymptomatic ET case.)**

**One event, in the endotracheal tube arm, asymptomatic, across 714 patients.** **So the review cannot
estimate aspiration risk at all, and the pooled results are necessarily about other things:**

| Outcome | Effect | 95% CI | I² |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Airway contamination** | **RR 0.27** | 0.15–0.47 | 0% |
| **Day 0 cough** | **RR 0.39** | 0.23–0.68 | 0% |
| Day 0 sore throat | RR 0.65 | 0.24–1.74 | **68.7%** |

Hoarseness, blood loss and operative time trended towards the supraglottic airway without reaching
significance. All three intervals are symmetric on the log scale.

**Three observations.**

**First, the contamination figures are not an estimate.** The review reports device contamination as **"SGAs
0–86%, ETs 53–77%"**. **A range running from zero to eighty-six per cent spans almost the whole possible
interval**, and the review's own gloss — *"of limited clinical consequence"* — is the right judgement about
something it cannot measure. The pooled RR of 0.27 for contamination is a relative statement sitting on top of
that.

**Second, the evidence base spans twenty-nine years of device evolution and the review does not flag it.**
A supraglottic airway in 1995 is a first-generation classic laryngeal mask with no gastric drain; in 2024 it
is a second-generation device with an oesophageal drainage channel and a higher seal pressure — **and gastric
drainage is precisely the feature that bears on the aspiration question being asked.** Pooling across that
span treats a changing intervention as one intervention.

**Third, and this now holds across two papers in three days: neither reports device failure.** **The
[26 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-26/) carried a randomised trial of the classic laryngeal
mask against a tube in the prone position which named "need for conversion of airway technique" as a primary
outcome and gave no result for it.** **Today's review, on the same device question in a different operation,
lists its primary outcomes as aspiration, contamination and post-operative symptoms — conversion and device
failure are not among them.** **So the literature now holds two recent comparisons of supraglottic airway
against tracheal tube in bleeding or positionally awkward surgery, and in neither can a reader find out how
often the supraglottic airway had to be exchanged.** That is the number a clinician needs in order to accept
the trade the papers are offering.

**My reading.** **The review's conclusion is defensible and carefully worded:** *"The historical concern that
SGAs offer inferior airway protection during ESS is not supported by current comparative evidence."* **Note
what that sentence does and does not say — it says the concern is unsupported, not that it is refuted**, and
with one aspiration event in 714 patients that is the strongest available statement. The closing caveat is the
right one too: *"findings should assist rather than replace clinical judgment."* **The useful positive finding
is the cough result** — RR 0.39 on day 0 with no heterogeneity — which is a real, if modest, patient-relevant
benefit, and the one thing here that does not depend on the aspiration question.

## 3. Fewer deaths, and no less brain injury

**[Air versus supplemental oxygen for resuscitation of term or late preterm infants at
birth](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd014781.pub2)** — *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*,
**first published 27 October 2026**. Searched to 12 January 2026. **Twelve studies, 2,054 participants.** No
dedicated funding. Cochrane RoB 2; GRADE for every outcome; **fixed-effect model**.

| Outcome | Effect | 95% CI | Studies | n | Certainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **All-cause mortality before discharge** | **RR 0.72** | **0.57–0.91** | 10 | 1,879 | **Low** |
| **HIE stage II–III in hospital** | RR 0.88 | 0.71–1.10 | 5 | 1,384 | Low |
| Mortality beyond discharge | RR 0.82 | 0.58–1.16 | 3 | 668 | Low |
| Neurodevelopmental impairment | **RR 1.24** | 0.74–2.10 | 3 | 414 | **Very low** |

All four intervals are symmetric on the log scale and every I² is 0%.

**The central tension is between the first two rows.** **Air reduces death before discharge by an estimated
28%, and does not reduce moderate-to-severe hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy.** **HIE stage II–III is the
proximate injury through which an oxygen-strategy effect on neonatal death would be expected to travel**, and
its estimate is 0.88 with an interval crossing 1.

**Three readings are available and the review does not choose between them, correctly.** The mortality benefit
may be real and mediated by something other than less hypoxic-ischaemic injury — oxidative stress and
reperfusion injury from 100% oxygen are the usual candidates, and they are not measured by a Sarnat stage.
Or the mortality estimate may be inflated: **"the risk of bias was high for most studies"** and the certainty
is low. Or HIE may simply be underpowered at five studies and 1,384 infants. **This archive's recorded
asymmetry applies and is worth restating in this form: a null on the proximate injury marker weakens a
mortality claim without refuting it, because the marker may not be the route.**

**The long-term neurological outcome runs the other way, and should be stated plainly rather than skipped.**
**Neurodevelopmental impairment is RR 1.24 — numerically favouring supplemental oxygen — on three studies,
414 infants, very low certainty.** That is close to no information, and it is not evidence of harm from air.
**But a review that reports a mortality benefit should not have its NDI row quietly omitted from the summary a
reader takes away, and the point estimate is above 1.**

**And then the limitation that matters most clinically, which the authors put first in their own conclusions:**

> **All 12 included studies compared air with 100% oxygen without titration to available oxygen saturation
> targets. Therefore, we are unable to draw conclusions about the optimal starting oxygen concentration when
> titration is available.**

**Nobody resuscitating a term infant today chooses between air and untitrated 100% oxygen.** The real question
is the starting fraction when a saturation target and a blender are available, **and this review states
explicitly that it cannot answer it.** **Three of its seven pre-specified outcomes had no data at all** — NDI
at twelve months or later as protocol-defined, days on invasive respiratory support, and length of
hospitalisation — and subgroup analysis by gestational age was impossible. There was no difference between
high-income and low- and middle-income settings.

**My reading.** **The honest summary is that air beats 100% oxygen on mortality with low certainty, that the
mechanism does not show up where it should, and that the comparison is not the one clinicians face.** The
value of the review is in its negatives: it records exactly which outcomes nobody has measured, which is the
specification for the trial that should follow. **A review whose most useful output is a list of missing data
has done its job**, and it is a better document than one that would have pooled titrated and untitrated
strategies together to produce an answer.

**Archive context: neonatal resuscitation oxygen strategy is new here.** The nearest held items are a neonatal
intubation respiratory-support meta-analysis logged today and abstract-free, and a neonatal
ventilator-associated pneumonia prevention review in the brief queue.

## 4. A difference between studies, reported as a fact about patients

**[Anticholinergic Drug Burden and Postoperative Delirium: A Systematic Review and
Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accpm.2026.101957)** — *Anaesthesia, Critical Care & Pain Medicine*,
**first published 27 October 2026**. PROSPERO CRD420261294941. Searched to February 2026. **Ten reports from
nine independent cohorts, 10,457 participants.** Random-effects pooling; NOS for risk of bias; GRADE.

**The results are modest, precise and plausible:**

- Patients who developed postoperative delirium had higher anticholinergic burden scores:
  **SMD 0.31 (0.20–0.43)**.
- **Each one-point increase in continuous burden: OR 1.22 (1.09–1.37).**
- Both low-to-moderate and high burden were associated with raised risk — **a graded relationship, which is
  the most persuasive feature here.**
- Significant specifically for the ACB scale.
- **Certainty of evidence: low.**

**Then one sentence of result, and one sentence of conclusion that does not follow from it.**

> Result: **"The association was attenuated in studies that excluded patients with cognitive impairment."**
>
> Conclusion: **"Baseline cognitive impairment appears to amplify this association."**

**The result is a difference between studies. The conclusion is a statement about individuals.** Going from
one to the other is an ecological inference, and it is not supported: **no patient-level interaction between
cognitive impairment and anticholinergic burden is reported anywhere in the abstract.**

**And a simpler explanation fits the same observation.** **Anticholinergic burden is itself higher in people
with cognitive impairment** — antimuscarinics for bladder symptoms, sedating antihistamines, tricyclics,
antipsychotics are all disproportionately prescribed in that group — **and cognitive impairment is the single
strongest known risk factor for postoperative delirium.** **So in cohorts that include those patients, the
burden score is partly a marker for the condition that causes the delirium.** Excluding them removes the
confounding and the association shrinks. **That is confounding, not effect modification, and it predicts
exactly the attenuation observed.** The two explanations are not distinguishable from study-level data, and
the abstract asserts the one that supports a clinical recommendation.

**My reading.** **The underlying association is probably real and the dose-gradient is the reason to believe
it:** a graded increase across burden levels, with an odds ratio of 1.22 per point, is the shape a causal
relationship takes, and the mechanism — impaired central cholinergic transmission — is specific and
pharmacologically coherent. **The recommendation the paper draws is also sound on its own terms and barely
needs this evidence**: preoperative review of anticholinergic medication in an older surgical patient is good
practice whether or not the burden score causes delirium, because most of those drugs have thin indications
in that population anyway.

**What should not survive is the amplification claim.** **If cognitive impairment amplifies the effect, then
burden reduction matters most in the cognitively impaired; if it confounds the effect, burden reduction may do
very little in exactly those patients.** Those are opposite operational conclusions, **the abstract picks one,
and the data cited cannot choose.** The paper's own closing call for *"prospective head-to-head comparisons of
anticholinergic burden scales"* is the right next step and would not settle this; **what would settle it is a
reported interaction term.**

**Archive context: postoperative delirium prevention is a standing thread here** — the dexmedetomidine
meta-analysis in the [25 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-25/) and the preoperative
cognitive-training review in the [26 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-26/). **Today's paper is
the first in that thread to address a modifiable exposure present before the patient reaches the anaesthetic
room.**

## 5. A service that measured its own planning errors in both directions

**[Observational study for perioperative intensive care unit (OPICU): Planned and unplanned ICU admissions,
follow-up, discharge, and outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2026.112323)** — *Journal of Clinical
Anesthesia*, **first published 27 October 2026**. **Multicentre, prospective, across 75 ICUs.**

Four groups: planned admission, unplanned admission, **planned but not admitted**, and emergent admission.
**55,963 patients preoperatively evaluated; 2,764 (4.9%) planned for ICU admission.**

**The two numbers that make this worth reading are the two ways the plan was wrong:**

- **804 of the 2,764 planned admissions — 29.1% — could not be admitted because of limited ICU beds.**
- **Of the 1,960 who could be, 411 — 21.0% — did not require postoperative ICU care at all.**

**So nearly three in ten planned ICU admissions were refused for want of capacity, and one in five of the rest
turned out to be unnecessary.** **The two errors run in opposite directions, the study measures both, and that
is a rarer thing than either finding.**

**The rest reconstructs cleanly.** 1,960 minus the 411 who did not need it leaves **1,549**, which is exactly
the stated 78.2% of the 1,980 patients actually admitted. Of those 1,980: **78.2% planned, 12.5% emergent,
9.1% unplanned.** POSSUM scores separated the planned-but-not-needed group (lower) and the emergent group
(higher) from the planned group. **Benefit indicators — vasopressor requirement, ventilation beyond 12 hours,
arrest events — were significantly higher in the unplanned and emergent groups than in the planned group
(p < 0.001)**, which is the study's central point: **the patients who actually needed intensive care were
disproportionately the ones nobody had planned for.**

Independent predictors of unplanned admission: **emergency surgery OR 12.4, intraoperative complications
OR 3.1, ASA above 2 OR 2.7** — **no confidence intervals are given for any of the three in the abstract**,
which is a real omission for odds ratios offered as a prediction rule. Minimally invasive surgery and regional
anaesthesia reduced admissions. **Overall ICU mortality 5.9% and 90-day mortality 7.5%, rising to 18.1% and
18.9% in the emergent group.**

**One discrepancy, reported as an observation rather than an error.** The abstract says the 1,980 admitted
patients are **"3.09% of all cases"**, but 1,980 of the 55,963 preoperatively evaluated is **3.54%**. **A
3.09% share implies a denominator of about 64,100** — so "all cases" is a larger population than the
preoperatively evaluated cohort, plausibly including patients who never received a preoperative assessment.
**The second denominator is never stated, and a reader comparing the two percentages cannot reconcile them
from the abstract.**

**My reading.** **The 29.1% refusal rate is the finding, and it reframes everything else.** A prediction rule
for who needs intensive care is only useful where the bed exists; **in a system refusing three in ten planned
admissions, improving the prediction redistributes a shortage rather than resolving it.** The paper's
conclusion — that combining modified Wang criteria, POSSUM, intraoperative complications, emergency status and
ASA class predicts the need for ICU admission — is about the prediction. **The 804 is about the capacity, and
it is the larger number.**

**And it pairs directly with yesterday's entry.** The [27 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-27/)
carried a UK nationwide study finding that enhanced care units with beds **reserved exclusively for elective
cases** had about half the odds of cancellation, and those **permitting emergency admissions** 61% higher odds.
**Today's paper measures what that looks like from inside the queue: 804 patients planned for a bed that was
not there.** **Two papers, two countries, two days — one measuring the structural feature and the other the
consequence.**
