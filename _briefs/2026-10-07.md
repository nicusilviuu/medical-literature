dates.** The *European Journal of Anaesthesiology* deposited a full issue under 7 October 2026. **One of the
16 is plausibly new. Nine are correspondence. The rest are second deposits, under new DOIs, of papers
published online between October 2025 and March 2026** — and the abstracts live on the older records, not
today's. **The trap and the papers are both below**, dated honestly.

## 1. A recurrence gap that grew after adjustment, and was still reported as equivalence

**Journal · Published:** *Interdisciplinary CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery*, **1 October 2026** — six
days old, and **the only genuinely new paper in today's brief.** Retrospective three-institution cohort with
propensity score matching. Carried on the queue since 1 October.
Ueda, Mimae, Kudo, Nagashima, Miyata, Ito, Ikeda, Okada

> **Objectives** The prognosis of invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma has not been well investigated because of
> its rarity, especially with regard to early-stage disease… **Methods** Patients with pathological stage IA
> lung adenocarcinoma, excluding pT1mi tumors, who underwent surgery at three institutions were
> retrospectively examined… **Propensity score matching was applied to reduce baseline imbalances.**
>
> **Results** Among the **1228 patients, 1128 had non-mucinous adenocarcinoma and 100 had invasive mucinous
> adenocarcinoma.** Before matching, invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma was significantly associated with
> **smaller pathological whole tumour size, larger pathological invasive tumour size and a lower frequency of
> lymphovascular invasion.** The 5-year overall survival rates were **92.5% and 92.7%**, and the 5-year
> recurrence-free survival rates were **87.5% and 92.3%**… Baseline covariates including pathological tumour
> size and stage were generally balanced after matching. The 5-year overall survival rates were **90.4% and
> 92.7%**, and the 5-year recurrence-free survival rates were **83.7% and 93.3%**, respectively, **with no
> significant differences in survival curves between groups.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **These findings suggest that mucinous histology alone should not be considered an
> adverse prognostic factor in early-stage disease.**

**Interpretation. Put the four recurrence-free survival numbers in order and the problem is visible without
any statistics.**

| | Invasive mucinous | Non-mucinous | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5-year RFS, **before** matching | 87.5% | 92.3% | **4.8 points** |
| 5-year RFS, **after** matching | **83.7%** | 93.3% | **9.6 points** |
| 5-year OS, after matching | 90.4% | 92.7% | 2.3 points |

**Adjustment doubled the recurrence gap, and the paper's conclusion is that histology does not matter.**
That is the opposite of the usual pattern, where controlling for confounders shrinks an apparent difference
toward the null. Here it moved **away** from the null — and the abstract tells you why, in its own baseline
description: **before matching, the mucinous tumours had two favourable features (smaller whole tumour size,
less lymphovascular invasion) against one unfavourable one (larger invasive size).** Balancing on size
removed part of the advantage the mucinous group started with, and the recurrence difference got bigger.

**So "no significant difference" here is an underpowered null, not a demonstration of equivalence.** With
**100 mucinous patients and roughly a 16% recurrence rate, there are on the order of 16 recurrence events in
that arm.** A 9.6-point difference in five-year RFS cannot be either confirmed or excluded on 16 events, and
the honest statement is that the study **cannot tell** whether mucinous histology carries extra recurrence
risk at stage IA. **The conclusion as written — that mucinous histology alone should not be considered an
adverse prognostic factor — is stronger than the data support**, and a surgeon reading only the conclusion
would take away the opposite of what the point estimates show.

**The overall-survival finding is real, though, and it is the useful part.** Overall survival was **90.4%
against 92.7%** after matching, a 2.3-point gap, while recurrence-free survival differed by 9.6. **Recurrences
in the mucinous group are not translating into deaths within five years** — which fits what is known about
invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma clinically: it recurs intrapulmonary rather than distantly, and
intrapulmonary recurrence is often resectable or slowly progressive. **That is a genuinely reassuring message
for a patient, and it does not require the equivalence claim.**

**This lands squarely on a thread this archive has been building.** Yesterday's [obstetric team-training
meta-analysis](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-06/) had the inverse shape: the outcome with the **best**
evidence was null and the one with a signal had conflicting designs. **Here a single cohort gives a null on
the endpoint it is underpowered for and a near-match on the endpoint that matters most to the patient, and
the paper leads with the null.** The general rule the two together support: **read which endpoint the
certainty attaches to before reading the conclusion.**

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/icvts/ivag272) — paywalled; the abstract above is the full deposited
abstract.

## 2. TIVA against sevoflurane on carbon and on cost — and the cost percentages in the abstract are the wrong way round

**Journal · Published:** *European Journal of Anaesthesiology*, **online 23 December 2025** — nine and a half
months old, **in print in today's issue** under a second DOI
(`10.1097/eja.0000000000002479`). Retrospective simulation over **11,909 consecutive patients** at a Belgian
tertiary hospital, costed on local tender prices with lifecycle assessment for drugs and disposables.
Van Belleghem, Rex, Teunkens, Vereecke, Kalmar

> **Results** For every 1000 procedures, CO<sub>2</sub>e emissions and costs were: **TIVA — 963 kg at a cost
> of €4300; minimal-flow sevoflurane — 25 562 kg at a cost of €6772; sevoflurane [2 l min<sup>-1</sup> fresh
> gas flow (FGF)] — 59 483 kg at a cost of €11 933.**
>
> **Conclusion** Our simulations indicate that **minimal flow sevoflurane produces 26.5 times more CO<sub>2</sub>e
> than TIVA, rising to 61.8 times more at 2 l min<sup>-1</sup> FGF.** TIVA also costs significantly less, **at
> 36% and 63% of sevoflurane anaesthesia costs with minimal flow and 2 l min<sup>-1</sup> FGF,
> respectively.**

**Interpretation. The emissions arithmetic checks exactly and the cost arithmetic does not.** 25,562 ÷ 963 =
**26.5** and 59,483 ÷ 963 = **61.8**, both as stated. But **€4,300 ÷ €6,772 = 63.5%** and **€4,300 ÷ €11,933 =
36.0%** — so TIVA costs **63% of minimal-flow sevoflurane and 36% of sevoflurane at 2 l/min.** The abstract
says *"36% and 63% … with minimal flow and 2 l min<sup>-1</sup> FGF, respectively."* **The two percentages are
transposed relative to the order in which the conditions are named.**

**Nothing about the paper's argument changes** — TIVA is cheaper in both comparisons either way, and the
direction and the raw euro figures are unambiguous. **But the sentence a reader is most likely to quote is
wrong in detail, and it is only checkable because the authors gave the underlying numbers in the same
abstract.** That is the argument for reporting absolute figures alongside ratios, and this archive has now
caught the same class of error twice in six weeks.

**On the substance: the emissions result is not close, and that is the point.** A factor of **26.5** between
TIVA and the most conservative volatile technique available — minimal-flow sevoflurane, which is what a
carbon-conscious department already does — means **flow optimisation is not a substitute for changing agent.**
The usual departmental response to anaesthetic carbon is to reduce fresh gas flow; this simulation says that
gets you from 61.8× to 26.5×, and the remaining 26.5× needs a different drug.

**Three limits worth stating, because a simulation is not a measurement.** It is **a single Belgian hospital's
case mix, priced at that hospital's tender prices** — drug prices vary several-fold between health systems,
and the cost conclusion travels much less well than the emissions one. **Lifecycle assessment of disposables
is the softest input**: propofol's footprint depends on syringes, giving sets, and on **what happens to unused
drug**, and the modelling choices there are not visible from the abstract. And **the comparison is
maintenance only** — induction, and the propofol used for it in both arms, is outside the model.

**Today's issue carries a published critique that goes at exactly those points.** A correspondence item
appears under today's date — *"Revisiting the economic conclusions of TIVA versus sevoflurane: the critical
impacts of BIS monitoring and propofol wastage"* (`10.1097/eja.0000000000002484`) — which names **two cost
items the model appears to omit: depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring, which TIVA practice generally requires and
volatile practice generally does not, and propofol discarded unused.** **That correspondence has no deposited
abstract**, so I cannot tell you what it concludes or by how much it moves the figures — only that the
critique exists and which two inputs it targets. **Both are on the cost side, which is the side whose
percentages are transposed and which travels least well between countries.** **The emissions finding is the
robust one; treat the cost finding as contested from today.**

## 3. Delirium in recovery as a marker, not an incident

**Journal · Published:** *European Journal of Anaesthesiology*, **online 22 January 2026** — eight and a half
months old, in print today under `10.1097/eja.0000000000002504`. Single-centre retrospective cohort,
**3,781 patients aged 70 or over**, elective noncardiac noncranial surgery under general anaesthesia,
2020–2022. Screened with **4AT** and supplemented by **CHART-DEL** chart review.
Weiss, Zarour, Hikry, Talmy, Itkin, Zegerman, Goren, Cohen, Matot

> **Results** PACU-POD was diagnosed in **9.1% of patients (341/3781)**, who were more likely to be older,
> **77.1 vs. 75.7 years**; frailer (**52 vs. 40%**); cognitively impaired (**36 vs. 18%**); and undergoing
> high-risk (**52 vs. 27%**), prolonged surgeries, **196 vs. 142 minutes** (all P < 0.001). **Ward-POD occurred
> in 6.2% of patients (234/3781) and was significantly more common among those with PACU-POD (24.6 vs. 4.3%,
> P < 0.001). PACU-POD was independently associated with ward-POD [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 4.4; 95% CI, 3.2
> to 6.3], in-hospital falls (aOR 2.3; 95% CI, 1.2 to 4.4) and 30-day mortality (aOR 4.4; 95% CI, 1.5 to
> 13.0)**, as well as prolonged PACU (**185 vs. 150 minutes**) and hospital stays (**6 vs. 3 days**).
>
> **Conclusion PACU-POD is a strong early marker of increased risk** for ward-POD and other postoperative
> complications…

**Interpretation. The claim being made is about screening, not about treatment, and it is well matched to the
design.** A retrospective cohort cannot say that detecting delirium in recovery improves anything; it can say
that **a patient who is delirious in the PACU has a 24.6% chance of being delirious on the ward against 4.3%
for everyone else**, and an adjusted odds ratio of **4.4** after accounting for age, frailty, cognitive
impairment and surgical risk. **The authors say exactly that and no more**, which is worth noting given how
often a cohort of this shape is written up as though it licensed an intervention.

**The number to be careful with is the mortality one.** **aOR 4.4 for 30-day death, 95% CI 1.5 to 13.0** — a
confidence interval spanning nearly a tenfold range, on 30-day deaths within a 3,781-patient elective
surgical cohort where absolute mortality is low. **The association is there; its magnitude is not
established**, and quoting "4.4-fold mortality" without the interval would misrepresent it.

**What the confounding structure allows and does not allow.** PACU-POD patients were older, frailer, more
cognitively impaired, and had longer higher-risk operations — **every one of those is also a risk factor for
ward delirium**, and adjustment handles measured confounding only. **But the clinical use proposed here
survives that objection**, because a marker does not need to be causal to be useful for triage: if 9.1% of
your over-70s screen positive in recovery and a quarter of them go on to ward delirium, **that is where the
delirium-prevention resource should go**, whether or not PACU delirium caused anything.

**Against the archive's human-factors thread, the contrast is sharp.** [Yesterday's
survey](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-06/) found **time-outs used by 16%** of French emergency clinicians
and **90% receiving less than one training session a year** on structured communication. **Here is a screening
tool — 4AT, four items, two minutes, in a unit where a nurse is already at the bedside — with a 4.4-fold
signal attached to it.** The implementation gap this archive keeps finding is not in the complicated
interventions.

## 4. A virtual reality headset, juniper oil, and half the delirium

**Journal · Published:** *European Journal of Anaesthesiology*, **online 11 March 2026** — seven months old,
in print today under `10.1097/eja.0000000000002487`. **Open access** (PMC13258079). Single-centre randomised
controlled trial, tertiary hospital in China, September 2023 – July 2024. **ChiCTR2300075624.**
Luo, Duan, Ni, Mao, Gao

> **Intervention** Participants were randomly assigned to either a **multisensory virtual reality group**, who
> received standard care along with a **20-min virtual reality session featuring a biophilic environment
> infused with juniper essential oil**, or a standard care group, who received standard care alone for 20 min
> pre-operatively.
>
> **Main outcome measures** The primary outcome was the incidence of POD, assessed **twice daily using the
> Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) for the three postoperative days.**
>
> **Results** The incidence of POD during the three postoperative days was lower in patients assigned to the
> multisensory virtual reality group (**9.4%, 16 of 170 patients**) than in those assigned to the standard
> care group (**19.2%, 33 of 172 patients**), **P = 0.010.** Multisensory virtual reality **reduced
> preoperative anxiety scores and anxiety scores during the three postoperative days, P = 0.001 for all.** No
> serious adverse events occurred during the intervention.

**Interpretation. The effect size is large, the intervention is 20 minutes of preoperative headset time, and
the trial cannot be blinded.** **9.4% against 19.2% is an absolute reduction of 9.8 points and a number
needed to treat of about 10** — 342 patients, 49 delirium events in total. For a single preoperative session
with no drug and no reported harm, that is a striking result if it holds.

**The reason to be cautious is not the effect size but the outcome ascertainment.** **Delirium assessed by CAM
twice daily is an observer-rated outcome, and no one in this trial can be blinded to whether a patient spent
20 minutes in a headset smelling of juniper.** The intervention also **reduced anxiety scores at every time
point, P = 0.001 for all** — which the authors offer as a mechanism, and which is equally an explanation for
how an unblinded assessor's impression of a calmer patient becomes a lower delirium rating. **A trial of a
pleasant, obvious, unblindable intervention measured by observer judgement is the configuration where
performance and detection bias are hardest to exclude**, and that is a design limit rather than a criticism
of the conduct.

**What would settle it is already specified in the trial's own design.** CAM twice daily for three days is a
reasonable schedule; **the missing piece is an assessor who does not know the allocation**, which is
achievable even when the patient cannot be blinded. **Until then the honest reading is: a large effect on an
unblinded observer-rated outcome, in one centre, consistent with a reduction in anxiety that was measured the
same way.**

**It belongs with item 3 rather than apart from it.** Item 3 says a quarter of patients delirious in recovery
will be delirious on the ward and identifies who to target; this trial says a cheap preoperative intervention
may halve the incidence. **Together they describe the two halves of a delirium pathway — who, and what —
with the "who" resting on 3,781 patients and the "what" on 342.** The asymmetry is the state of the field.

[Read the trial](https://doi.org/10.1097/eja.0000000000002380)

*Stated as a rule, because it will recur every month: <em>EJA</em> deposits each paper **twice** — once at
online-first publication with a full abstract, once at print publication with none — and **the two DOIs differ
in the fourth-from-last digit, while the titles differ in punctuation** ("anaesthesia." against "anaesthesia:")
so an exact-title match will not pair them. **A sweep that trusts the deposit date would have reported five
papers up to a year old as new today.** The pairing route that works is a title search on a distinctive phrase
with the journal filter, reading all hits rather than the newest.*

*Also from today's issue and not written up: **the effects of intraoperative low-dose dexmedetomidine on
postoperative systemic inflammation and cognitive outcomes** (`10.1097/eja.0000000000002523`, online-first

*A tooling defect found today, before it reached the page. The helper that strips markup from a deposited
abstract used `<[^>]+>`, which **deletes everything between a real `<` and the next `>`** — so an abstract
containing `P < 0.001` silently loses the rest of its Results section. **On the PACU delirium paper it removed
every effect estimate**, and I was two minutes from writing that the deposit had no results in it. **The fix
is to strip only named tags**, and a check of every abstract quoted here over the past week found **no
published quote was affected** — three of them did contain a bare `<`, and in each case the text had been read
from the raw record. **Recorded so the next run inherits the fixed stripper rather than the habit.** A related
discipline adopted today: **mark an elision inside a quotation rather than silently dropping a statistic** —
yesterday's biopsy-trial quote tidied away three χ² values without saying so.*
