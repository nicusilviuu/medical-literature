## The lagged pass, three days late, found three JAMA trials this archive never reported

**The lagged pass is a publication-date re-sweep of a window ten to forty days old, run to catch what the
daily index-date route misses. It was due around 10 November. It was not run on the 10th, 11th or 12th —
twice because the index was frozen and once because the day's sweep was already large — and each time this
archive said so and moved on.** It was run today.

**Window 6 October to 5 November by publication date, trial titles, in scope: 750 records, 750 collected,
shortfall zero.** After removing everything already in the archive, **166 unreported in-scope records with
readable abstracts remain.** Among them, published on one day — **14 October 2026** — three trials in *JAMA*:

- **VASSPR** — vasopressin initiation threshold in septic shock, 3,959 patients, 13 hospitals (item 1 below)
- **Isoflurane-based inhaled sedation** in mechanically ventilated adults with acute hypoxaemic respiratory
  failure (`10.1001/jama.2026.18471`)
- **Intraoperative dobutamine plus norepinephrine** to prevent postoperative acute kidney injury
  (`10.1001/jama.2026.21322`)

**And a fourth, from 7 October: a target trial emulation of vasopressin initiation timing in sepsis**
(`10.1177/08850666261479700`), which asks VASSPR's question observationally.

**I checked whether the index could be blamed, and it cannot.** All three *JAMA* records carry
`firstIndexDate` **19 October**; the emulation carries **16 October**. **Those are ordinary dates that the
daily route was reading — there were briefs published on both.** **So the restamping chaos of mid-October is
not the explanation, and I am not going to offer one I cannot support.** What can be said: 16 October was
the rollback day, when every record first indexed on or after 25 September briefly vanished and the brief was
written during the nineteen minutes it lasted — **a real candidate for the emulation, and no candidate at all
for the three *JAMA* trials.** The retrieval defect found on 5 November, where a sweep reported 107 records
and returned 73, is the other candidate, and it is unproven here. **Four trials with ordinary index dates
were missed by the daily route and the cause is not established.**

**What is established is the instrument.** **The publication-date lagged pass catches what the index-date
daily route misses — that is precisely the job it was built for, and three days of deferral cost three weeks
of delay on a 3,959-patient trial.** The 166 unreported records are now the queue's backlog, and the four
items above are reported here with their real publication dates, never as new.

**One more thing the pass settled.** The paged collector built today uses `cursorMark` with
`resultType=core`, and it worked — **750 records across eight pages, shortfall zero.** See the method record:
**the 5 November diagnosis of why the retrieval failed was wrong, and the standing to-do built on it is

## 1. VASSPR: randomising when to start vasopressin changes practice by twenty-five points and mortality by nothing

**[Effect of Vasopressin Initiation Threshold on Septic Shock Mortality: The VASSPR Cluster Crossover
Randomized Strategy Trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.21333)** · *JAMA* · **published 14 October
2026 — four weeks old, recovered by today's lagged pass and never reported here before** · paywalled;
abstract quoted below

**Cluster-randomised, cluster-crossover, pragmatic strategy trial. 13 hospitals in Idaho and Utah, February
2024 to July 2025, crossing over monthly. 3,959 adults receiving vasopressors for septic shock.** The two
strategies: start fixed-dose vasopressin when other vasopressors reach **0.1 µg/kg/min** of norepinephrine
equivalent, or when they reach **0.4 µg/kg/min**.

> Of the patients more likely to experience differential management under the tested hospital-level
> vasopressin dose strategies (maximum vasopressor dose ≥0.07 µg/kg/min of norepinephrine or equivalent),
> **1034 of 1521 (68.0%)** cared for under the lower-threshold strategy received vasopressin compared with
> **651 of 1526 (42.7%)** cared for under the higher-threshold strategy. … the maximum vasopressor dose
> received prior to vasopressin initiation was a median of 0.14 (IQR, 0.10 to 0.22) µg/kg/min … vs 0.34 (IQR,
> 0.16 to 0.42) µg/kg/min … Mortality at 28 days occurred in 628 of 1975 patients (31.8%) … vs 655 of 1984
> patients (33.0%) … (adjusted marginal odds ratio, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.87 to 1.06], P = .62; adjusted risk
> difference, -0.4 [95% CI, -2.7 to 1.3] percentage points).

**Every figure reconstructs: 628 of 1,975 is 31.80%, 655 of 1,984 is 33.01%, and the differential-management
subset is 3,047 of 3,959 patients, 77.0% of the trial.**

**Read the separation first, because it is what makes the null interpretable.** **Vasopressin use went from
42.7% to 68.0% — a 25.3-point difference — and the median dose at which it was started went from 0.34 to
0.14 µg/kg/min.** A cluster trial of a recommendation can fail because nobody follows the recommendation;
this one did not. **The strategies were implemented, practice moved substantially, and mortality did not:
aOR 0.98, adjusted risk difference −0.4 points, with an interval excluding anything larger than a 2.7-point
benefit or a 1.3-point harm.** Kidney replacement therapy-free days: median 29 in both arms, aOR 1.01.

**This settles something this archive has been circling.** An observational study reported a **hazard ratio
of 2.55** for vasopressin in septic shock, which this archive used as its standing example of confounding by
indication being quoted as a drug effect — sicker patients get the second vasopressor. **A target trial
emulation of the same timing question was published a week before VASSPR and also missed by the daily
route.** **Now there is a randomised answer, and it is that the threshold does not matter for mortality.**
**The 2.55 was never a drug effect, and the cleanest possible demonstration is a trial in which the drug's
timing was randomised and nothing happened.**

**Two technical notes.** **Both intervals are asymmetric about their point estimates** — the odds ratio's log
arms differ by about 1.5 to 1 and the risk difference's by 1.35 to 1 — which is expected from a marginal
estimate out of a generalised linear mixed model and is **not** the symmetric Wald interval this archive's
reconstruction normally assumes. **And the crude mortality difference is 1.21 points in favour of the lower
threshold while the adjusted risk difference is −0.4 points**: adjustment for site and baseline
characteristics shrank the difference by two thirds, which is the right direction for a cluster-crossover
trial in which months and hospitals differ.

**My reading.** **Stop treating the vasopressin threshold as a decision that carries mortality.** Within the
range 0.1 to 0.4 µg/kg/min it does not, in 3,959 patients, with practice genuinely separated. **What the
trial cannot say is whether vasopressin itself helps** — both arms used it, 42.7% against 68.0%, so this is
a timing trial and not a placebo comparison.

## 2. FRAGILE: a relative risk pointing the other way from the title

**[Off-Pump versus On-Pump Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting in Prefrail and Frail
Patients](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.125.078842)** · *Circulation* · **published 10 November
2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Multicentre, randomised, open-label. Patients aged 60 or over classified as prefrail or frail, randomised
1:1 between January 2018 and December 2024. 508 patients: 258 off-pump, 250 on-pump; median age 68
(IQR 65.0–73); 30.5% female.**

> The primary composite outcome occurred in 28 patients receiving OPCAB (10.9%) and 40 patients receiving
> ONCAB (16.0%; relative risk, 1.47 [95% CI, 0.94-2.31]; P =0.09). Surgical wound infection was more frequent
> with ONCAB (20 [8.0%] versus 9 [3.5%]; P =0.04). … Estimated 1-year survival probabilities were 96.1% and
> 92.8% in the OPCAB and ONCAB groups, respectively ( P =0.099).

**The reader trap is in the first sentence, and it is worth stating plainly.** The title reads "off-pump
versus on-pump", the sentence names off-pump first, and the relative risk is **1.47** — which a reader takes
to mean off-pump carries 47% more risk. **It does not. 16.0 divided by 10.853 is 1.474: the relative risk is
on-pump relative to off-pump, the reverse of the order in which both the title and the sentence name the
arms.** **The off-pump-relative-to-on-pump risk ratio is 0.68.** Everything in the trial favours off-pump
numerically; the stated 1.47 is the number a careless reading inverts.

**The arithmetic checks, including the p-values.** 28 of 258 is 10.853% against a stated 10.9; 40 of 250 is
exactly 16.0%. **The confidence interval is log-symmetric to within 0.005, and reconstructing from it gives
z = 1.68 and p = 0.093 against a stated 0.09.** **For the wound infection difference the uncorrected
two-proportion z gives p = 0.028 against a stated 0.04, which identifies the authors' test as an exact or
continuity-corrected one rather than contradicting it.**

**So what did the trial find?** **The primary composite missed significance at P = 0.09 and one-year survival
missed it at P = 0.099, while the only significant result is more wound infections with on-pump — and all
three point the same way.** **This is an underpowered trial, not a null one.** 508 patients over seven years
across multiple centres is 72.6 a year, and a 5.1-point difference on a composite with 68 total events was
never going to reach significance.

**Two cautions on the composite.** **It is open-label, and two of its six components — reoperation for
bleeding and acute respiratory distress syndrome — are judged by the unblinded team.** **And the
neurocognitive outcome, which is the reason frailty makes this question interesting, showed no difference at
one year** — reported as a null in a trial underpowered for its primary endpoint, so it is uninformative
rather than reassuring.

**My reading.** **Off-pump looks better here on every measured axis and the trial cannot prove it.** For a
prefrail or frail patient the wound infection result — 8.0% against 3.5%, the one significant finding — is
the concrete thing to carry away.

## 3. Oxygen delivery on bypass: a real signal, and two numbers that imply different effects

**[Oxygen Delivery during Cardiopulmonary Bypass and Postoperative
Mortality](https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000006393)** · *Anesthesiology* · **published 12 November
2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Retrospective cohort, 4,358 adults having cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass at one tertiary
centre, 2018 to 2024.** Time-weighted average indexed oxygen delivery (DO₂i) from pump flow, arterial
saturation, haemoglobin and body surface area. **75 deaths within 30 days (1.72%).**

> Each 10-ml · min -1 · m -2 decrease in DO 2i was associated with increased odds of mortality (adjusted odds
> ratio, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.23; P < 0.001). The adjusted 30-day mortality was 1.69% (95% CI, 1.32 to
> 2.05%) at the 25th percentile of DO 2i (226.2 ml · min -1 · m -2 ) compared with 0.74% (95% CI 0.41 to
> 1.07%) at the 75th percentile (295.6 ml · min -1 · m -2 ). Lower DO 2i was also associated with AKI
> (adjusted OR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.03 to 1.07) … but not with stroke (adjusted OR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.95 to 1.06;
> P = 0.877).

**Here is an internal inconsistency worth reporting and not adjudicating.** **The interquartile spread is
295.6 − 226.2 = 69.4, which is 6.94 units of 10. At an odds ratio of 1.16 per unit, the model implies
1.16^6.94 = 2.80-fold odds across that spread. The quoted percentile mortalities imply 1.69% against 0.74%,
an odds ratio of 2.31.** **Those are not the same number**, and the gap is large enough that the two results
cannot both come from the same linear model — a spline or a model evaluated at different covariate values
would explain it, and the abstract does not say which. **Report the discrepancy; do not pick a side.**

**A second observation the paper does not make.** **The adjusted mortality at the 25th percentile, 1.69%, is
essentially the cohort's own crude mortality of 1.72%.** So the contrast is not "the low quartile is
dangerous" but "the high quartile is unusually safe" — the same data, a different sentence, and a different
implication for a threshold.

**And the endpoint pattern is the one this archive has catalogued.** **DO₂i tracks mortality, tracks
creatinine-defined acute kidney injury, and does not track stroke** (OR 1.00, 0.95–1.06). **Across 33
cardiac-surgery kidney trials over 22 years, short-term creatinine endpoints produced the positive results
and harder endpoints did not** — so an association with AKI and not with stroke is exactly the shape that
pattern predicts, and it is a reason for caution rather than confidence.

**The confounding is structural and the paper's design cannot remove it.** **Low DO₂i is produced by low
haemoglobin, low pump flow or low saturation. Anaemia, small body surface area, long complex operations and
sicker patients all push it down**, and all independently raise mortality. The authors are appropriately
modest — they call for "future mechanistic and interventional studies" rather than a target.

**My reading.** **The signal is probably real and the number to intervene on is not established.** **75
deaths is a small event count behind a dose-response claim**, and this archive's rule applies: count the
events before crediting the precision of an odds ratio reported to two decimals.

## 4. A sepsis bundle triggered by an early warning score: nine deaths against four, reported as no difference

**[National Early Warning Score 2-Guided Sepsis Hour-1 Bundle Care for Adults With Suspected Sepsis in the
Emergency Department (NEWS-1 TRIPS)](https://doi.org/10.1097/CCM.0000000000007447)** · *Critical Care
Medicine* · **published 12 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**A pilot type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial: stepped-wedge cluster randomised, four emergency
departments in Hong Kong, with focus groups and a staff survey.** The model, "NEWS-1 care", triggers the
Surviving Sepsis Campaign Hour-1 Bundle objectively when **NEWS-2 is 5 or more**.

> All progression criteria were met, except for suboptimal overall bundle compliance (56.1%). Exploratory
> intention-to-treat analysis (standard care group, n = 102; NEWS-1 group, n = 98) showed no significant
> between-group difference in 30-day all-cause mortality (standard care group, 3.9%; NEWS-1 group, 9.2%;
> absolute risk difference, 5.3%; 95% CI -1.6% to 12.1%) … Implementation evaluation identified 15 barriers
> and 13 facilitators.

**The headline is feasibility and the feasibility result is good: 13 of 14 progression criteria met,
compliance with lactate, blood cultures and antibiotics all significantly up.** The failure is the bundle as
a whole at 56.1%, which is the honest finding of an implementation study.

**But read the mortality sentence against its own numbers.** **3.9% of 102 is four deaths; 9.2% of 98 is
nine. The intervention arm had more than twice the mortality of the control arm, and the absolute risk
difference of 5.3 points has an interval running from a 1.6-point benefit to a 12.1-point harm.** "No
significant between-group difference" is technically correct and it conceals both the direction and the
width. **RULE: when a feasibility pilot reports an exploratory mortality comparison, state the event counts
and the direction of the point estimate, because thirteen deaths cannot support any conclusion and the
phrase "no significant difference" invites the reader to assume the arms looked alike.** **They did not.**
The interval arms are 6.9 and 6.8 points, so this is a symmetric Wald interval on a risk difference and
nothing is wrong with the statistics — only with how little they can carry.

**On the substance, this is the week's third document about sepsis triggers.** Yesterday's brief carried an
analysis of 1.85 million encounters finding that antibiotic delay predicts death for two of seven time-zero
criteria; the guidelines watch carried two competing consensus definitions of refractory septic shock that
disagree about whether a vasopressor dose belongs in the definition at all. **NEWS-1 TRIPS attacks the same
problem from the operational end: replace clinical suspicion with a score of 5 or more.** The authors'
closing line — that "real-world implementation barriers and facilitators should be considered in sepsis
management guidelines" — is the argument of all three.

**My reading.** **A worthwhile pilot whose clinical signal must not be read as reassuring or alarming**, and
whose real contribution is 56.1% bundle compliance under an objective trigger with dedicated
implementation support. **That number is the one a guideline committee should look at.**

## 5. Thirteen-fold disagreement about who has a dangerous QT

**[The correction conundrum: divergence of QTc formulae in a paediatric cardiac surgery
cohort](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1047951126124652)** · *Cardiology in the Young* · **published 12 November
2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Prospective observational study: 130 consecutive children in a paediatric cardiac intensive care unit, each
with a 12-lead ECG 24 hours after cardiac surgery.** QTc computed five ways — Bazett, Fridericia, Framingham,
Hodges, Rautaharju — plus the bundle-branch-block-specific Bogossian adjustment. Severe prolongation defined
as QTc ≥ 500 ms. **No reference standard for arrhythmic risk was available.**

> Prevalence of severe QTc prolongation ranged 10.0% ( n = 13) by Bazett versus 0.8% ( n = 1) by Fridericia
> and Framingham, 1.5% by Hodges and 4.6% by Rautaharju. Twelve of 13 children with QTc≥ 500 ms by Bazett
> were< 500 ms by Fridericia and Framingham ( P < 0.001). … Without a reference standard or arrhythmic
> events, these data show disagreement but cannot establish which formula is more accurate.

**Every prevalence reconstructs against 130: 13 is 10.00%, 1 is 0.77%, Hodges' 1.5% is 2 children, and
Rautaharju's 4.6% is 6.** **The ratio between the most and least inclusive formula is thirteen-fold, and
twelve of the thirteen children Bazett labels high-risk are not labelled by two of the alternatives.**

**Interpretation.** **This is a measurement paper and it belongs beside this week's definition papers.**
A postoperative pulmonary complication rate depends on which components you count; refractory septic shock
depends on whether a vasopressor dose is in the definition; and here a child's risk label depends on which
of five algebraic corrections the monitor happens to apply. **In each case the quantity being measured is
unchanged and the number reported moves by a factor of two to thirteen.**

**The authors' restraint is the model.** **With one child in the smallest category, no torsade de pointes,
and no reference standard, they say explicitly that they can show disagreement and cannot show which formula
is right — and they recommend only that the formula used be reported.** **Compare the trial yesterday that
claimed "high certainty evidence" for itself.** **A paper that declines to adjudicate is doing more for the
reader than one that picks a winner it cannot justify**, and the practical instruction — report your
formula — costs nothing and fixes the comparability problem the paper identifies.

**One caution: Bazett over-corrects at high heart rates, which is textbook, and postoperative children in an
intensive care unit are tachycardic.** So the direction of the disagreement is predictable; **what this
paper supplies is the size of it in exactly the population where the correction performs worst.** The
bundle-branch-block finding — Bogossian moving four of six children below the threshold — is six patients
and is labelled exploratory by the authors.
