## 1. Do-not-resuscitate status and regional analgesia for fracture pain

**Journal · Published:** *Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine*, **4 September 2026** — **three weeks
old.** Flagged as a lead in the [11 September brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-11/) and never
written up until now
Siddiqui, Rodriguez, Hu, Choi, Jano, Manohara, Marshall, Douglas, Xu

The premise is stated as a tension rather than a hypothesis:

> **Do-not-resuscitate orders are intended to prioritize patient comfort, but do-not-resuscitate
> patients may receive fewer procedural interventions.** Regional analgesia is recommended for rib and
> hip fractures and may provide superior pain control to systemic analgesics. **However, utilization
> remains low.** Whether do-not-resuscitate status is associated with the utilization of
> comfort-enhancing regional analgesia has not been studied.

Cross-sectional study of the **National Inpatient Sample**, adult rib and hip fracture
hospitalisations, **1 January 2016 to 31 December 2022.**

> **298,343 rib fracture and 462,796 hip fracture hospitalizations** met inclusion criteria, of which
> **8,568 (2.9%) and 13,730 (3.0%) received regional analgesia**, respectively. Do-not-resuscitate
> status was present in **31,882 (10.7%)** of rib fracture and **77,255 (16.7%)** of hip fracture
> hospitalizations.

> In adjusted analyses, do-not-resuscitate status was associated with **lower odds of regional
> analgesia for rib fractures (OR 0.76; 95% CI 0.69 to 0.83), but not hip fractures (OR 1.00; 95% CI
> 0.94 to 1.06).** In frailty-stratified analyses, the association… **was strongest among the least
> frail rib fracture hospitalizations (OR 0.34; 95% CI 0.25 to 0.48).**

**Interpretation.** The best item in today's brief, and the sharpest finding is in the subgroup rather
than the headline.

**Start with the baseline, because it reframes everything else: only 2.9% and 3.0% of these
hospitalisations received regional analgesia at all.** Rib and hip fracture blocks are recommended,
they work, and **97% of patients do not get one.** The DNR question sits on top of a much larger
failure of uptake, and any discussion of differential access should acknowledge that the intervention
is rare for everyone.

**The rib–hip dissociation is the structural clue.** Hip fracture blocks are increasingly protocolised
— on pathways, in order sets, often done by a named service — while **rib fracture blocks remain
discretionary, requested case by case.** DNR status made no difference where the decision was built
into a pathway (OR 1.00) and a substantial difference where it depended on someone choosing to ask
(OR 0.76). **That is a strong argument that the effect is in clinician decision-making rather than in
patient preference**, because a patient's wishes about resuscitation should not vary with how
protocolised the analgesia happens to be.

**And the least-frail finding is the one that should be uncomfortable.** Among the **least frail** rib
fracture patients — those with the most physiological reserve, the longest expected survival, and the
most to gain from good analgesia — **DNR status was associated with a two-thirds reduction in the odds
of regional analgesia (OR 0.34).** The frail patients, in whom one might at least argue that a block
carries competing risks, showed a weaker association. **The pattern is the opposite of what a
risk-based explanation predicts**, and it is consistent with DNR being read as a general instruction
to do less rather than a specific instruction not to attempt resuscitation.

**The limits are real and the authors state the right one.** This is administrative-data
cross-sectional work: DNR status and procedure codes, no timing within the admission, no pain scores,
no record of patient or family preference — so a patient who declined a block is indistinguishable
from one never offered it. **Adjusted odds ratios from the National Inpatient Sample with hundreds of
thousands of admissions will reach statistical significance on small effects**, though 0.34 is not a
small effect. And the authors' closing sentence — *"prospective studies are needed to confirm these
findings"* — is appropriate rather than defensive.

**This is the third code-status paper written up in this archive**, after the *Annals of Surgery*
cohort on treatment limitation and early death and the
[study of regret in VV-ECMO candidacy](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-22/). A fourth — the
*Journal of Critical Care* report on the 3 Wishes Project in end-of-life care — **surfaced in a sweep
on 19 September and was logged rather than written up, so nothing is claimed about it here.** **The
thread across the three is fairly clear: a documented limitation on one intervention is associated
with fewer of others that were never limited** — and in this paper most of all in the patients with
the least reason for it.

## 2. Mild hypercapnia after cardiac arrest — better cerebral oxygenation, no better outcome

**Journal · Published:** *Intensive Care Medicine Experimental*, **18 September 2026** — **eight days
old.** A comprehensive review; carried on this archive's outstanding list since the 22nd
Laosuksri, Wittayachamnankul, S. C. Chattipakorn, N. Chattipakorn

> Post-cardiac arrest care remains challenging, with persistently poor neurological and survival
> outcomes… **While maintaining normocapnia is the current standard, hypercapnia has been proposed as
> a potential intervention. However, its role and potential benefits remain unclear.**

The verdict, quoted closely:

> Observational studies have sparked interest in the potential benefits of hypercapnia during the
> post-cardiac arrest period… Methodological variations and conflicting findings from earlier studies
> have prompted the conduct of controlled trials and animal experiments to clarify its effects.
> **Mild hypercapnia appears to be a practical and clinically safe approach, improving cerebral
> oxygenation without major adverse events. However, no improvement in long-term neurological recovery
> or survival has been demonstrated.** Moreover, **the effects on the cardiovascular and other organ
> systems, as well as mechanisms at the cellular level, remain inconclusive.**

> Although mild hypercapnia **may be considered part of a comprehensive post-cardiac arrest care
> strategy**, further studies are needed to define its optimal application…

**Interpretation.** A clear, honest summary of an intervention that does what it was designed to do
without helping, which is a category this archive has been accumulating all month.

**The physiological logic was always good.** Arterial CO₂ is a dominant determinant of cerebral blood
flow; the post-arrest brain is hypoperfused; therefore raising CO₂ should raise cerebral blood flow
and oxygenation. **It does.** The review confirms mild hypercapnia improves cerebral oxygenation
safely. **And long-term neurological recovery and survival are unchanged.**

**That is the same shape as four device papers covered here this month** — the Hypotension Prediction
Index, end-tidal CO₂ as a mortality marker, the analgesia nociception index, AI nerve tracking — with
one important difference. **Those were measurements; this is an intervention.** A monitor that
measures accurately without changing outcome is disappointing. **An intervention that moves a
physiological surrogate in the intended direction without changing outcome is more informative: it
says the surrogate is not the mechanism**, or that the mechanism is not limiting. Post-arrest brain
injury may simply not be primarily a cerebral-blood-flow problem, which would explain a good deal of
the field's history.

**The lineage is worth recording because it shows how long this has taken.** The search for this
review also returned the **Carbon Control and Cardiac Arrest (CCC) trial protocol** (Eastwood,
Schneider, Suzuki, Bailey, **Bellomo** and colleagues, *Trials*, 7 April 2015), a pilot randomising
post-arrest patients to normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg) against **mild hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 50–55
mmHg)** for 24 hours, with neuron-specific enolase and S100b as biological efficacy endpoints.
**Eleven years from that pilot to a review concluding the approach is safe and does not improve
survival.** The targets in that protocol are the ones still being discussed.

**The practical reading**: *"may be considered part of a comprehensive strategy"* is guideline
language for "harmless, do it if you like." Given that the ERC/ESICM post-resuscitation care section
and the [defibrillation review](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-24/) are both pushing toward
physiology-informed, individualised post-arrest care, **mild hypercapnia is a reasonable component of
such a strategy and a poor candidate for a standalone target.**

*Access note: a review, summarised from its abstract, which is unusually complete. Its constituent
trials are not named in the abstract and were not chased individually.*

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40635-026-00974-6) · PMID 42758270 ·
[the 2015 CCC pilot protocol](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-015-0676-3) · PMID 25872502

## 3. The patient who arrives with an intrathecal pump

**Journal · Published:** *Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine*, **8 September 2026** — **eighteen days
old.** A narrative review; on the outstanding list since the 10th
Jenkinson, Jergensen, Sivanesan, **Leffert**, Brogan

> Patients with implanted **intrathecal drug delivery systems (IDDS)** present unique challenges for
> anesthesiologists in the **perioperative, peripartum, and intensive care settings.** A comprehensive
> and practical review on this topic is needed.

Three stated objectives: familiarity with **IDDS technology and therapeutics**; **the appropriateness
of neuraxial procedures in IDDS patients**; and **IDDS-related complications in the operating room and
intensive care unit.** Five sections: intrathecal pharmacology, implantation considerations,
perioperative care, neuraxial anaesthesia, and device-related safety.

> Key topics covered include **intrathecal medications, their performance under MRI, and precautions
> prior to attempting any neuraxial access in patients with IDDS.**

Method, stated plainly: PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar, manual reference screening, **and the
device manuals themselves** — with *"contextual interpretation informed by expert opinion when
evidence is limited."*

**Interpretation.** Not a new finding, and useful precisely because of that.

**This is the third document in a week whose value is that it teaches rather than recommends.** The
[SPAQI statement on patients wearing insulin pumps and CGMs](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-24/)
called itself educational material; the
[paediatric exercise consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-25/) exists to close a gap
between evidence and practice caused by clinician unfamiliarity. **Same structure here: the problem is
not that the evidence is contested, it is that most anaesthetists meet an intrathecal pump rarely
enough never to become fluent in one** — and then meet it at two in the morning in an obstetric unit.

**Reviewing the device manuals is the detail that marks it as a practical document rather than an
academic one.** The manufacturer's manual is where the refill port geometry, the interrogation
procedure and the MRI conditions actually live, and it is not indexed in MEDLINE. **A review that
went and read them is doing work its readers cannot easily repeat.**

**Two of the named topics are the ones that will matter at the bedside.** **MRI behaviour** — these
devices can be affected by the scanner, and a patient may arrive from radiology with a pump in an
unexpected state. And **precautions before any neuraxial access**, which is the situation that
actually recurs: an obstetric patient with an intrathecal baclofen or morphine pump requesting an
epidural, where the catheter's position is unknown and the consequences of puncturing it include
**abrupt intrathecal baclofen withdrawal** — a genuine emergency that few anaesthetists have seen.

**Leffert's presence in the author list matters for that reason**: she is an obstetric anaesthesia
authority, and the peripartum case is the one where this knowledge is least likely to be available and
most likely to be needed.

*Access note: a narrative review whose abstract describes structure and scope rather than
recommendations. **The precautions themselves are in the full text, which was not reachable** — and of
the three items today this is the one whose full text would be most immediately useful.*

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2025-107458) · PMID 42710951
