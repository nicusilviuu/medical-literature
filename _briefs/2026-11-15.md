## Correction 14: a past bucket can grow, and three Bookshelf records prove it

**On 19 October this archive corrected itself once on this point.** It had written that a past
`FIRST_IDATE` bucket "is complete by construction and cannot grow", found that 16 October had fallen from
37,260 to 37,243, and replaced the claim with a weaker one: **"A past bucket cannot grow and can shrink,
which makes it an upper bound rather than a count."** That replacement has been used ever since.

**It is also wrong.**

| Reading | 14 November bucket |
| --- | --- |
| Yesterday, 14:03:51 | **8,218** |
| Today, 14:05:27 | **8,221** |
| Today, 14:05:42 | **8,221** |

**The bucket grew by three, and the same read repeated fifteen seconds later gives the same figure, so it is
not a transient.** **And the mechanism is identifiable, which is what makes this more than an anomaly:**

- **`NBK`, the Bookshelf source, went from 5 records to 8 overnight — exactly +3.**
- **Querying `SRC:NBK AND FIRST_IDATE:2026-11-14` returns exactly 3.**
- **Querying the same for 15 November returns 0.**

**Three records entered the index overnight and were stamped with yesterday's date, so yesterday's bucket
grew by three.** **A past `FIRST_IDATE` bucket is therefore not an upper bound and not a count: it is a
reading at an instant, which can rise or fall.**

**This is the eighth member of a family.** Corrections 4 to 8, 11, 12 and now 14 are all the same error in
different clothes: **a structural claim about the index that went beyond "this is the reading at this
instant".** **The 19 October correction is instructive precisely because it was already a retreat — and it
still over-reached, because "cannot grow" is a claim about mechanism and the only defensible claims here are
claims about readings.** **The replacement is deliberately minimal and this archive should not improve on
it: read it, date it, label it, and assert nothing about what it must do next.**

**Yesterday's finding is weakened by today's reading too, and that needs saying.** Yesterday PubMed Central
shed 1,078 records while the 12 November bucket shed 1,075 — a three-record gap — and this archive called it
"strong evidence of a common cause, not an identity of records". **Today PMC shed 666 and the 12 November
bucket shed 555: a gap of 111, which is 16.7% of the contraction.** **So the direction reproduces — PMC falls
and the 12 November bucket is the one that falls with it — and the magnitudes do not.** **One day's
three-record alignment was closer than the relationship warrants, and this archive will not be quoting it as
a rule.**

**The rest of the reading.** Whole **49,257,564 → 49,262,989, up 5,425**, residual zero: `MED` +5,880,
`PMC` −666, `PPR` +208, `NBK` +3, `CTX` unchanged, summing exactly. **Second consecutive day with a
shrinking source, so the subtraction bound stays unavailable** — today's bucket of 6,091 is well below the
net gain in any case. September and 1–7 October are unchanged for a second and third day; **October month
fell 51, the largest single-day drift this month.**

## 1. A consensus that says wait until symptoms resolve, then declines to say what resolution is

**[Perioperative management of patients with recent concussion undergoing noncardiac extracranial surgery: A
modified Delphi consensus study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2026.112370)** · *Journal of Clinical
Anesthesia* · **published 14 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Two-round modified Delphi, November 2025 to March 2026. Thirty-five experts from 17 countries completed
both rounds, 33 of them anaesthesiologists — 94.3% of the panel.** Items drafted from a systematic search
and including case vignettes, rated on a five-point Likert scale. **Consensus defined a priori as 75%
agreement, strong consensus as 85%.**

> Thirty-five experts (33 anesthesiologists) from 17 countries completed both rounds; 13 of 16 Round 2
> statements reached consensus. Agreement was unanimous for preoperative screening for concussion symptoms
> and neurologic red flags, and for avoidance of hypoxemia, abnormal carbon dioxide tension, sustained
> hypotension, and marked hypertension. Strong consensus supported generally deferring elective surgery
> until concussion symptoms resolve (97%), preoperative counseling (97%), structured follow-up (94%), and
> extended postoperative monitoring in higher-risk patients (91%). Consensus was not reached for regional
> anesthesia or monitored anesthesia care over general anesthesia (74%), electroencephalography-based depth
> of anesthesia monitoring (73%), or cautious long-acting neuraxial opioid use (69%). **The panel did not
> define symptom resolution and established no symptom-independent waiting interval or concussion-specific
> numeric physiologic threshold.**

**The quoted sentence is the document, and the panel put it in its own abstract.** **97% agreed that elective
surgery should generally be deferred until concussion symptoms resolve, and the panel did not define
resolution, set no waiting interval independent of symptoms, and named no numeric physiologic target.**
**So the strongest recommendation in the document is unactionable as written: a clinician who accepts it
still has to decide, unaided, when a concussion has resolved.** **This is the fortnight's definitional theme
seen from inside a panel** — after a consensus on what counts as a postoperative pulmonary complication, two
disagreeing definitions of refractory septic shock, and five QTc formulas disagreeing thirteen-fold. **Here
the panel reached near-unanimity on a threshold and left the threshold undefined.**

**Now the arithmetic of the threshold, which this archive has been cataloguing all fortnight, and this is a
new point about all of them.** **Three statements failed: 74%, 73% and 69%, against a 75% bar.** **On a
panel of 35, one rater is 2.86 percentage points.** **So the statement that "failed" at 74% missed the bar
by less than a third of one person's vote** — and with "do not know" responses excluded from the
denominator, the effective denominator for any given item is smaller than 35 and the granularity coarser
still. **RULE: a consensus threshold quoted to the percentage point is meaningless unless the panel is large
enough to resolve it. At n = 35 the grid is 2.86 points wide, so 74% and 75% are the same reading.**
**Reporting that regional anaesthesia "did not reach consensus" at 74% implies a precision the method cannot
deliver.**

**The ninth distinct threshold scheme, and the first with two tiers.** 75% for consensus and 85% for strong
consensus, on a five-point Likert, with "do not know" excluded. **The catalogue now holds nine schemes across
four scale lengths, and no two are comparable.**

**What is usable here is the unanimous material, and it is worth having.** **Unanimity on preoperative
screening for concussion symptoms and neurological red flags, and on avoiding hypoxaemia, abnormal carbon
dioxide tension, sustained hypotension and marked hypertension.** **Those are physiological aims an
anaesthetist can act on without a definition of resolution**, and the reason they matter after concussion is
exactly the premise in the paper's first sentence: cerebral autoregulation may still be disturbed.
**And what remains unresolved is specific and honest: anaesthetic technique, depth-of-anaesthesia
monitoring, and long-acting neuraxial opioids.**

**The authors close by disclaiming the document's status: "The statements form an expert-informed framework,
not an evidence-based practice guideline."** **That is the second self-disclaiming document this archive has
reported in a week**, after the fluid-terminology paper that called its own matrix provisional. **Both are
better than the alternative.**

## 2. Perfusion-guided resuscitation saves a quarter of a litre and changes nothing else

**[Resuscitation strategies incorporating peripheral perfusion targets in septic shock: A systematic review
and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155829)** · *Journal
of Critical Care* · **published 13 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Five randomised trials, 2,180 patients with septic shock. Searches to 27 May 2026, PROSPERO registered,
RoB 2 and GRADE applied.**

> Compared with conventional strategies, RSIPPT were not associated with significant differences in 28/30-day
> mortality (four RCTs; RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.78-1.09; I2 = 10%; moderate CoE) or 90-day mortality (three RCTs;
> RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.83-1.05; I2 = 0%; low CoE). However, RSIPPT were associated with fluid reduction during
> the first 6-8 h of resuscitation (three RCTs; MD -264.53 mL, 95% CI -425.28 to -103.77; I2 = 1%; low CoE).
> No significant differences were observed in intensive care unit or hospital length of stay, organ
> dysfunction, vasopressor-free days, ventilator-free days, or renal replacement therapy-free days.
> **Differences in intervention algorithms and comparators limit attribution of these findings to peripheral
> perfusion monitoring itself.**

**Both mortality estimates reconstruct as clean nulls — p = 0.33 and p = 0.30 — with low heterogeneity, and
the fluid difference is exactly symmetric at 160.75 mL each side, so it is a plain Wald interval on a mean
difference.** 264.53 mL is a quarter of a litre in the first six to eight hours.

**Read this against what the week has already established about perfusion targets.** **Capillary refill time
is one of the thirteen clinical criteria in the joint SCCM and ESICM definition of refractory septic shock,
where it reached 76.8% consensus** — so the measurement is being written into a definition at the same time
as a meta-analysis finds that resuscitating to it changes no outcome except the volume infused.
**Those two facts are compatible and the combination is worth stating plainly: a perfusion target can be a
good diagnostic marker and a poor treatment goal, and the definition work and the trial work are answering
different questions.**

**The authors' final sentence is the most useful thing in the abstract and this archive wants to credit
it.** **"Differences in intervention algorithms and comparators limit attribution of these findings to
peripheral perfusion monitoring itself."** Five trials that each wrapped capillary refill time in a different
resuscitation algorithm, against comparators that also differed, cannot isolate the monitoring. **A
meta-analysis that says so in its abstract is doing the reader's scepticism for them.**

**Two cautions on the fluid result.** **It is graded low certainty from three trials with I² of 1%**, and
this archive's standing rule is that near-zero heterogeneity across a handful of small trials is
uninformative rather than reassuring. **And a quarter of a litre over six to eight hours is a real but modest
difference — the kind of effect that matters if fluid is harmful and does not if it is not**, which is the
question the mortality nulls leave open.

**My reading.** **Not a reason to change resuscitation targets, and a good reason to keep measuring
capillary refill time.** The honest summary is that perfusion-targeted resuscitation is safe, uses slightly
less fluid, and has not been shown to change any outcome a patient would notice — with moderate certainty on
the 28-day mortality null, which is the firmest statement in the paper.

## 3. One in ten critically ill patients develops atrial fibrillation, and a third of them are anticoagulated

**[New-Onset Atrial Fibrillation in Critically Ill Patients: A Systematic Review of In-Hospital and
Post-Discharge Outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1097/CCM.0000000000007412)** · *Critical Care Medicine* ·
**published 12 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Bayesian meta-analysis of 54 studies and 367,896 patients, searched to 20 April 2026.** New-onset atrial
fibrillation defined as atrial fibrillation without prior history, arising during admission to a general
intensive care or high-dependency unit.

> Pooled incidence of NOAF in critically ill patients was 10.6% (95% credible interval [CrI], 8.6-12.9%).
> Absolute risk of post-discharge systemic thromboembolism was 7.1% (95% CrI, 4.5-11.0%)… Evidence from six
> studies reports that critically ill patients with NOAF had 1.94 times (95% CrI, 1.23-3.29) higher relative
> risk of systemic thromboembolism post-discharge than patients without NOAF, rising to 2.01 (95% CrI,
> 1.54-2.80) with adjustment. **Absolute and relative risk are evaluated with follow-up durations ranging
> from 1.5 months to 5 years, with variable anticoagulation exposure.** Anticoagulation rates were low:
> **32.9% in-hospital (95% CrI, 14.6-57.9%)** and 35.9% post-discharge (95% CrI, 19.7-55.9%). On follow-up,
> 23.9% (95% CrI, 13.9-38.5%) of critically ill patients with NOAF were subsequently diagnosed with AF.

**This is a timely companion to yesterday's guidelines watch, which found that the Society of Thoracic
Surgeons has had a fifteen-recommendation clinical practice guideline on postoperative atrial fibrillation
after cardiac surgery since 18 April 2026 — a document this archive had missed.** **So the same arrhythmia
has, in cardiac surgery, a graded guideline with two Class I recommendations; and in the general intensive
care unit, this review.** **Put the two together and the case writes itself: the general unit has a 10.6%
incidence, a doubled post-discharge thromboembolism risk, and an anticoagulation rate whose credible
interval spans fourfold — 14.6% to 57.9% — which is the signature of a practice with no guideline.**

**Three things to hold the estimates to.**

**The headline relative risk rests on six studies, not 54.** The 54 studies carry the incidence; **the
central causal-sounding claim — doubled thromboembolism risk — comes from six.** This archive's rule is to
count the studies behind the number that will be quoted.

**The adjusted estimate is both larger and more precise than the crude one**: 1.94 (1.23–3.29) becomes 2.01
(1.54–2.80), with the interval's log width falling from 0.98 to 0.60. **Adjustment usually widens an interval
or moves an estimate toward the null; doing both the other way is not impossible but it is unexplained, and
this archive has a standing note that an estimate which grows on adjustment or validation is unexplained
rather than reassuring.**

**And the absolute risks are not a fixed-horizon risk.** **Follow-up ranges from 1.5 months to five years
with variable anticoagulation**, which the authors state. A 7.1% absolute thromboembolism risk pooled across
horizons differing fortyfold is a number without a denominator in time.

**My reading.** **The incidence and the anticoagulation gap are the findings worth acting on, and they are
the two least contested numbers in the paper.** **A fourfold credible interval on whether a patient with new
atrial fibrillation in intensive care gets anticoagulated is not uncertainty about biology, it is a
description of practice** — and the 23.9% later diagnosed with atrial fibrillation says the arrhythmia is
frequently not transient. **The doubled thromboembolism risk should be read as the reason to study the
question properly rather than as an effect size to dose against.**

## 4. Does sex modify pulmonary complication risk? No — and the paper proves it the right way

**[Effect of sex on the relationship between perioperative risk factors and postoperative pulmonary
complications after noncardiothoracic surgery in patients classified ASA Physical Status
III](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03218-z)** · *Canadian Journal of Anesthesia* · **published
14 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Secondary analysis of prospective data from 1,202 patients having non-cardiothoracic surgery under general
anaesthesia, all ASA III. Primary outcome a composite of postoperative pulmonary complications by day 7.
Mixed-effects models with sex as both a main and an interaction effect.**

> Age, upper abdominal surgery, and surgical duration significantly increased the odds of ≥ 1 PPC by
> postoperative day 7. Male patients presented a higher rate of ≥ 3 PPCs, and female patients received
> higher tidal volumes per predicted body weight and driving pressures. **Sex did not significantly modify
> the relationship between perioperative factors and the primary outcome (no P < 0.05 for the interaction
> with sex).** In the secondary analysis, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) affected atelectasis more
> strongly in male than in female patients (relative odds ratio for female vs male patients, 0.27; 95%
> confidence interval, 0.13 to 0.57).

**This archive has complained about the opposite behaviour four briefs running, so it should say clearly
when a paper gets it right.** **The question was whether sex modifies risk; the test for effect modification
is the interaction term; the paper fitted the interaction, reports that none reached significance, and
concludes that perioperative factors are "predominantly not modified by sex".** **That is the correct
instrument reported in the correct direction, and the null is the contribution.**

**The incidental finding is the one that will travel, and it needs the standard caution.** **Female patients
received higher tidal volumes per predicted body weight and higher driving pressures.** That is not a
finding about biology; **it is a finding about how ventilators are set for women, and predicted body weight
is derived from height, so a systematic difference in tidal volume per predicted kilogram means the
calculation is not being applied.** **It is the most actionable sentence in the abstract and it is reported
as a baseline characteristic.**

**The secondary analysis needs the opposite caution.** **Reflux disease affected atelectasis more strongly in
men, relative odds ratio 0.27 (0.13 to 0.57), which reconstructs to p = 0.0005 and is log-symmetric.** **But
it is an interaction found in a secondary analysis after the primary interactions were all null** — one
survivor from a search across many factors and outcomes, with no multiplicity correction described in the
abstract. **The authors handle it correctly: they propose it "could be explored in future prospective
studies".** **Read as a hypothesis, it is a good one — reflux and atelectasis share a plausible mechanism
in aspiration and airway closure. Read as a result, it is a subgroup finding in a secondary analysis of a
secondary analysis.**

**And a timeliness note: this paper lands four days after PrECiSIOn, the international Delphi consensus that
standardised what counts as a postoperative pulmonary complication and ranked the components by severity.**
**This study's primary outcome is a composite of PPCs by day 7, and its most interesting secondary signal is
on one component, atelectasis.** **A paper published next year with the same data and the PrECiSIOn
construct would report a severity-ranked ordinal outcome and might find something different** — which is
precisely the comparability problem PrECiSIOn exists to fix.

## 5. Fifty-two postponed operations for six interventions

**[Impact of preoperative cardiology consultations on perioperative management in patients undergoing
non-cardiac surgery](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accpm.2026.101990)** · *Anaesthesia Critical Care & Pain
Medicine* · **published 14 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Prospective, observational, single-centre study at Angers University Hospital, 1 September 2024 to
31 August 2025. All patients referred for cardiology assessment after their anaesthetic consultation.**

> Among 9,621 preoperative anesthetic consultations, 138 (1%) patients were referred for cardiology
> assessment by the anesthesiologist and included in the study. 59 (43%) patients experienced a change in
> perioperative management. Of them, **surgery was postponed in 52 (38%) patients, with a median delay of 81
> [47-145] days, whereas only 6 patients underwent an invasive cardiac intervention** (angioplasty,
> valvuloplasty, or electrical cardioversion). A previously unknown cardiac condition was identified in 19
> (15%) patients. 111 (80%) cardiology referrals were consistent with current recommendations.

**The ratio is the finding and the paper does not state it: 52 postponements for 6 invasive interventions,
which is 8.7 operations delayed for every one that led to a cardiac procedure, with a median delay of
81 days and an interquartile range running to 145.**

**That number can be read two ways and the honest position is that this study cannot decide between them.**
**Either the postponements were mostly appropriate — time used for medical optimisation, which the paper
counts separately as "specific preoperative cardiac treatment" — or the referral pathway converts diagnostic
uncertainty into delay.** **A design that observes referrals without outcomes cannot distinguish a prudent
delay from an unnecessary one**, and the abstract reports no outcome of the postponed operations at all.
**What it does establish is the scale: three months is the median wait a referral costs.**

**Two numbers reconstruct with a wrinkle worth noting.** **138 of 9,621 is 1.43%, reported as 1%**, which is
rounding to one figure. **And 19 of 138 is 13.8%, reported as 15%** — a 19 of 128 denominator would give
14.8%, so the 15% appears to use a smaller denominator than the 138 referrals, and the abstract does not say
which. **111 of 138 is 80.4%, matching the reported 80% exactly.**

**The most reassuring result is the one stated last: 80% of the anaesthetists' referrals were consistent with
current recommendations.** **A fifth were not, and 15% of referrals found a previously unknown cardiac
condition** — so the pathway is finding real disease at a rate that justifies its existence while delaying
nine operations for every intervention it produces.

**My reading.** **A useful audit of a pathway that everyone uses and nobody measures, from a single centre
and one year.** **The practical value is the median 81-day delay, which is a number to quote to a surgeon
deciding whether a referral is worth it** — and the gap this study leaves is the one that matters: nobody
here knows what happened to the 52 patients whose operations were postponed.
