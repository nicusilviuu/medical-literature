**The rule now reads: the subtraction bound requires a non-negative decomposition. Check the decomposition
before using it.**

## 1. SAVE-ICU: isoflurane changes nothing it was meant to and two things it was not

**[Isoflurane-Based Inhaled Sedation in Mechanically Ventilated Adults With Acute Hypoxemic Respiratory
Failure: The SAVE-ICU Randomized Clinical Trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.18471)** · *JAMA* ·
**published 14 October 2026 — recovered by last week's overdue lagged pass, never reported here before** ·
paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Pragmatic, open-label, superiority trial in 14 Canadian intensive care units, September 2020 to March
2025. Adults ventilated for acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, sedated for under 72 hours, randomised to
an inhaled isoflurane strategy with intravenous adjuncts as needed or to usual intravenous sedation.**
786 randomised, 780 analysed; mean age 61.1, 34.0% female, 73.7% non-COVID, mean PaO₂:FiO₂ 152.6 mmHg.

> At hospital discharge, 161 of 379 patients (42.5%) in the inhaled sedation group had died, compared with
> 171 of 390 patients (43.8%) in the intravenous sedation group (risk ratio, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.84 to 1.14];
> P = .85). … The inhaled sedation group received more adjunctive therapies for hypoxemia (239 [63.9%] vs 214
> [55.0%]; between-group difference, 8.89% [95% CI, 1.95% to 15.83%]) and had fewer delirium- and coma-free
> days (between-group difference, -0.95 [95% CI, -1.69 to -0.22]).

**The mortality result is clean and the trial is large enough to mean it: 42.5% against 43.8% in 769
patients, with an interval excluding more than a 16% relative benefit.**

**Three things the abstract contains that a summary would lose.**

**First, about half the intervention arm also received the comparator.** **"In the inhaled sedation group,
49.9% to 55.4% of patients received concomitant intravenous sedation on any day during the first 14 days."**
So this is a strategy trial and the honest statement of what was compared is **isoflurane-plus-intravenous
against intravenous** — which makes the null easier to believe and makes any claim about isoflurane itself
weaker than the headline suggests.

**Second, the denominators are not the arm sizes.** **The arms are 385 and 395, totalling 780; the mortality
denominators are 379 and 390, totalling 769.** Eleven patients are in the analysis population and not in the
primary outcome. The abstract does not say why. **This archive flagged the same shape in COVALENT on
16 October, where complication denominators were not arm sizes — so it is a recurring reporting habit rather
than a one-off**, and eleven of 780 cannot change this result.

**Third, and this is the week's thread: the trial's two significant findings are both harms, and one of them
is measured in delirium- and coma-free days.** **0.95 fewer such days with inhaled sedation (−1.69 to
−0.22).** **This is the third appearance of that endpoint in this archive in one week** — after the
*Critical Care Medicine* methods paper stating that these composites "have not been rigorously evaluated"
and the guanfacine pilot whose entire clinical signal rested on them. **Here a 780-patient trial in a major
journal is reporting a harm on an endpoint a methods paper in the same month says nobody has validated.**
**That is not a reason to dismiss the harm; it is a reason to notice that the field's best trials and its
own methodologists are out of step.**

**On the technical side: the reported P of .85 reconstructs from the interval to 0.80**, and the crude risk
ratio from the quoted counts is 0.969 against a reported 0.98 — both differences are consistent with
rounding to two decimals and an adjusted analysis, and both are far from mattering. **The adjunctive-therapy
difference is exactly symmetric (6.94 points each side), so that one is a plain Wald interval.**

**My reading.** **Inhaled isoflurane does not reduce mortality in hypoxaemic respiratory failure, and the
signal that it costs delirium- and coma-free days and provokes more rescue therapy for hypoxaemia deserves
more attention than the null.** **A sedation strategy that needs the comparator in half its patients, and
that is followed by more adjunctive therapy for the very problem the patients were admitted with, has not
earned a place in routine practice.**

## 2. Dobutamine and kidney injury: a real effect size on an endpoint measured three times

**[Intraoperative Dobutamine Plus Norepinephrine to Prevent Postoperative Acute Kidney Injury in Septic
Shock](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2026.21322)** · *JAMA* · **published 14 October 2026 — also recovered by
the lagged pass** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Randomised, open-label, 39 tertiary hospitals in China, June 2024 to August 2025. 584 adults with septic
shock undergoing surgical source control, randomised to intraoperative dobutamine up to 5 µg/kg/min plus
noradrenaline, or noradrenaline alone.** Median age 67.5, 32.9% female.

> …new-onset or worsening AKI within 7 postoperative days occurred in 22.8% (66/290) randomized to receive
> dobutamine plus norepinephrine vs 34.7% (102/294) randomized to receive norepinephrine alone (adjusted
> risk ratio [ARR], 0.64 [95% CI, 0.49-0.83]). **No significant differences were observed in 9 of the 11
> secondary outcomes.** SOFA scores on postoperative day 1 (adjusted difference, -0.53 [95% CI, -0.98 to
> -0.07]) were lower and urine output 1 hour post intervention (adjusted difference, 0.55 [95% CI, 0.03 to
> 1.07] mL/kg/h) was higher … Stage 2 and 3 AKI were lower … (stage 2: 4.8% vs 9.9%; ARR, 0.48 [0.26-0.88];
> stage 3: 7.9% vs 13.6%; ARR, 0.56 [0.35-0.91]). Tachycardia (heart rate >100/min) occurred more frequently
> …

**The effect size is large and legible: an absolute reduction of 11.94 percentage points, so a number needed
to treat of 8.4.** The counts reconstruct exactly (66 of 290 is 22.76%, 102 of 294 is 34.69%, crude risk
ratio 0.656 against an adjusted 0.64), every interval is log-symmetric, and the primary reconstructs to
p = 0.0009. **The severity gradient is there too — stage 2 halved and stage 3 down by 44% — which is what a
real renal effect looks like rather than a shift across a threshold.**

**Now the problem, and it is the archive's standing rule in its purest form.** **The primary endpoint is
KDIGO acute kidney injury within seven days — a creatinine and urine-output definition. The two secondary
outcomes that moved are the day-1 SOFA score, which contains a renal sub-score built from creatinine and
urine output, and urine output itself at one hour.** **So the two "supporting" results are not independent
corroboration; they are the primary endpoint measured again, once at one hour and once folded into a
composite score.** **Nine of eleven secondaries were null, and the two that were not are the two closest to
the primary.**

**And the mechanism makes that circularity predictable rather than suspicious.** Dobutamine is an inotrope:
it raises cardiac output and renal perfusion, which raises urine output and lowers creatinine, **which is
the definition of the endpoint.** **This archive's rule — ask whether the drug can move the endpoint without
touching the organ — applies, and here the honest answer is that improving renal perfusion is exactly how
dobutamine would produce this result whether or not any kidney was spared.** **Across 33 cardiac-surgery
kidney trials over 22 years, short-term creatinine endpoints produced the positives and MAKE-90 produced the
negatives, and no agent benefited both.** **No mortality or dialysis-at-90-days outcome appears anywhere in
this abstract.**

**Two further cautions.** **Open-label**, with an intervention whose effect on heart rate is visible to
everyone in the room — and tachycardia was more frequent, which is also the drug announcing itself. **And
the trial is a single-country trial of a drug the guidelines already recommend on very low-certainty
evidence**, which the authors say in their first sentence.

**My reading.** **The most interesting trial of the three and the one most likely to be over-read.** An
11.9-point reduction in KDIGO-defined kidney injury with a number needed to treat of eight is worth having,
and it is not yet evidence that fewer patients end up on dialysis or alive. **The next trial needs a 90-day
kidney endpoint, and until it exists this result should change how we read creatinine, not necessarily how
we treat.**

## 3. The vasopressin window, tested exactly where it was claimed

**[Timing of Vasopressin Initiation in Sepsis Patients on Norepinephrine: A Target Trial
Emulation](https://doi.org/10.1177/08850666261479700)** · *Journal of Intensive Care Medicine* ·
**published 7 October 2026 — recovered by the lagged pass; read here against VASSPR, published a week
later** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**5,461 patients with septic shock from MIMIC-IV, clone-censor-weight target trial emulation with inverse
probability of censoring weighting for immortal time bias, piecewise landmark Cox models stratified by
baseline noradrenaline dose.**

> The absolute risk reduction in the broad >0.1 μg/kg/min cohort was not statistically significant, but a
> survival benefit emerged in the late phase for the >0.25 μg/kg/min subgroup (late-phase HR 0.82, 95% CI
> 0.69-0.97, P=.018). In contrast, for patients with more severe shock (NE >0.5 μg/kg/min), early initiation
> was associated with increased risk in the first 48 h. This early hazard was most pronounced in the >0.75
> μg/kg/min subgroup … (HR 2.87, 95% CI 1.68-4.90, P<.001). … The findings suggest a potential therapeutic
> window at norepinephrine equivalent doses between 0.1 and 0.5 μg/kg/min, with the most consistent benefit
> around 0.25 μg/kg/min.

**Both intervals are log-symmetric and both reconstruct: the late benefit to p = 0.022 against a stated .018,
the early hazard to p = 0.0001.** **The paper is methodologically careful** — it names immortal time bias
and uses the right instrument against it, and it concedes in its own conclusion that the early hazard "may
reflect residual confounding by shock severity rather than a direct causal harm."

**Read it against VASSPR, which was published seven days later, and the pairing is the point.** **The
emulation proposes a therapeutic window between 0.1 and 0.5 µg/kg/min. VASSPR randomised 13 hospitals to
start vasopressin at 0.1 or at 0.4 — both thresholds inside that window — and found 28-day mortality of
31.8% against 33.0%, adjusted odds ratio 0.98, with practice separated by 25 percentage points.**
**So the window hypothesis was tested, prospectively and at scale, precisely across the range where it was
claimed to operate, and it did not survive.**

**That is a more useful comparison than either paper alone, and it is a general lesson about this
instrument.** **A target trial emulation can control immortal time bias impeccably and still be reporting
the shape of confounding rather than the shape of a treatment effect** — which the emulation's own authors
say about their 2.87, and which the randomised trial then demonstrates about the protective half as well.
**The archive's original observational hazard ratio of 2.55 for vasopressin, the emulation's 2.87 at high
dose, and VASSPR's 0.98 now form a complete series: two observational estimates pointing at severity, one
randomised estimate pointing at nothing.**

**One limit worth naming: the emulation's late-phase benefit at >0.25 is a subgroup of a stratified analysis
across four dose thresholds, and a difference in significance between strata is not a significant
difference** — no interaction test is reported in the abstract.

## 4. A mortality benefit from a ventilator mode, and an upper bound of 0.99

**[Effects of neurally adjusted ventilatory assist on mortality in critically ill patients: A systematic
review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/03000605261506599)** · *Journal of International Medical
Research* · **published 13 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Nine randomised trials, 842 patients — 389 on neurally adjusted ventilatory assist and 453 on conventional
modes, which sums exactly.** Searches to 18 July 2026.

> In patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation … neurally adjusted ventilatory assist reduced
> intensive care unit mortality (risk ratio = 0.72, 95% confidence interval: 0.53-0.99) and hospital
> mortality (risk ratio = 0.71, 95% confidence interval: 0.56-0.90) and increased the mechanical ventilation
> success rate (risk ratio = 1.13, 95% confidence interval: 1.04-1.22) and the number of 28-day
> ventilator-free days (mean difference = 3.48, 95% confidence interval: 0.97-6.00). In patients receiving
> noninvasive ventilation, neurally adjusted ventilatory assist did not significantly reduce hospital
> mortality (p = 0.23) or increase the mechanical ventilation success rate (p = 0.66) …

**Treat this with the scepticism the archive reserves for mortality benefits from small-trial pools.**
**Nine trials and 842 patients is roughly 94 patients per trial; the intensive care mortality interval runs
to 0.99 and reconstructs to p = 0.039 — the least informative significant result available.** The hospital
mortality estimate is firmer (p = 0.005) and all four intervals are essentially log-symmetric. **But a
ventilator mode reducing mortality by 28% in 842 patients, when far larger trials of ventilation strategy
have struggled to move mortality at all, is the kind of result that shrinks as evidence accumulates.**

**What makes it worth reporting is the structure rather than the size.** **The benefit appears in invasive
ventilation and vanishes in non-invasive, and the authors say so instead of pooling the two** — and the
non-invasive nulls come with I² of 0%, so the absence is not heterogeneity. **A mode that synchronises
ventilator support to diaphragmatic electrical activity ought to matter more when the patient is
instrumented and sedated than when they are wearing a mask, so the direction of the subgroup difference is
at least mechanistically coherent.**

**And it lands on a live thread.** **Yesterday's guidelines watch recorded that ESICM is writing a weaning
guideline jointly with the European Respiratory Society.** **This meta-analysis offers that committee a
"mechanical ventilation success rate" of 1.13 (1.04–1.22) — the same weaning-success family of endpoints
that a proportional assist trial moved while leaving reintubation and mortality untouched.** **The committee
will have to decide whether weaning success counts as a benefit on its own, and this paper is exactly the
evidence that forces the question.**

**My reading.** **Not practice-changing, and useful as a hypothesis with a number attached.** The honest
summary is that nine small trials favour neurally adjusted ventilatory assist during invasive ventilation on
every endpoint they measured, that the mortality claim rests on an interval touching 1.0, and that the
non-invasive evidence is too thin to interpret — which the authors state plainly.

## 5. Perineural versus intravenous dexamethasone in 60 children: the primary says no, the secondary says 42%

**[Does Perineural Dexamethasone Prolong the Time to First Rescue Opioid Analgesia Compared With Intravenous
Dexamethasone in Pediatric Hip Surgery?](https://doi.org/10.1097/CORR.0000000000004182)** · *Clinical
Orthopaedics and Related Research* · **published 13 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Randomised, blinded trial at one tertiary centre in Poznań, Poland, February 2025 to March 2026. 60
children aged 3 months to 6 years having reconstructive hip surgery for developmental dysplasia or
neurogenic hip displacement, all with a pericapsular nerve group block and standardised spinal anaesthesia
and multimodal analgesia.** Parents, anaesthetists, surgeons and outcome assessors all blinded; **no losses
and no incomplete data over 48 hours.**

> We found no difference between the perineural and intravenous dexamethasone groups in time to first rescue
> opioid analgesia after adjustment for age, body weight, and duration of surgery (HR 0.56 [95% CI 0.28 to
> 1.08]; p = 0.08). Perineural dexamethasone reduced total opioid consumption during the first 48
> postoperative hours (mean ± SD 0.131 ± 0.048 versus 0.225 ± 0.053 mg/kg morphine milligram equivalents
> (MMEs), mean difference -0.095 mg/kg [95% CI -0.121 to -0.068]; p < 0.001) … blood glucose levels were
> lower in the perineural dexamethasone group at 12 hours (112 ± 17 versus 139 ± 20 mg/dL; p < 0.001) and 24
> hours … No neurologic complications occurred in either group …

**Everything checks: 0.131 against 0.225 is a 41.8% reduction against a stated "approximately 42%", the mean
difference computes to −0.094 against a stated −0.095, and 0.095 mg/kg for a 20-kg child is 1.90 mg morphine
equivalents, exactly as the authors say.** **The hazard ratio reconstructs to p = 0.092 against a stated
0.08, a rounding-level difference in the direction that identifies nothing worse than two-decimal bounds.**

**The shape of this trial is the lesson.** **Its primary endpoint is null and its secondary endpoint is
overwhelming — and the secondary is the one that will be quoted.** **A 42% relative reduction in opioid
consumption at p < 0.001 is a striking sentence; "no difference in time to first rescue analgesia" is the
trial's actual answer to its actual question.** **The two are reconcilable: if children in both arms first
needed rescue at about the same time but the perineural group then needed less, the block's benefit is in
the depth of analgesia rather than its onset or duration.** **But a trial of 60 children is powered for
neither conclusion, and the honest reading is that the primary was negative and the mechanism of the
secondary is unexplained.**

**The absolute size deserves saying out loud.** **1.90 mg of morphine equivalent, for a 20-kg child, over
48 hours.** **The relative reduction is 42% and the absolute difference is a fraction of one dose of
nalbuphine.** This archive's standing rule about significance and importance cuts both ways: **a p below
0.001 on a difference this small is a statement about measurement precision in a trial with no missing data,
not about how the child felt.**

**The glucose finding is the most mechanistically interesting result and it is not what it will be used
for.** **Blood glucose was 27 mg/dL lower at 12 hours in the perineural group.** Dexamethasone raises
glucose systemically; a lower systemic glucose after perineural administration is consistent with less of
the drug reaching the circulation — **which is evidence that the perineural dose acts locally, and therefore
evidence against the common claim that perineural and intravenous dexamethasone are interchangeable because
both work systemically.** **In a population including children with cerebral palsy, a smaller glucose
excursion is also a small clinical good in itself.**

**My reading.** **A well-conducted small trial whose primary endpoint is negative, reported honestly as
negative in its own conclusion, and whose two secondary findings — less opioid and less hyperglycaemia —
point the same way and need a multicentre trial, which the authors ask for.** **Level I therapeutic study at
n = 60 is the designation doing the most work here, and it should not be mistaken for a definitive answer.**
