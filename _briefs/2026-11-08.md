## 1. A balloon pump started in intensive care, and an adjustment that undoes the finding

**[Timing of intra-aortic balloon pump support and early mortality after isolated coronary artery bypass
grafting in patients with preserved ejection fraction](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10984219/v1)** ·
**preprint, Research Square** · **posted 1 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**1,224 consecutive adults having elective or urgent isolated on-pump bypass grafting with an ejection
fraction of at least 50%, July 2024 to December 2025. 57 — 4.7% — received a balloon pump.** The comparison
is **support already running as the patient came off bypass (OR-IABP, n = 25) against rescue support started
later in intensive care (ICU-IABP, n = 28).** From the abstract:

> Operative mortality was 49.1% with IABP and 2.3% without (p < 0.001), and rose across the preoperative,
> intraoperative and postoperative groups (18.8%, 38.5% and 71.4%; p = 0.002). It was 32.0% for OR-IABP
> (n = 25) and 71.4% for ICU-IABP (n = 28; p = 0.004); 30-day mortality was 24.0% and 67.9% (p = 0.001).
> Urgent surgery was more common in the OR-IABP group (76.0% versus 50.0%; p = 0.051). Crude odds of death
> were higher with ICU-IABP (odds ratio 5.31, 95% CI 1.64–17.19; p = 0.005); after adjustment for age, sex,
> renal function and urgency the estimate was 4.54 (0.84–24.46; p = 0.078).

**The first number is the one to sit with. Needing a balloon pump after elective or urgent bypass grafting in
a patient with a normal ejection fraction carried 49.1% operative mortality against 2.3% without** — about
28 deaths among 57 patients against 27 among the other 1,167. **A twenty-one-fold difference in mortality
marks these 57 patients as a different population, not a treated subgroup.**

**Then the adjustment, which is where this paper earns its place.** **The crude odds ratio of 5.31 has an
interval from 1.64 to 17.19, a 10.5-fold span; after adjustment for four variables it becomes 4.54 with an
interval from 0.84 to 24.46, a 29.1-fold span that crosses one.** **The point estimate barely moved and the
precision collapsed** — which is what adjusting for four covariates on 53 patients and 28 deaths does.
**And the authors say so in the clearest sentence any preprint in this archive has produced:**

> These findings raise a hypothesis; they do not show that earlier support saves lives.

**The confounding runs visibly against them and they report that too.** **Urgent surgery was more common in
the group that did better** — 76.0% against 50.0% — **so the arm with the better outcome was the more
acutely ill one, and the paper notes it.** **A patient who needs rescue support in intensive care at hour six
is not the same patient as one supported prophylactically in theatre, and nothing in this design can make
them comparable.**

**One arithmetic gap worth naming.** **25 plus 28 is 53, and 57 patients received a pump, so four are in
neither timing group** and the abstract does not say where they went. **The three-way split by timing
(18.8%, 38.5%, 71.4%) is a different partition from the two-way OR-versus-ICU split**, and a reader could
easily take them for the same thing.

**My reading.** **The clinical signal that survives is prognostic, not therapeutic: a balloon pump after
bypass grafting in a preserved-function patient is a marker of catastrophe, and a pump started in intensive
care is a marker of a worse one.** **Whether earlier support would change that is the question the paper
poses and explicitly declines to answer**, and this archive's standing rule — count the events before
crediting the size — gives 28 deaths across two arms of 25 and 28. **It is a hypothesis, correctly labelled.**

## 2. Four strategies, 51 deaths, nested arms, and a multiplicity correction that does its job

**[Timing of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker restart after cardiac
surgery and postoperative outcomes: a target trial emulation](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11548906/v1)**
· **preprint, Research Square** · **posted 1 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**10,939 open cardiac operations in MIMIC-IV; 5,496 patients — 50.2% — were on renin-angiotensin blockade
before surgery; 1,420 — 25.8% of those — restarted before discharge, at a median of 53.9 hours.** Four
strategies were compared: restart within 24, 48 or 72 hours, or from 72 hours to discharge. From the abstract:

> The four strategies contained 259, 664, 872 and 548 patients, respectively. There were 51 deaths within
> 1 year, 24 within 90 days and 12 within 30 days. … Compared with restart within 48 h, restart within 24 h
> had higher 1-year mortality (RD +2.14 percentage points, 95%CI 0.05～4.27; relative risk 1.78, 1.02～2.67;
> hazard ratio 1.70, 1.01～2.55; E-value 2.95), but this comparison was no longer significant after correction
> for 6 comparisons within the mortality family (q = 0.23).

**Two structural facts have to be stated before any result.** **First, 259 plus 664 plus 872 plus 548 is
2,343, and only 1,420 patients restarted — so the four arms are nested, not disjoint.** "Within 48 hours"
contains "within 24 hours". **A reader who treats them as four groups will double-count most of the cohort**,
and the abstract does not say they overlap. **Second, the whole analysis rests on 51 deaths at one year, 24 at
90 days and 12 at 30 days** — 3.59%, 1.69% and 0.85% of 1,420. **Twelve deaths spread across four nested
strategies cannot support a 30-day comparison at all**, and the paper's own result agrees: at 30 and 90 days
every pairwise interval included zero.

**The multiplicity handling is the reason this is worth publishing, and it is exemplary.** **The one
significant finding — restarting within 24 hours carried 2.14 percentage points more one-year mortality than
within 48 hours, with a relative risk of 1.78 and a hazard ratio of 1.70 — did not survive correction across
the six comparisons in its own family, reaching q = 0.23.** **The authors report the corrected value and draw
the negative conclusion.** And they go further: **of the 15 comparisons whose uncorrected intervals excluded
zero, 8 reached q below 0.05, 3 landed at 0.054 to 0.060, and 4 at 0.09 to 0.23** — the three buckets sum to
15. **Publishing the distribution of corrected q-values, including the near-misses, is better practice than
this archive sees in most peer-reviewed work.**

**One note on the surviving complication signals**, which the abstract reports as directions without
intervals: reintubation lower with restart within 24 hours; hyperkalaemia and liver injury highest at 72 hours
and later; infection, pulmonary and cardiovascular complications higher within 48 hours than at 72 hours and
later. **Those are not consistent with a single monotonic story, and at these event counts they should be read
as the texture of a null rather than as a dose-response.**

**My reading.** **The paper's conclusion — that restart timing did not differ for 30-day or 90-day mortality,
and the one-year association was not robust to correction — is the right conclusion and is itself useful.**
Clinicians restarting renin-angiotensin blockade after cardiac surgery have no evidence that the hour matters,
and now have a well-constructed emulation saying so. **The E-value of 2.95 attached to the discarded result is
a curiosity worth noting: it was the most robust-to-confounding finding in the paper and still did not survive
multiplicity, which is a reminder that the two corrections answer different questions.**

## 3. What a week of calibration findings was converging on, settled in three systems

**[Transportability, dataset shift, and local recalibration of early ICU deterioration prediction across
MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and NWICU](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11054375/v1)** · **preprint, Research
Square** · **posted 1 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**Penalised logistic regression and XGBoost developed in 31,054 MIMIC-IV stays on 17 predictors from the
first 24 hours, then applied without adaptation to 90,393 eICU-CRD and 16,562 NWICU stays.** Primary outcome
ICU death or new invasive ventilation during hours 24 to 48; event prevalences **3.45%, 2.42% and 2.75%**.
From the abstract:

> External discrimination did not collapse uniformly (AUROC 0.674–0.728), but calibration failures differed by
> environment and model. In eICU, calibration slopes were 0.893 for logistic regression and 1.003 for XGBoost;
> in NWICU, they were 1.234 and 1.054. Intercept-only recalibration was often effective for risk-level shift,
> whereas slope updating became more useful with increasing local event information when slope mismatch was
> present. … Transport failure was driven more consistently by environment-specific miscalibration than by
> loss of ranking performance.

**That last sentence is the one this archive has been assembling for a week, and here it is tested in three
systems rather than inferred from one.** **On 1 November a perioperative infection model discriminated at
0.876 and overpredicted the odds of infection 2.8-fold, fixed almost entirely by recalibrating the
intercept.** **On 5 November an atrial fibrillation model developed on a case-enriched sample predicted a mean
risk of 0.442 against an observed 0.263, and intercept-only recalibration cut its calibration index by 72%.**
**Today's paper states the general case and provides the discipline the other two could not: the failure mode
is environment-specific miscalibration, and ranking survives.**

**The detail that makes it more than a slogan is that the failure mode differed by environment and by
model.** **In eICU the logistic slope was 0.893 and XGBoost's 1.003; in NWICU they were 1.234 and 1.054.**
**So the same model miscalibrated in opposite directions in two different systems, and the two models
miscalibrated differently in the same system.** **There is no single correction to ship with a model.** The
authors' recalibration experiments follow from that: **intercept-only updating handles a shift in risk level,
slope updating is needed when the slope is wrong, and which one you need depends on where you are deploying.**

**And the paper closes a question this archive raised on 1 November without answering.** **"The results do not
support a universal event-count threshold."** The 1 November audit found a six-event stratum that was not
estimable and left open how many events local recalibration needs. **Today's answer is that the number depends
on the model, the environment, the failure mode and the outcome horizon** — and the horizon effect is
quantified: **the 24-to-72-hour composite needed 31.5% to 32.6% fewer labelled patients in eICU and 33.9% to
35.2% fewer in NWICU than the 24-to-48-hour outcome, at matched event targets.** **Widening the outcome window
is a cheaper route to a recalibrated model than collecting more patients.**

**My reading.** **This is the most useful methodological paper this archive has published in two weeks and it
is a preprint, which is where this literature now lives.** **The transferable instruction is specific: when a
published model arrives at your unit, do not ask whether its area under the curve holds — assume it does, and
measure its calibration slope and intercept locally before anybody reads a predicted risk to a patient.**
**Discrimination is a property of the model; calibration is a property of the model in your hospital.**

## 4. A shock index that separates a phenotype cleanly, and finds no treatment to attach to it

**[Vasoactive-Adjusted Shock Index for Risk Stratification in Adult
Sepsis](https://doi.org/10.1097/shk.0000000000002958)** · *Shock* · **published 2 November 2026** ·
paywalled; abstract quoted below

**63,853 patients.** Group-based trajectory modelling on the vasoactive-adjusted shock index — a measure
validated in paediatric sepsis as marking refractory shock — with Cox regression across trajectory groups and
a covariate-balancing propensity-score analysis of treatment-effect heterogeneity. From the abstract:

> The VASI predicted ICU mortality significantly better than the shock index and provided incremental value
> beyond SOFA. Two distinct VASI trajectories were identified, with low VASI trajectory that remained
> consistently low and high VASI trajectory characterized by an early transient rise followed by a sustained
> decline. The high VASI group had markedly higher 28-day mortality (HR 2.89, 95% CI 2.72-3.08) and 90-day
> mortality (HR 2.85, 95% CI 2.68-3.03). Hydrocortisone treatment was associated with mortality in both
> groups, with no significant interaction. Albumin showed a protective effect only in the low VASI group
> (OR 0.667, 95% CI 0.545-0.816), but the interaction was not significant.

**The prognostic result is as clean as this literature gets.** **Hazard ratios of 2.89 and 2.85 with intervals
only 1.13-fold wide, in 63,853 patients** — and, unusually for a new index, **the paper reports incremental
value beyond SOFA rather than leaving a reader to wonder.** **That is the question this archive put to the two
endothelial-index preprints on 5 November and neither answered.** **Asking it and answering it is the
difference between an index and a publication.**

**The negative result is the more important one.** **Hydrocortisone was associated with mortality in both
trajectory groups with no significant interaction; albumin looked protective only in the low-index group, and
that interaction was not significant either.** **So the index identifies who will do badly and does not
identify who should be treated differently** — and the authors state it in their conclusion rather than
burying it.

**This is the fourth consecutive brief in which a paper constructs a subgroup story and then refuses it**,
after the organ-failure score revision and the fluid emulation on 3 November and the TTM heterogeneity
analysis on 5 November. **In all four the refusal is the finding**, and it is worth noting what the four have
in common: **every one of them ran a formal interaction test on a pattern that looked convincing by eye.**

**Two cautions.** **The albumin result is exactly the shape that gets over-read**: significant in one
stratum at an odds ratio of 0.667, absent in the other, interaction non-significant. **That is a difference in
significance, not a significant difference** — the rule recorded here on 3 November. **And a trajectory
"characterised by an early transient rise followed by a sustained decline" in the high-risk group is
describing survivors**; a patient who dies on day two contributes no declining tail, so the shape of the
trajectory is partly drawn by who stayed alive to be measured.

## 5. A 677-patient null and a 50-patient signal

**[Cell salvage volume and hemostatic changes in cardiac surgery](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2026.112362)**
· *Journal of Clinical Anesthesia* · **published 31 October 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Two cohorts of patients having cardiac surgery with intraoperative cell salvage: a routine coagulation
cohort of 677 (the primary analysis) and a viscoelastic cohort of 50 (exploratory).** From the abstract:

> In the routine coagulation cohort (N = 677; median CS 0.5 L, range 0.1-1.6 L), CS volume was not
> significantly associated with absolute changes in any parameter (all p ≥ 0.144). In the viscoelastic cohort
> (N = 50; median CS 0.8 L, range 0.2-3.7 L), CS volume was significantly associated with decreased functional
> fibrinogen clot strength (CFF MA: β = -3.31 mm/L; 95% CI: -5.53, -1.08; p = 0.004) and overall clot strength
> (CK MA: β = -2.93 mm/L; 95% CI: -5.73, -0.13; p = 0.041); after Holm correction for multiple testing, the
> CFF MA association remained significant (adjusted p = 0.031), whereas the CK MA association did not
> (adjusted p = 0.244).

**The structure is the lesson. The 677-patient cohort found nothing — every p-value at or above 0.144 — and
the 50-patient cohort found two things, one of which survived correction.** **A reader who meets this paper
through its conclusion will remember the fibrinogen signal and not the 13.5-fold larger null.**

**But the two cohorts are not asking the same question, and that rescues the paper from the obvious
criticism.** **The large cohort measured routine coagulation tests; the small one measured viscoelastic clot
strength** — and **the small cohort also had higher salvage volumes, a median of 0.8 L against 0.5 L and a
range reaching 3.7 L against 1.6 L.** **So the exploratory finding sits where the exposure was larger and the
assay was more sensitive**, which is a coherent reason for a signal to appear in the smaller sample rather
than a reason to distrust it. **The Holm correction is applied and reported honestly, including the
association that did not survive it.**

**My reading.** **The practical conclusion the authors draw is the right size for the evidence: viscoelastic
monitoring has potential value when higher salvage volumes are anticipated.** **Not "cell salvage impairs
haemostasis" — at the volumes most patients receive, 677 patients say it does not.** **This pairs directly
with the Brazilian bloodless-surgery guideline published in this archive's
[1 November guidelines watch](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-11-01/)**, whose postoperative part opened
by naming judicious laboratory testing as a cause of iatrogenic anaemia. **Both documents are about the hidden
costs of blood-conservation machinery, and both find them smaller than feared and worth measuring rather than
assuming.**
