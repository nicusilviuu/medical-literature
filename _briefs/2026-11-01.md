and 0.** Thirteen of the thirty-one days sit below 3,000.

**And the four Sundays read 2,503, 185, 0 and 11,175.**

**That is the finding, and it is a correction to this archive's own method.** The standing instruction, written
here two days ago, was *read buckets daily as a measurement*. **Today's scan shows what the measurement
measures.** A first-index date is not a timestamp of arrival that is written once; it is **restamped by bulk
reloads**, which is why a quiet day can hold one record and the day of a reload can hold sixty-three thousand.
**A daily bucket therefore measures the most recent restamping of whatever is in it, not the volume a source
delivered that day.**

**Three consequences, and I am stating all three against my own earlier text.**

**First, the three Sunday observations recorded this week — 18 October zero, 25 October 10,915, 1 November
zero — are not three observations of Sunday.** 18 October holds zero records **of every source**, which no
account of weekend publishing can produce; and 25 October now reads 11,175 where it read 10,915 when measured.
**Those three numbers were never a comparison, and retiring "Sunday is quiet" as a rule was the right act for
the wrong reason.**

**Second, today's "MEDLINE delivered zero in twenty-four hours" is a statement about MEDLINE's total, which is
sound, and about today's bucket, which is not informative.** The total is unchanged to the record: that is a
real fact about ingestion. **The empty bucket is not independent evidence for it.**

**Third, the eighth outcome of the week's bucket observable arrives today, and it is +1.** The 31 October
bucket went 7,269 to 7,270 — **MEDLINE 4,837, PMC 1,527, preprints 904, clinical trials 1 all unchanged, and

now reads **+4,555, +6, +121, +16,506, −15, 0, +41, +1**. **It was already closed as uninformative; today it

**One more thing the scan explains.** The sizing pass for today's sweep counted 101 hits for a fixed query over

## 1. Weaning success improves; reintubation and mortality do not

**[Impact of Proportional Assist Ventilation Versus Pressure Support Ventilation on Adult Liberation From
Mechanical Ventilation: A Revised Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of RCTs](https://doi.org/10.1155/ccrp/7536067)**
· *Critical Care Research and Practice* · **published 29 October 2026** · open access

**Eight randomised trials, 1,057 patients**, comparing proportional assist ventilation with pressure support
ventilation in mechanically ventilated adults; PubMed, Embase and Cochrane searched for trials published
before October 2025. The abstract, quoted on the results:

> **PAV was associated with a higher rate of weaning success as compared to PSV (RR: 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01-1.18;
> p = 0.03; I2 = 23%). PAV was associated with a lesser duration of ventilation (MD: -1.06; 95% CI, -1.82 to
> -0.29, p = 0.006; I2 = 0%) and intensive care unit (ICU) stay (MD: -1.49; 95% CI: -2.43 to -0.54, p = 0.002;
> I2 = 25%) as compared to PSV. However, no statistically significant benefit was observed for PAV over PSV
> with regard to rate of reintubation (RR: 0.82; 95%, CI: 0.58-1.16, p = 0.21, I2 = 0%) and mortality
> (RR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.63-1.25 p = 0.4 I2 = 30%).**

**The three positive results reconstruct exactly.** Weaning success: the interval implies a standard error of
0.0397 on the log scale and a z of 2.17, for p = 0.0299 against the stated 0.03. Ventilation duration: z of
−2.72, p = 0.0066 against 0.006. Intensive care stay: z of −3.09, p = 0.0020 against 0.002. **All three
intervals are symmetric on their own scale.**

**The two null results do not.** Reintubation at 0.82 (0.58–1.16) has a log-symmetric interval implying z of
−1.12 and **p = 0.26, where 0.21 is reported**; mortality at 0.89 (0.63–1.25) implies z of −0.67 and
**p = 0.51, where 0.4 is reported**. **Both intervals are symmetric, so this is not the signature of an exact
method** — the same reconstruction that matches three stated p-values to the third decimal fails on the other
two. **Nothing in the direction of either null changes, and I am not claiming the underlying analysis is
wrong: the reported probabilities are inconsistent with the reported intervals, and a reader cannot tell
which of the two is the typographical one.**

**The substantive finding is the dissociation.** Weaning success rises 9%, ventilation shortens by about a day,
intensive care stay by about a day and a half — **and reintubation and mortality are untouched.** This archive
has a rule for that shape: **a process measure can move without the outcome it proxies.** Weaning success is
defined by the protocol; reintubation is defined by the patient. **When a mode improves the first and not the
second, the honest reading is that it improves the weaning trial, and the claim that it improves liberation is
the one not yet demonstrated.**

**Three smaller observations.** The upper bound on weaning success is **1.18, and the lower bound is 1.01** —
the result clears zero effect by a margin narrow enough that the direction, not the size, is what the data
support. **I² of 0% on reintubation and 0% on ventilation duration across eight small trials is uninformative
rather than reassuring**, which is a standing rule here. And the abstract's own first sentence asserts the
conclusion as its premise — *"Pressure assist ventilation (PAV) is considered superior to pressure support
ventilation (PSV)"* — while also **misnaming the intervention the title gets right**.

**The record carries a 1 January 2026 print date with a month of publication of 0 and a date of publication of
"2026"**, which is the **year-only** family this archive catalogued, not a dual-deposit placeholder. The
electronic date, 29 October, is the real one.

**My reading.** **Proportional assist ventilation is a reasonable mode and this is a reasonable synthesis of a
thin literature**, and its own numbers say the gain is in how the weaning trial goes rather than in whether
the patient stays extubated. **At 1,057 patients across eight trials, reintubation and mortality are
underpowered rather than refuted**, and the authors' conclusion — *"PAV is superior to PSV for weaning"* —
is the defensible one precisely because it says weaning and not survival.

## 2 and 3. Two papers on the difference between an association and a test

**These two landed a day apart and make one point together.** The first has a model that discriminates well
and is unusable as it stands; the second has a marker with a p-value of 0.0002 and almost no discrimination at
all. **Discrimination, calibration and statistical significance are three different things, and each paper is
missing a different one.**

### 2. An area under the curve of 0.876, and odds overpredicted 2.8-fold

**[Subgroup deployment-readiness audit of calibration and clinical utility in perioperative infection
prediction](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocag184)** · *Journal of the American Medical Informatics
Association* · **published 30 October 2026** · paywalled; the abstract below is the published abstract

**10,719 procedures from 2014 to 2022 with 504 coded postoperative infection events** — a base rate of
**4.70%** — audited across sex, age and a physiological-reserve proxy. From the abstract:

> **Overall area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was 0.876, with similar
> sex-marginal discrimination in women and men (0.878 vs 0.875). Calibration-in-the-large was -1.02 overall,
> -0.86 in frail, and -1.34 in prefrail patients; the 6-event nonfrail stratum was not estimable. At t = 0.10,
> net benefit was positive in frail and negative in prefrail patients in the source cohort, but negative
> prefrail utility did not persist in the ascertainable-period (2018-2022) and temporal analyses. Common
> recalibration moved overall calibration-in-the-large from -1.020 to -0.007.**

**What those calibration numbers mean in clinical terms.** Calibration-in-the-large of **−1.02** on the
log-odds scale is a predicted odds of infection **e^1.02 = 2.8 times** the observed odds. **In the prefrail it
is −1.34, a factor of 3.8; in the frail −0.86, a factor of 2.4.** So the model ranks patients well and tells
every one of them a number that is roughly three times too large. **Recalibration fixes it almost
completely** — from a factor of 2.8 down to 1.007 — **which is the useful half of the paper: the defect is in
the intercept, not the ranking.**

**The threshold matters more than it looks.** The audit anchors on t = 0.10 against a base rate of 4.70%:
**a threshold at more than twice the prevalence, applied to a model that overpredicts by about threefold.**
That combination is exactly how a well-discriminating model produces negative net benefit in a subgroup, which
is what it did in the prefrail.

**And the nonfrail stratum had six events.** The authors report it as **not estimable** rather than reporting
it, which is the correct act and the one most papers skip. **Six events support no calibration statement at
all.** Separately, the outcome coding was **effectively absent before 2018**, so the prevalence-dependent
results are reported twice, for the whole period and for the ascertainable 2018–2022 window — **and the
negative prefrail utility did not survive that restriction.** Temporal-holdout discrimination fell from 0.876
to **0.851**, a drop of 0.025, with overprediction persisting.

**My reading.** **This is a model audit done the way audits should be done, published with the answer "not
ready".** Its transferable content is the sentence the authors put plainest: **discrimination-only or
sex-marginal evaluation would have missed the heterogeneity that matters.** This archive's standing rule is
that **discrimination is a statement about individuals and calibration a statement about groups**; here the
discrimination is good in both sexes and the calibration is wrong by a factor of three, and only the second
of those reaches the patient being quoted a risk.

### 3. A p-value of 0.0002 at an area under the curve of 0.59

**[Outcome-Blind EEG Microstate Topography After Cardiac Arrest: Spatial Expression of a Lateralized Microstate
and Association with Neurological Outcome](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10548-026-01267-7)** · *Brain Topography*
· **published 29 October 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**A secondary analysis of the multicentre I-CARE/PhysioNet database: 601 subjects screened, 34 excluded,
567 analysed** — **209 with favourable and 358 with unfavourable neurological outcome**, summing exactly, with
favourable at 36.9%. Microstates were decomposed **outcome-blind**, on a fixed 300-second window, with the
four-state solution frozen before any outcome analysis. From the abstract:

> **M2 global explained variance (GEV) was higher in patients with favorable outcome (median 0.0852 vs. 0.0564;
> rank-biserial correlation = 0.185; p = 0.000243; false-discovery-rate [FDR]-adjusted p = 0.000568 within the
> four-state GEV family). … Standalone ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.59 (95% CI 0.54-0.64). After adjustment
> for age, sex, and shockable rhythm in the extended cohort without return-of-spontaneous-circulation (ROSC)
> time (N = 535), each 1-SD increase in M2 GEV was associated with greater odds of favorable outcome
> (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.07-1.58; p = 0.0092). In the complete-case model additionally adjusted for ROSC time
> (N = 292), the association remained significant (OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.10-1.97; p = 0.0087).**

**The correlation and the curve are the same statistic written twice.** A rank-biserial correlation of 0.185
is an area under the curve of **(0.185 + 1) / 2 = 0.5925**, which is the reported 0.59. **So the paper's
headline p-value of 0.000243 and its area under the curve of 0.59 are not two findings, one strong and one
weak; they are one finding reported on two scales.** The p-value measures how certain we are that the medians
differ; the curve measures how much they differ. **At 567 patients the first can reach 0.0002 while the second
stays at 0.59, and this archive has the rule for it: significance without importance at large N.**

**The authors say so themselves**, and it is worth quoting because it is unusual:

> **Rather than functioning as a standalone prognostic test, M2 GEV reflects residual spatial organization
> that complements established clinical and background EEG markers.**

**Two further observations.** The adjusted estimate **rose** from 1.30 to 1.47 when return-of-circulation time
was added — but that model is complete-case at **N = 292, having dropped 243 of 535 patients, 45.4%.** **The
larger effect comes from the smaller and more selected subset**, which is the opposite of the attenuation
pattern that normally marks selection, and neither direction is reassuring at that attrition. Both intervals
are log-symmetric and their implied p-values, 0.0083 and 0.0096, sit near the stated 0.0092 and 0.0087.
**And only the spatial measure moved**: M2 coverage, mean duration and occurrence were all null after
multiplicity correction, so the signal is in how much of the field the configuration explains and not in how
long or how often it appears.

**My reading.** **The method is close to exemplary** — outcome-blind decomposition, a prespecified screening
rule, the model order frozen before backfitting, false-discovery control within a declared family, and
stability under winsorisation, bootstrap, leave-one-out, leave-five-out and centre clustering. **What it buys
is a real but small association**, and the honest framing is the authors' own. **Set beside yesterday's
processed-EEG meta-analysis, which cut delirium by 27% across 87 trials, the contrast is instructive: the same
signal source supports a strong intervention claim and a weak prognostic one, because an intervention is
judged on an average effect and a prognostic test on an individual one.**

## 4 and 5. Two studies whose intervals tell you the event count

### 4. Counterpulsation after bypass grafting, in 39 treated patients, starting three months late

**[Effect of enhanced external counterpulsation on saphenous vein graft patency in patients undergoing coronary
artery bypass grafting](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10800060/v1)** · **preprint, Research Square** ·
**posted 30 October 2026** · **not peer reviewed, and the numbers below should be read with that in mind**

**Retrospective; 145 patients having isolated coronary artery bypass grafting between January 2017 and June
2023, of whom 39 received enhanced external counterpulsation** — 35 one-hour sessions **beginning three months
after the operation** — against 106 who did not. Primary outcome **graft occlusion at one year on computed
tomography angiography**, with propensity score matching, logistic regression and Kaplan–Meier analysis at
both patient and graft level. From the abstract:

> **After PSM, the EECP group showed a lower incidence of SVG occlusion than controls in per-patient (1-year
> OR 0.325, 95% CI 0.102–0.931; 2-year OR 0.296, 95% CI 0.088–0.916) and per-graft (1-year OR 0.336, 95% CI
> 0.137–0.768; 2-year OR 0.232, 95% CI 0.084–0.58). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed these results after PSM
> (per patient: hazard ratio [HR] 0.245, 95% CI 0.074–0.808; per graft: HR 0.384, 95% CI 0.176–0.839). No
> significant differences were observed in stroke, heart failure, coronary revascularization, or recurrent
> angina.**

**Three problems, in order of size.**

**First, the exposure begins three months after the outcome clock starts.** Occlusion is counted at one year
from surgery; the therapy starts at month three and runs 35 sessions. **Everyone in the treated group
therefore survived three months, remained well enough to attend, and completed a course of hospital-based
treatment** — and nobody in the control group had to do any of that. **This is immortal time, and it is not a
subtlety here: the treated group is defined partly by having done well in the window before treatment
started.** The paper reports no landmark analysis and no time-varying exposure.

**Second, the per-graft analysis is the per-patient analysis with a borrowed denominator.** The exposure is
assigned at patient level; the outcome is observed at graft level, and grafts within one patient share that
patient's surgeon, conduit quality, blood pressure and antiplatelet adherence. **So the effective sample size
is the number of patients, not the number of grafts** — and the per-graft intervals are visibly narrower, the
one-year interval spanning 5.6-fold where the per-patient one spans 9.1-fold. **If the graft-level model does
not cluster by patient, that narrowing is manufactured rather than earned**, and the abstract does not say
whether it does.

**Third, the intervals say how little is behind them.** One-year per patient spans **9.1-fold**, two-year per
patient **10.4-fold**, the patient-level hazard ratio **10.9-fold**. **Intervals that wide come from a handful
of events**, and with 39 treated patients at a baseline occlusion risk the paper puts at 10–20% in the first
year, the treated-arm events are plausibly in the single digits. **The four odds ratios are also asymmetric on
the log scale by 7 to 10%, while both hazard ratios are symmetric** — consistent with a non-Wald interval for
the odds ratios in small samples, and recorded here as an observation rather than a defect.

**And the registration line is odd.** A ClinicalTrials.gov number dated 5 September 2017 is given for a study
the abstract describes as retrospectively enrolling patients from January 2017, with the registration itself
labelled *"retrospectively registered"*. **Prospective registration of a retrospective cohort is not a
contradiction, but the reader cannot tell from this which analyses were prespecified.**

**My reading.** **The mechanism is not absurd** — counterpulsation raises diastolic coronary perfusion, and
early vein graft failure is partly a flow problem. **But the design cannot distinguish the therapy from the
kind of patient who gets 35 hours of it starting three months after bypass surgery**, and every secondary
clinical outcome was null while the imaging surrogate moved. **The right next step is a landmark-anchored
analysis from month three, and after that a randomised trial; the right use of this preprint today is as a
hypothesis.**

### 5. An odds ratio of 2.75 resting on four events

**[Prognostic value of the sPESI score in critically ill patients with severe pulmonary embolism admitted to
the intensive care unit](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.medcli.2026.107664)** · *Medicina Clínica* ·
**published 30 October 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**A retrospective single-centre study at the University Hospital of Salamanca, 2021 to 2022: 64 patients with
intermediate-high or high-risk pulmonary embolism** — **57 intermediate-high (89.1%) and 7 high (10.9%)**,
summing exactly. From the abstract:

> **Among the 57 patients initially classified as intermediate-high risk, 4 (7.0%) subsequently progressed to
> high-risk PE. Patients who progressed had higher baseline sPESI scores than those who remained
> haemodynamically stable (3.5 [IQR: 2.5-4.2] vs 2.0 [IQR: 1.0-2.0]; P=.029). In exploratory Firth penalised
> logistic regression, a higher baseline sPESI score was associated with progression to high-risk PE (OR per
> 1-point increase: 2.75; 95%CI: 1.23-8.44; P=.012). In-hospital mortality was 7.8%.**

**The entire analysis rests on four patients.** 4 of 57 is the stated 7.0%; in-hospital mortality of 7.8% of
64 is **five deaths**. **So a regression with one predictor is being fitted to four events** — and the authors
label it *exploratory* and use **Firth penalisation**, which is the correct choice when the alternative is
a maximum-likelihood estimate that may not exist. **Both of those are credits, not criticisms.**

**What the numbers cannot carry.** The odds ratio interval spans **6.9-fold**, from 1.23 to 8.44, and is
asymmetric on the log scale by a factor of 1.39 — **as a penalised profile-likelihood interval should be, so
the usual reconstruction does not apply here and I have not applied it.** The median comparison is
**3.5 with an interquartile range of 2.5 to 4.2, computed from four observations**: with n = 4 the quartiles
are interpolations between adjacent values, so that range is close to the range of the four numbers
themselves. **And the seven patients already at high risk are excluded from the progression analysis**, which
is correct — they have already met the endpoint — but it means the paper's 64 patients contribute 57 to its
central question.

**My reading.** **The direction is unsurprising and probably right**: a severity score built to predict death
in pulmonary embolism also tracks haemodynamic deterioration. **What the paper establishes is that four
progressions in a single centre over two years cannot measure how much.** The honest statement is the
authors' last sentence — a higher score **was associated with** progression — and the per-point odds ratio of
2.75 should not be carried into a bedside threshold. **This archive's rule applies directly: count the events
behind an estimate before crediting its size.** Four.
