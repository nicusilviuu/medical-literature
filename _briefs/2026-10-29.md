## 1. A trial of the tube version of a recommendation that already exists as an incision

**[PROlonged posterior PERicardial chest tube treatment to reduce rates of postoperative atrial fibrillation
following cardiac surgery (PROPER trial): Rationale and design](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ahj.2026.107635)** —
*American Heart Journal*, **first published 28 October 2026**. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06800781.

**This is a protocol, not a result, and nothing below is an outcome.** It is included because of where it sits
relative to what this archive published three days ago.

**The design:** prospective, multinational, randomised 1:1; **624 patients across eight Nordic cardiac surgery
centres** — about 78 per centre; patients undergoing **non-emergent, first-time coronary bypass grafting,
aortic valve replacement, aortic surgery without circulatory arrest, or a combination**; intervention is
**intraoperative placement of a posterior pericardial chest tube** for prolonged drainage against standard
care. **Primary endpoint: atrial fibrillation recorded within seven days of surgery.** Secondary: AF
recurrence, hospitalisation for bleeding or ischaemic stroke, all-cause mortality. **Follow-up five years.**
**Monitoring is by wearable electrocardiographic patch in addition to routine surveillance.**

**The mechanism, as stated:**

> Accumulation of pericardial blood after surgery triggers a **localized inflammatory response** that has been
> identified as a contributing factor in POAF development.

**Why this matters here, and it is specific.** **Both of the 2026 guidelines this archive compared on
[26 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/) already recommend acting on exactly that mechanism.**
STS 2026 gives **posterior pericardiotomy Class IIa**; APHRS 2026 Part III gives **posterior left
pericardiotomy Class IIa, Level A**, citing the PALACS trial's 17% against 32%. **Those recommendations are
for a surgical incision in the pericardium. PROPER tests a chest tube.**

**Same mechanism, two implementations, and the guidelines have adopted the more invasive one while the simpler
one is only now enrolling.** That is not a criticism of the guidelines — they followed the trial that existed —
**but it is the clearest example this archive has of a recommendation resting on one way of achieving a
mechanism while the obvious alternative is untested.** The protocol's own framing agrees: posterior pericardial
drainage *"represents a readily available, low-risk strategy with the potential for rapid and broad adoption."*

**One methodological point that will matter when the results arrive, and it is worth recording now.**
**PROPER monitors with a wearable ECG patch; the trials underpinning the guidelines used routine
surveillance.** **Detected POAF incidence is a function of how hard you look.** Both PROPER arms get the patch,
so the comparison within the trial is fair — **but PROPER's absolute event rates will not be comparable with
PALACS's**, and anyone later setting a 2026 incision trial beside a 2031 drainage trial will be comparing two
detection regimes as well as two interventions. **The archive should hold this prediction and check it against
the published results.**

**My reading.** **A 624-patient trial of a chest tube is the kind of study that rarely gets funded and should
be.** If prolonged posterior drainage works as well as a pericardiotomy, it removes a surgical step from a
Class IIa recommendation; if it does not, that is informative about whether the mechanism is blood volume or
the incision itself. **The five-year follow-up with AF recurrence as a secondary endpoint is the better part of
the design**, because this archive already holds the finding — from the APHRS text — that postoperative AF
*"often reflects the unmasking of an underlying atrial substrate"* rather than being transient. **A trial that
prevents a seven-day arrhythmia and follows it for five years can test that claim directly.**

## 2. The guideline disagreement has an evidence base of 302 patients

**[Direct Oral Anticoagulants Versus Vitamin K Antagonists for Atrial Fibrillation After Cardiac Surgery: A
Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled
Trials](https://doi.org/10.1097/crd.0000000000001516)** — *Cardiology in Review*, **first published
29 October 2026** — today. Searched from inception through August 2026; Cochrane RoB 2; GRADE; random-effects.

**On 26 October this archive reported that STS 2026 and APHRS 2026 Part III disagree about anticoagulating
postoperative atrial fibrillation** — APHRS at **Class IIa, Level B** for POAF lasting ≥ 24–48 hours, STS
recorded as **Class IIb, individualised**, from secondary sources, with the page stating plainly that the STS
class could **not** be confirmed from its abstract and publishing the third disagreement as unconfirmed.

**This paper is the randomised evidence base for that question. Here is its size:**

> **Five randomized trials including 302 patients (153 DOACs and 149 warfarin) were included; rivaroxaban was
> tested in 3 trials, and apixaban and dabigatran in 1 trial each.**
>
> **Any bleeding did not differ significantly between DOACs and VKAs (9.2% vs 8.1%; OR, 1.24; 95% confidence
> interval [CI], 0.39–3.99).**

**Three hundred and two patients, across five trials, three different drugs.** Reconstructing the event counts
from the percentages: **9.2% of 153 is 14 patients and 8.1% of 149 is 12 — twenty-six bleeding events in
total.** **The confidence interval runs from 0.39 to 3.99, a tenfold span**, which is what twenty-six events
buys. The pooled odds ratio of 1.24 is modestly above the crude ratio of the summed counts, about 1.15, which
is normal for random-effects pooling across five small trials and not a discrepancy.

**So the question on which two 2026 guidelines take different positions rests on 302 randomised patients and
twenty-six bleeding events.** **That reframes the disagreement entirely.** It is not two panels reading the
same strong evidence differently — **it is two panels placing different weights on an absence**, and the
honest description of the state of knowledge is that nobody knows. **A Class IIa and a Class IIb are both
defensible readings of nothing.**

**One thing must be said plainly: the abstract in the index is incomplete.** It stops after the primary
outcome, mid-report. **The secondary outcomes are named in the methods — stroke or transient ischaemic attack,
systemic embolism, major bleeding, intracranial haemorrhage, reoperation, and length of hospital stay — and
none of their results appears.** The record has no PMCID and is not flagged open access, so the two-field
recovery route does not apply and the full text cannot be read from the index. **Nothing is inferred about
those six outcomes, and with 302 patients none of them can have been adequately powered.**

**My reading.** **The clinically useful statement is the negative one, and it is worth stating despite the
tiny sample: there is no randomised signal that direct oral anticoagulants bleed more than warfarin in this
population.** For a group of patients who are days past a sternotomy, on antiplatelets, often with chest
drains recently removed, that absence is reassuring as far as it goes — **and it goes about as far as
twenty-six events allow.** **The paper's real contribution is as a measurement of how little evidence there
is**, and that is the number a guideline panel should be reporting alongside its class.

## 3. A model its authors say cannot predict an individual, used correctly to benchmark hospitals

**[Variation in Pediatric Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation Survival After In-Hospital Cardiac
Arrest: Development and Validation of a Survival Model for Risk-Standardized Survival
Rates](https://doi.org/10.1097/ccm.0000000000007383)** — *Critical Care Medicine*, **first published
29 October 2026**. Extracorporeal Life Support Organization registry, **2020–2023**; children under 18 treated
with ECPR for in-hospital cardiac arrest.

**Method.** **1,060 patients in derivation and 317 in validation — 1,377 in total**, split by hospital to
preserve within-hospital clustering, which is the right split for a model that will be used to compare
hospitals. **Penalised logistic regression with LASSO; 14 routinely available clinical variables.**

| Metric | Derivation | Validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area under the curve | **0.728** | **0.678** |
| Observed-to-expected ratio | — | **0.995** |
| Brier score | — | **0.222** |

**Between-hospital variation, for centres reporting five or more ECPR cases: median odds ratio 1.30 (95% CI
1.11–1.93)**, described as indicating *"significant differences in survival likelihood between centers after
adjusting for patient-level risk."*

**The paper's own verdict on its model is the sentence to lead with:**

> **The model performance as a discriminator likely limits its use as a standalone tool for clinical
> decision-making.** However, **strong internal calibration** allowed for determination of hospital-level
> RSSRs…

**That distinction is correct and under-appreciated, and it is the reason this paper is worth reading.**
**Discrimination is about individuals; calibration is about groups.** An area under the curve of 0.678 means
that given one survivor and one non-survivor, the model ranks them correctly about two times in three — far
too weak to tell a family anything. **But a model that is systematically unbiased across the range, with an
observed-to-expected ratio of 0.995, can legitimately adjust a hospital's aggregate rate even while being
useless for any one child.** **Most papers conflate the two; this one separates them and acts on the
separation.**

**Two cautions, and the second is a genuine gap.**

**First, the median odds ratio's interval is strikingly asymmetric** — 1.30 with bounds at 1.11 and 1.93, so
on the log scale the upper half-width is two and a half times the lower. **That is expected rather than
wrong:** a median odds ratio is derived from the random-intercept variance, whose sampling distribution is
skewed, so symmetric intervals should not be expected here. **Worth saying because this archive checks every
interval for symmetry, and this is the case where asymmetry is the correct behaviour.**

**Second, and this is the omission: the abstract never states the survival rate.** **A Brier score cannot be
interpreted without it.** For a binary outcome, predicting the base rate for every patient gives a Brier score
of p(1−p): **at 45% survival that is 0.2475, so the model's 0.222 is a real improvement; at 30% survival it is
0.2100, which would be better than the model.** **So whether 0.222 represents useful calibration or none at
all depends on a number the abstract does not give.** Reconstructing it is not possible from what is reported.

**My reading.** **The honest summary is that this is an infrastructure paper and should be judged as one.** It
is not trying to predict whether a child will survive; it is trying to make it possible to say whether a
centre's results are better or worse than its case mix warrants, which is a prerequisite for improvement work
and does not exist for paediatric ECPR today. **The authors then decline to use it: "additional external
validation and evaluation of residual case-mix differences are needed before such models can be used for
benchmarking across pediatric ECPR centers."** **A paper that builds a benchmarking tool and says do not
benchmark with it yet is behaving properly**, and the median odds ratio of 1.30 should be read as evidence
that between-centre variation is worth investigating, not as a league table.

**Archive context, and it is a direct pair.** The [27 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-27/)
carried a nationwide adult ECPR analysis whose contribution was a redefinition of who counts as a control, and
whose treated fraction was **1.55%**. **Today's paper is the same modality in children with the opposite
problem: not who to compare the treated against, but whether the treated's outcomes can be compared across
centres at all.** **Two ECPR papers in three days, both about the comparison rather than the treatment.**

## 4. The drug a sustainability statement targets, and what its clinical case actually rests on

**[Evidence for formulating recommendations on the use of desflurane vs sevoflurane and propofol in general
anaesthesia: an umbrella review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.redare.2026.502163)** — *Revista Española de
Anestesiología y Reanimación*, **first published 28 October 2026**. **Thirty-one systematic reviews published
between 2014 and 2024**; methodological quality by **AMSTAR 2**, certainty by **GRADE**.

**The findings, in full, because the abstract is short:**

> **Desflurane was consistently associated with faster recovery than sevoflurane** across the analysed
> subgroups, **although the certainty of evidence was low or very low.** Compared with propofol, favourable
> findings for desflurane in selected early recovery outcomes were **less consistent**. **No clinically
> relevant differences were identified for morbidity outcomes**, except for **a possible advantage of propofol
> in postoperative nausea and vomiting and emergence agitation.** Desflurane may be **conditionally
> considered** when rapid recovery is prioritized, with individualized decision-making required.

**Read this one day after yesterday's guidelines entry.** The
[28 October watch](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-28/) carried the **Singapore Society of
Anaesthesiologists position statement on sustainability**, whose premise is that healthcare accounts for 7% of
Singapore's carbon emissions. **Desflurane is the single largest target of anaesthetic sustainability work** —
it has by far the highest global warming potential of the volatile agents in routine use, and restricting or
removing it is the one intervention in this field with an unambiguous effect size.

**So the two documents, a day apart, between them answer the question a clinician actually faces.** **The
clinical case for keeping desflurane is faster recovery than sevoflurane, consistent in direction across
subgroups, at low or very low certainty.** Against propofol the recovery advantage is **less consistent**.
**No morbidity difference anywhere**, and the one morbidity signal that exists runs **against** desflurane, in
favour of propofol, on nausea, vomiting and emergence agitation.

**My reading.** **An umbrella review is the right instrument for this question and the result is as clean as
this literature allows: the benefit is real in direction, small, confined to recovery speed, and poorly
certain.** **That is a weak counterweight to an unambiguous environmental cost**, and the review's own verdict
— *"may be conditionally considered when rapid recovery is prioritized"* — is close to the operational
conclusion: a reserve agent for the specific case where minutes to emergence matter, not a default.

**Two limitations to hold.** **Thirty-one reviews over eleven publication years is a synthesis of syntheses,
and AMSTAR 2 assesses the reviews rather than the trials inside them** — so "low or very low certainty"
is carrying the weight here and should not be skimmed. **And the subgroups are not named in the abstract**:
"consistently … across the analysed subgroups" is only as strong as which subgroups were analysed, and the
obvious ones for a recovery-speed claim — obesity, the elderly, long cases — are exactly where the claim would
matter most and where the abstract leaves a reader blind.

**Archive context: desflurane has not appeared in this archive before**, and anaesthetic gas choice has
appeared only as yesterday's sustainability statement and a volatile-agent ischaemia-reperfusion review
logged in passing. **This is the first evidence synthesis on the clinical side of the question.**
