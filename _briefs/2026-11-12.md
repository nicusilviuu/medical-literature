## 1. Stop the ACE inhibitor: hypotension down fourteen points, nothing different at thirty days

**[Effect of discontinuing antihypertensives on perioperative hypotension in older patients before non-cardiac
surgery](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2026-101087)** · *BMJ* · **published 11 November 2026** · paywalled;
abstract quoted below

**Multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled. Five tertiary teaching hospitals in China,
November 2020 to December 2023. 2,036 patients aged 60 to 80 on an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor
blocker for at least two weeks, having non-cardiac surgery under general anaesthesia, randomised to their
usual drug or an identical placebo on the morning of surgery.** 2,012 completed — 24 lost, 1.2% — in arms of
1,004 and 1,008. From the abstract:

> The incidence of perioperative hypotension was lower in the no antihypertensive group (314/1008, 31.2%)
> versus antihypertensive group (457/1004, 45.5%): adjusted odds ratio 0.52 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.43
> to 0.63); absolute risk difference -14.4% (95% CI -18.5% to -10.1%); P<0.001. The duration of hypotension
> and requirement for a vasopressor were both reduced in the no antihypertensive group: adjusted mean
> difference -1.3 minutes (95% CI -1.8 to -0.8 minutes) and 28.5% v 41.1%; adjusted odds ratio 0.54 (95% CI
> 0.44 to 0.66). Intraoperative fluid volume, postoperative hypotension, 30 day complications, and mortality
> did not differ significantly between groups.

**Every figure reconstructs: 314 of 1,008 is 31.15%, 457 of 1,004 is 45.52%, and the difference is 14.37
points against a stated 14.4.** **The number needed to treat to prevent one episode of perioperative
hypotension is 6.96 — about seven patients.** Both odds-ratio intervals are log-symmetric and both are
decisively away from one.

**This is a large, clean, well-conducted trial and the finding is real. Two things qualify what it means.**

**First, and this is the number the headline will lose: the duration of hypotension fell by 1.3 minutes.**
**Incidence down 14.4 percentage points, duration down 78 seconds.** **So the episodes being prevented were
short ones.** **Those two numbers together describe a trial that reduced the count of a briefly-defined event
rather than the burden of hypotension** — and the primary outcome's definition does the work: mean pressure
below 65 mmHg **or a 30% fall from baseline requiring treatment.** **An event that requires treatment is an
event a clinician noticed and corrected, which is why there are fewer minutes of it than the incidence
difference suggests.**

**Second, nothing differed at thirty days.** **Neurocognitive disorders, major cardiocerebral events and
mortality were all unchanged**, as was postoperative hypotension. **So this is an intraoperative process
result**, and this archive's standing rule applies: a process measure can move without the outcome it proxies.
**The honest claim is that withholding the drug makes the anaesthetic easier to conduct, not that it makes
the patient safer** — and at 2,012 patients the thirty-day outcomes are underpowered rather than refuted.

**One claim to push back on.** The authors write that the strategy **"provides high certainty evidence for
preoperative drug management in this patient population."** **High certainty is a GRADE judgement about a body
of evidence, not a property a single trial can confer on itself.** **One trial in one country, however well
run, is not high-certainty evidence** — and the phrase matters because guideline committees reading it will
be the audience.

**My reading.** **Withhold the morning dose: the intraoperative case for it is now strong and the thirty-day
case is absent in both directions.** **The practical value is real — fewer vasopressor boluses, 28.5% against
41.1% — and the practical limit is that the trial measured what the anaesthetist experiences rather than what
the patient takes home.**

## 2 and 3. A journal publishes a delirium trial and a critique of its endpoint in the same week

**These two are from the same journal with publication dates one day apart, and reading them together is more
instructive than reading either alone.**

### 2. The endpoint: never rigorously evaluated

**[Composite Endpoints in Critical Care Delirium Trials](https://doi.org/10.1097/ccm.0000000000007420)** ·
*Critical Care Medicine* · **published 11 November 2026** · paywalled

**A methods paper, using data from the MENDS trial.** From the abstract:

> …measuring and analyzing "duration of delirium" as an endpoint in randomized trials is challenging due to
> the episodic nature of delirium and the competing events of coma and death, which can bias analyses of
> duration of delirium. Given these challenges, delirium trials have adopted one of two composite endpoints:
> delirium- and coma-free days, defined as the number of days alive without delirium or coma for both
> survivors and nonsurvivors, and an alternative composite that ranks death as the worst state and orders days
> alive without delirium or coma among survivors. To date, these composite endpoints have not been rigorously
> evaluated.

**The problem it names is specific and real.** **Delirium is episodic, and coma and death are competing events
that remove a patient from the risk of being delirious.** **A patient who dies on day two accrues no delirium
days, which makes a naive duration analysis reward killing patients.** **Delirium- and coma-free days solves
that by counting non-survivors' days as zero — and in doing so makes death and delirium exchangeable at a
fixed rate nobody chose.** **The ranked alternative, which puts death below every survivor outcome, is the
same move this archive praised in the core outcome set published two days ago: a hierarchical endpoint
resolves by what matters most rather than summing dissimilar events.**

**"To date, these composite endpoints have not been rigorously evaluated" is a strong sentence about a
literature's primary outcome measure**, and it is the third time in three days that this archive has met the
composite-endpoint problem — after OPTIPAM's death-or-kidney-injury-or-neurological composite on 10 November
and the hierarchical endpoint ratified in the acute brain injury core outcome set the same day.
**Three independent documents converging on one methodological complaint within a week is the kind of
coincidence worth naming.**

### 3. The trial: the endpoint in use, and significant only unadjusted

**[The Effect of Guanfacine on Delirium in Surgical and Trauma ICU Patients: A Pilot Randomized Clinical
Trial](https://doi.org/10.1097/ccm.0000000000007448)** · *Critical Care Medicine* · **published
11 November 2026** · paywalled

**Single-centre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot. 100 critically ill adults with
CAM-ICU-confirmed delirium, randomised 1:1 to 2 mg guanfacine nightly or placebo for up to 14 days.** The
primary outcomes are feasibility, not efficacy. From the abstract:

> …protocol adherence was 100%, retention was 100%, and data completeness was 100%. Recruitment was
> significantly impacted by COVID-19 pandemic disruptions, achieving 0.9 patients/wk during active recruitment
> periods. For clinical outcomes, in unadjusted analyses, median delirium and coma-free days were higher with
> guanfacine than placebo (2 [interquartile range [IQR], 0-6] vs. 1 [IQR, 0-4]; p = 0.02); however, this
> association was not statistically significant after adjustment for prespecified baseline covariates.

**The feasibility result is genuinely good: adherence, retention and data completeness all exactly 100%.**
That is rare and the trial should be credited for it.

**The recruitment figure needs unpicking, because as stated it is not what it appears.** **The target was at
least 2 patients per month; the reported achievement is 0.9 patients per week during active recruitment
periods, which is 3.9 per month — above target.** **But it is conditioned on "active recruitment periods",
and the sentence immediately before says recruitment was significantly impacted by pandemic disruptions.**
**A rate computed only over the periods when recruitment was running is not the trial's recruitment rate**,
and the two halves of that sentence pull in opposite directions. **The trial took from November 2020 to
February 2023 — about 27 months — to enrol 100 patients, which is 3.7 per month overall; so the conditioning
turns out not to matter much, but the abstract does not give the reader that check.**

**And the clinical signal is exactly what item 2 is about.** **Median delirium- and coma-free days of 2
against 1, p = 0.02 unadjusted, not significant after adjustment for prespecified covariates.** **Both
interquartile ranges start at zero**, so a substantial share of patients in both arms accrued no
delirium-free, coma-free days at all — which is the floor effect that makes this endpoint hard to analyse, in
a trial reporting it, in the same week as a paper saying so.

**My reading of the pair.** **Guanfacine remains unevaluated and the pilot says so honestly: feasibility
established, efficacy signal present unadjusted and gone adjusted, progression to a multicentre trial
recommended.** **What the pair demonstrates is that the field's measurement problem is now acknowledged in
the same pages where it is being incurred.** **A definitive guanfacine trial designed after reading its
journal-mate would choose its endpoint differently — and the archive's practical rule is that a delirium
trial's reported effect is not comparable across trials until you know which composite it used and how death
was ranked.**

## 4. Antibiotic delay matters for two of seven sepsis criteria

**[Optimizing Sepsis Time Zero Definitions Through Associations between Antibiotic Timing and
Mortality](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajrccm/aamag610)** · *American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care
Medicine* · **published 12 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Electronic health record data from 145 US hospitals, 2018 to 2023. 1,849,729 encounters with suspected
infection; 624,416 — 33.8% — had at least one organ dysfunction and received antibiotics within six hours of
time zero.** Seven SEP-1 organ dysfunction criteria tested, alone and in combination, with inverse
probability weighting for baseline and time-varying confounders. From the abstract:

> Antibiotics at 1-3 vs. 0-1h were associated with higher mortality when time zero was defined by hypotension
> (aRR 1.07; 95% CI, 1.03-1.10) or lactate ≥4.0mmol/L (aRR 1.10; 1.05-1.15), but not for other dysfunctions
> alone or in combination. Among patients without hypotension or lactate ≥4.0mmol/L, antibiotics at 3-6 vs.
> 0-3h were only associated with higher mortality in patients with acute respiratory failure (aRR 1.11;
> 1.05-1.18).

**The negative result is the finding.** **Seven criteria were tested and delay beyond one hour was associated
with death for two of them — hypotension and a lactate of 4.0 or more. For renal dysfunction,
hyperbilirubinaemia, thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy, alone or in any combination, it was not.** **A third,
acute respiratory failure, showed an association only beyond three hours.**

**That is an argument about definitions, not about antibiotics**, and it is the sharpest instance yet of a
pattern this archive named two days ago: across eight commentaries on the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign
guidelines, **the disagreements are about definitions and triggers rather than drugs.** **Here the complaint
is operational and quantified: SEP-1's time zero lumps together patients for whom an hour costs lives and
patients for whom it demonstrably does not, and a performance measure built on the union of seven criteria
inherits the average.**

**Two cautions on the effect sizes.** **The relative risks are 1.07, 1.10 and 1.11 — small, and significant
because the denominator is 624,416.** **This archive's rule about significance without importance at large N
applies, but with a qualification: the outcome is in-hospital death, so a 7% relative increase is not
negligible even when the relative scale is unimpressive.** **What cannot be read from the abstract is the
absolute difference**, and without a baseline mortality the 1.07 cannot be converted into lives. **A second,
smaller point: the hypotension interval, 1.03 to 1.10 around 1.07, looks asymmetric on the log scale — but at
two decimal places the rounding is larger than the asymmetry, so no inference is available from it.**

**And this is observational, at scale.** Inverse probability weighting for time-varying confounders is the
right tool and does not make antibiotic timing randomised. **The patients who got antibiotics in the first
hour were recognised in the first hour, and recognition tracks severity in both directions.**

**My reading.** **The practical proposal is good and the paper states it modestly: these findings could inform
refinements to time-zero definitions.** **A sepsis performance measure that demands antibiotics within an
hour of any of seven triggers is, on this evidence, right about two of them** — and the cost of being wrong
about the other four is antibiotic exposure in patients for whom the clock was never the issue.
**That is the stewardship argument the SSC 2026 document, routed to this archive's guidelines watch, says
both major guidelines are moving toward.**

## 5. Zero events, twice, and an interval twice as long on one side

**[Efficacy and safety of oliceridine versus remifentanil for analgesia in mechanically ventilated critically
ill adults (CO-ROAM)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08643-8)** · *Intensive Care Medicine* ·
**published 10 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Multicentre, assessor- and patient-blinded, non-inferiority randomised trial across 19 intensive care units
in China. Adults on invasive mechanical ventilation randomised 1:1 to oliceridine — a G-protein-biased
μ-opioid agonist — or remifentanil, titrated to a Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool score below 3.**
307 randomised, 304 in the modified intention-to-treat population (151 and 153, summing exactly).
**Primary endpoint time in target analgesic range, non-inferiority margin −10%.** From the abstract:

> Median TTR was 96.0% versus 92.3% (absolute difference 3.7%, 95% CI -0.3-5.7%). Oliceridine was associated
> with fewer mild-to-moderate adverse drug reactions (ADRs), occurring at 15.9 versus 22.2 events per 100
> patient-days (IRR 0.72, 95% CI 0.52-1.00, P = 0.049), and a lower rate of respiratory depression during the
> study period (0% vs. 4.6%, P = 0.015). Post-extubation respiratory depression (0% vs. 7.9%, P = 0.005) and
> analgesic-sedation costs were both lower with oliceridine.

**Non-inferiority is met with an enormous margin to spare: a margin of −10% against an observed difference of
+3.7%.** **As with the remimazolam trial published here yesterday, a non-inferiority design that overshoots
its own margin this far was set up for a question the drug has already answered.**

**The confidence interval is the thing to flag, and it is not a rounding artefact.** **A difference of 3.7%
with an interval from −0.3% to 5.7% is 4.0 points below the estimate and 2.0 above it — asymmetric by exactly
two to one.** **A difference of proportions has a symmetric Wald interval, so this is not one**; it is
plausibly a bootstrap or exact interval, or the medians rather than means are being differenced. **The
abstract does not say which, and the asymmetry is large enough that the usual reconstruction cannot be applied
to it.** **Recorded as a flag, not a defect.**

**The safety results are the substance and they contain zero twice.** **Respiratory depression occurred in 0%
of the oliceridine arm against 4.6% on remifentanil during the study, and 0% against 7.9% after
extubation.** **Two zero-event arms in a 151-patient group is a real finding and also the hardest kind to
quantify** — no confidence interval can be constructed from zero in the usual way, and the p-values of 0.015
and 0.005 carry the whole claim. **On 153 remifentanil patients, 4.6% and 7.9% are about seven and twelve
events.** **So the comparison is nought against seven and nought against twelve, which is suggestive and
thin.**

**And the adverse-reaction result sits exactly on the boundary.** **An incidence rate ratio of 0.72 with an
interval from 0.52 to 1.00 and p = 0.049.** **An upper bound of exactly 1.00 is the least informative
statistically significant result available**, and this archive has logged the same shape before.

**My reading.** **Oliceridine looks like a genuinely better-tolerated opioid for ventilated patients and the
respiratory-depression signal is the reason to care — a G-protein-biased agonist is designed to do precisely
that, so the finding is mechanistically expected rather than surprising.** **What the trial does not
establish is any difference in what happens to patients: ventilation duration, intensive care stay and
28-day mortality were all unchanged.** **The honest summary is a tolerability advantage at equal analgesia
and lower cost, in 304 patients in one country, with the key safety comparisons resting on about nineteen
events in total.**
