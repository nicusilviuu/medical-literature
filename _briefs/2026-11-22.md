## 1. A trial that set its threshold in advance and then declared against itself

**[Opioid-free vs. opioid-inclusive anaesthesia in laparoscopic cholecystectomy: a multicentre randomised
controlled trial](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.70462)** · *Anaesthesia* · **published 20 November 2026** ·
315 patients, March to September 2025

> "The primary outcome was the overall benefit of analgesia score (0-28, lower scores indicate greater
> benefit) at 24 h postoperatively; a 3-point difference was considered clinically relevant... The mean (SD)
> 24-h overall benefit of analgesia score was 2.2 (2.4) after opioid-free anaesthesia vs 3.4 (3.1) after
> opioid-inclusive anaesthesia (adjusted mean difference -1.3; 95%CI -1.8 to -0.8, p < 0.001), without
> reaching the threshold for clinical importance... chronic postsurgical pain (16.1% vs 15.5%; adjusted
> relative risk 1.03; 95%CI 0.55-1.91) did not differ... Opioid-free anaesthesia was associated with reduced
> intra-operative hypotension (19.6% vs 35.7%; relative risk 0.55; 95%CI 0.37-0.80) and lower incidence of
> postoperative nausea and vomiting (23.4% vs 33.8%; relative risk 0.69; 95%CI 0.48-0.98). Opioid-free
> anaesthesia did not achieve a clinically meaningful improvement... These findings do not support its
> routine adoption."

**Interpretation — this is the third instance in a fortnight of significance below the minimal important
difference, and it is the best-handled of the three.** The quadratus-lumborum-versus-TAP comparison reported
−0.25 and −0.40 on a pain scale without supplying the comparison at all; the pregabalin review reported
SMD −0.51 and stated the problem itself. **Here the threshold was pre-specified at 3 points, the observed
difference was 1.3, and the conclusion is that the finding "does not support routine adoption."** A trial
that hits p < 0.001 and recommends against its own intervention has done the thing the whole apparatus of
pre-specification exists for.

**The threshold itself deserves scrutiny, and this is a criticism of the design rather than of the result.**
The overall benefit of analgesia score runs 0 to 28 with lower being better, and **both arms scored under 4
— 2.2 and 3.4.** A 3-point minimal important difference on a scale where the *control* arm averages 3.4
means the trial could only have succeeded by driving the score to roughly zero. **The threshold was almost
certainly derived from a population in more pain than this one**, and in a well-managed laparoscopic
cholecystectomy with prophylactic antiemetics, flurbiprofen axetil and ropivacaine infiltration in both
arms, there is very little room left to improve. **The standard deviations say the same thing: 2.4 on a mean
of 2.2 and 3.1 on a mean of 3.4, so both distributions are pressed against the floor of the scale and
right-skewed.**

**The hypotension finding is close to a tautology of the drug choice, and the abstract's wording invites the
wrong reading.** "Opioid-free" here means **dexmedetomidine and esketamine**; "opioid-inclusive" means
**sufentanil**. Esketamine is sympathomimetic and raises blood pressure; sufentanil lowers it. **So
intra-operative hypotension of 19.6% against 35.7% is substantially "esketamine raises blood pressure",
not "avoiding opioids protects perfusion"** — and it is the more interesting for being a net effect, because
dexmedetomidine causes hypotension and bradycardia in its own right, as yesterday's cross-database
pharmacovigilance study documented at 717 events. **The regimen as a whole reduced hypotension; the label
"opioid-free" does not explain why.**

**The number in this abstract that matters most is not about either arm.** **Chronic postsurgical pain at
90 days was 16.1% and 15.5% — about one in six patients after the most common abdominal operation in the
world, unchanged by the anaesthetic technique.** The trial was not powered for it (aRR 1.03, 0.55–1.91, a
3.5-fold interval), so the null is uninformative about the comparison. **The incidence is not uninformative
at all.**

**And the postoperative nausea finding has a bound worth stating: all patients received prophylactic
anti-emetics**, so the 33.8% in the opioid-inclusive arm is nausea and vomiting *despite* prophylaxis, and
the RR of 0.69 has an upper bound of 0.98 — the one borderline result in the set.

## 2. Sixty-six minutes of ventilation saved, and nothing else moved

**[Effect of fascial plane blocks providing postoperative analgesia on mechanical ventilation duration in
the intensive care unit and early oxygenation after cardiac
surgery](https://doi.org/10.23736/s0375-9393.26.20175-x)** · *Minerva Anestesiologica* · **published
20 November 2026** · 206 patients, retrospective with inverse probability of treatment weighting

> "Patients in the Block group demonstrated a significantly shorter median duration of mechanical
> ventilation compared to controls (300 vs. 420 min; RMST difference: -65.8 min; 95% CI: -94.2 to -41.6;
> P<0.001). The incidence of prolonged intubation, defined as mechanical ventilation exceeding 480 minutes,
> was markedly lower in the Block group (G1=6.5% vs. G0=20.2%; Firth OR: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.11-0.75; P=0.011).
> Postoperative pain scores were significantly reduced across all assessment time points... total opioid
> requirements declined by approximately 28%... No statistically significant between-group differences were
> detected with respect to the PaO2/FiO2 ratio or the length of intensive care unit stay."

**Interpretation — the two nulls are the finding.** Ventilation was 66 minutes shorter; **oxygenation did
not differ and ICU length of stay did not differ.** The authors' stated mechanism was that better analgesia
after sternotomy improves respiratory mechanics, and **the oxygenation measure that would show that is
null.** What moved was the time to extubation.

**Which makes this an instance of two standing rules at once.** **A process measure can move without the
outcome it proxies** — an hour of ventilation saved that does not shorten the ICU stay has not changed the
patient's trajectory through the unit. And **extubation timing after cardiac surgery is a decision, not an
event**: a patient in less pain, on 28% less opioid, is judged ready sooner. **That is a real benefit, to
the pathway and to the patient's experience, and it is not evidence that the lung did better.** The paper's
own conclusion claims "meaningful clinical benefits", which is defensible for opioid reduction and pain
scores and overstates what the ventilation figure shows on its own.

**Credit where it is due, because the statistical handling is unusually careful for a 206-patient
single-centre retrospective study.** Inverse probability of treatment weighting on seven covariates rather
than a bare comparison; **restricted mean survival time reported alongside Kaplan-Meier and Cox**, which is
the right choice when the question is "how much time" rather than "what hazard"; **Firth penalisation for
prolonged intubation**, appropriate at 7 events in one arm; and **Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the
pain timepoints.** Four methodological choices that most papers of this size skip.

**The bound is the allocation and it is not closeable by weighting.** Groups were formed "based on the
postoperative analgesic strategy employed" — that is, by clinician choice at the time, and the clinicians
choosing to place blocks could be selecting patients they already expected to extubate early.
**Seven covariates is a reasonable attempt and the exposure is a clinical judgement, which is exactly the
channel propensity methods leave open.** The control is also only described as "conventional multimodal
analgesia", undefined.

## 3. A head-to-head comparison in which the two arms share no trials

**[Gut microbiome-based interventions to prevent ICU-acquired infections: A systematic review and
meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1080/19490976.2026.2765035)** · *Gut Microbes* · **published 19 November
2026** · 9 SDD trials (19,907 subjects) and 29 biotic trials (5,483 subjects)

> "In the absence of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing these two interventions, this
> systematic review and meta-analysis sought to compare the effect sizes of SDD versus biotic
> interventions... neither intervention significantly reduced overall mortality (SDD: RR = 0.96 [95%CI:
> 0.88-1.05]; biotics: RR = 0.93 [95%CI: 0.87-1.00]). SDD was associated with modestly lower observed risk
> for ICU acquired all-cause infections compared to biotics (SDD: RR = 0.76 [95%CI: 0.60-0.98]; biotics:
> RR = 0.82 [95%CI: 0.64-1.05], no statistically significant difference) and specifically lower risk for
> pneumonia (SDD: RR = 0.50 [95%CI: 0.26-0.96]; prebiotics and synbiotics: RR = 0.70 [95%CI: 0.54-0.90])...
> The studies selected were heterogeneous, especially those testing biotic interventions."

**Interpretation — the design is named honestly in the abstract and still cannot bear the comparison.** The
two arms are separate trial sets measured against their own comparators, so **this is an indirect
comparison with no adjustment for the differences between the two evidence bases**, and the paper's own
phrasing is "compare the effect sizes" rather than compare the interventions.

**Two days ago this archive's lead was a meta-research study showing that 42 reviews of corticosteroids in
sepsis mostly share no randomised trial, so counting their conclusions counts disjoint evidence bases.**
Here the two bodies share **no trial by construction**, which is the same problem made explicit.

**And the asymmetry between the two evidence bases is severe enough to explain the headline on its own.**
**19,907 subjects across 9 SDD trials is 2,212 per trial; 5,483 across 29 biotic trials is 189 per trial —
an 11.7-fold difference.** So the finding that SDD's infection RR of 0.76 is "modestly lower" than biotics'
0.82 with "no statistically significant difference" is close to arithmetic: **the biotic interval is wide
because its trials are small, and two estimates 0.06 apart with one interval crossing unity is what that
produces.**

**The pneumonia comparison is the least defensible line and it changes its own exposure definition.** SDD
0.50 (0.26–0.96) against **"prebiotics and synbiotics"** 0.70 (0.54–0.90) — probiotics have silently dropped
out of the biotic arm between the infection outcome and the pneumonia outcome. **And the SDD estimate is
both larger and wider than the biotic one, which is the signature of fewer events, not of a stronger
effect.**

**The sharpest point is a cross-reference to yesterday.** Yesterday's guidelines watch reported a *Journal
of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy* critique arguing that the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign's new SDD suggestion
cannot distinguish benefit to treated patients from harm to concurrent controls sharing the unit, because
antimicrobial spillover works in the opposite direction. **That objection applies directly to the RR of 0.50
for pneumonia here, which is this paper's strongest number, and it is not mentioned.** Two papers published
one day apart, one of which is the methodological objection to the other's headline.

**One further bound: the search was PubMed only.** A single-database search for a systematic review is a
limitation the abstract does not list among the ones it does.

## 4. A 2.1-fold respiratory risk, from an exposure that is a diagnosis rather than a habit

**[Association of preoperative vaping-related disorder versus cigarette nicotine dependence with
postoperative respiratory complications](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2026.08.002)** · *British Journal of
Anaesthesia* · **published 20 November 2026** · TriNetX US Collaborative Network, 3,817 matched pairs

> "Among 3817 matched pairs, preoperative vaping-related disorder was associated with a higher risk of
> 30-day respiratory complications than cigarette nicotine dependence (10.7% vs 5.1%, relative risk [RR]
> 2.11, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.79-2.49)... pneumonia (RR 3.20, 95% CI 2.40-4.28), respiratory
> failure (RR 2.48, 95% CI 1.98-3.10), bronchospasm (RR 1.92, 95% CI, 1.38-2.68), and noninvasive
> ventilation (RR 2.56, 95% CI 1.64-3.98)... These findings suggest that vaping history should be
> specifically assessed during preoperative evaluation."

**Interpretation — the exposure is in the title and it is not what the conclusion generalises to.** The
exposure is **"vaping-related disorder", a coded diagnosis**, and the comparator is **"cigarette nicotine
dependence"**, also coded. **Those two codes do not select comparable populations.** Nicotine dependence is
recorded routinely for anyone who smokes and says so. A vaping-related *disorder* is recorded when vaping
has been clinically identified as causing a problem — which selects symptomatic vapers, including those with
vaping-associated lung injury. **The comparison is a symptomatic subgroup against an unselected one, and
that configuration can generate a two-fold composite without any excess risk attaching to vaping as a
habit.**

**The internal pattern is consistent with that reading rather than against it: pneumonia at RR 3.20 is the
largest component**, and vaping-associated lung injury presents as pneumonitis, so the outcome most likely
to be the coded disorder's own manifestation is the one most elevated.

**Which makes the conclusion's step too large.** "Vaping history should be specifically assessed" moves from
a coded disorder to a history, and the data speak only to the former. **Asking about vaping at preoperative
assessment is sensible advice on other grounds; this study does not establish the risk estimate that would
justify it.**

**The most persuasive element is the gradient, with one caveat about how it is built.** Vaping alone against
neither diagnosis gives RR 3.11 (2.56–3.76); vaping plus cigarettes against cigarettes alone gives 1.98
(1.55–2.53). **The two comparisons have different reference groups, so they are not points on one scale and
should not be read as a dose-response** — but both pointing the same way with intervals well clear of unity
is the strongest thing here.

**Credit for reporting four components separately with intervals**, so the composite is not carrying the
result alone. **The composite rates imply roughly 408 and 195 events across the matched pairs**, which is
ample for the primary estimate and thinner for bronchospasm and non-invasive ventilation, whose intervals
show it.

## 5. The paper this archive said two days ago did not exist

**[A Machine Learning-Based Early Warning System for Renal Replacement Therapy in the Intensive Care
Unit](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000008405)** · *Anesthesia & Analgesia* · **published 17 November
2026** · MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV and eICU

> "In internal validation, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was 0.90 (95%
> confidence interval [CI], 0.886-0.906) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) was 0.53
> (95% CI, 0.496-0.564) in MIMIC-III... External validation on ICU patients admitted during the coronavirus
> disease (COVID) period yielded an AUROC of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.894-0.913) and an AUPRC of 0.57 (95% CI,
> 0.534-0.602). Additional hospital-level external validation across eight individual eICU hospitals showed
> AUROCs ranging from 0.86 to 0.91 and AUPRCs ranging from 0.35 to 0.52... the early prediction ML model
> could serve as a triage tool to prompt closer nephrology evaluation."

**Interpretation — read this against the appraisal published two days ago.** That re-evaluation of machine
learning for extubation failure audited 14 published studies of the outcome and found that **7 reported
calibration, 4 had any external validation, 3 reported net benefit, and none used repeated or nested
cross-validation**, and that explainability figures outnumbered calibration reports. **This paper reports
AUPRC alongside every single AUROC, validates on a temporally distinct COVID-era cohort, and then validates
on eight individual held-out hospitals.** It is in the minority on the two deficits that matter most.

**And reporting both metrics is exactly how it produces the most useful finding in it.** Across the eight
held-out hospitals, **AUROC ranges 0.86 to 0.91 — a spread of 0.05 — while AUPRC ranges 0.35 to 0.52, a
spread of 0.17, which is nearly 50% in relative terms.** **The metric that looks stable across hospitals is
the uninformative one, and the metric that decides whether the alarms are worth answering varies by half.**
A reader given only the AUROCs would conclude the model transports well. **It is only visible because they
printed both, and it is the argument for the reporting standard in a single pair of numbers.**

**What an AUPRC of 0.35 means operationally is worth saying plainly: at the worst of the eight hospitals,
the precision-recall performance is such that a usable sensitivity will come with a large majority of alarms
being false.** For a tool whose stated purpose is "to prompt closer nephrology evaluation" that may be
acceptable; for anything with a cost attached it would not be.

**The framing is appropriately modest and matters.** The abstract calls the decision to start renal
replacement therapy "highly complex and subjective" and positions the model as a **triage** tool to prompt
evaluation or earlier fluid and haemodynamic optimisation — **not as a recommendation to initiate.** A model
that predicts a decision rather than a physiological event is predicting what clinicians will do, and
naming the target as "documented RRT initiation" rather than "need for RRT" is the honest label.

**The one deficit it does not fix is the one its own genre is worst at: there is no net benefit or
decision-curve analysis.** Two days ago that was present in 3 of 14 studies. **Four of five reporting
deficits addressed and the fifth left, which is a fair summary.**

23 hours and 43 minutes.** The three-band classification this archive has used — stall at zero, moderate at
+2,105 to +7,120, burst at +23,000 to +26,000 — **has no band for 92. It is a fourth category: a
near-stall.** For scale, the last three days were **+15,011, +2,314 and +92.**

**And the decomposition says exactly where the near-stall is.** **`MED` reads 41,405,924, which is identical
to all three of yesterday's readings** — MEDLINE, which is 84% of the database, **has taken nothing in

**This is the fifth day in a row on which the day's whole-database movement decomposes exactly onto one or
two named sources**, and the first on which MEDLINE is the one that is flat.

### Today's index date holds 61 records, and the calendar does not explain it

**`FIRST_IDATE:2026-11-22` returns 61 records in total at 14:04**, against **7,900** for 21 November. **Both
scoped sweeps for today returned zero: none of those 61 records is in this archive's scope.** The brief is
therefore built from 21 November's index date.

**Today is a Sunday, and this archive's notes forbid the obvious inference.** The 26 October entry records
that **"Sunday is quiet" was retired on 18 October and contradicted again on 25 October, when a Sunday took
10,915 records**, and concludes: **"Stop proposing them: batch size is not a function of the calendar. Four
attempts to make it one have failed."**

**Today is the fifth observation in that family and it confirms the retirement rather than reopening it.**
Two Sundays, **10,915 and 61 — a 179-fold difference.** **No calendar explanation is offered here, and the
61 is recorded as a fact about today rather than about Sundays.**

### The subtraction bound is available, and it is the largest yet

**The 21 November bucket read 7,646 at 14:21 yesterday and reads 7,900 at 14:04:53 today — a gain of 254.
The whole database gained 92 across the same interval. No source shed records: `MED` flat at zero, `PMC`
+31, `PPR` +61, everything else unchanged**, so the precondition recorded on 14 November holds.

**254 − 92 = 162. At least 162 records added to the 21 November bucket overnight were already in the
database and were redated into it.** That is the largest bound this archive has computed, and the cleanest:
**both the bucket and the whole are read within about a minute of each other inside each pass, so the
instant-mixing that compromised the 20 November bound does not apply.**

**The other three buckets did not move at all:** 18 November 6,652, 19 November 4,063 and 20 November 15,610
are identical to yesterday's readings. **So on a day when MEDLINE took nothing, the only bucket that moved
is the most recent completed one, and it moved by more than the whole database grew.**

### The end-of-run reading is identical, and today that carries no information at all

**End of run at 14:06:23: every number identical to 14:03:50** — whole 49,298,729, all eleven sources,
residual zero, and all five buckets unchanged. The gap is **153 seconds.**

**Yesterday's test would call that a stall, and today it cannot, for a reason yesterday's test itself
supplies.** Yesterday the index was measured at roughly **2.6 records per minute**, and at that rate
153 seconds predicts about 6 or 7 records of movement, so zero would be informative. **But today's whole-day
gain is 92 records in 23 hours 43 minutes, which is 0.065 records per minute — a rate forty times lower.**
**At 0.065 per minute, 153 seconds predicts 0.17 records. Observing zero is exactly what an inactive index
and a barely-active one both produce, so the reading distinguishes nothing.**

**That sharpens yesterday's rule rather than restating it. Yesterday's conclusion was that a within-run
window needs to be about ten minutes to mean anything. Today's correction is that the required window is not
a constant — it depends on the day's rate, and the day's rate varies by a factor of forty.** At today's
rate even a fifteen-minute window would predict a single record, so **no within-run comparison available on
a day like this could demonstrate a stall.**

**RULE: before reporting a within-run reading as a stall, compute what the day's own rate predicts for that
window. If the prediction is under about three records, the comparison has no power and should be reported
as having none.** Today's has none, and that is what is reported.
