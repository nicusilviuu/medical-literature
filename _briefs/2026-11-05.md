## 1. The definitive absence of a subgroup: TTM and TTM2, 2,789 patients, no heterogeneity

**[Differentiated effect of hypothermia versus normothermia after cardiac arrest by neurological status and
clinical correlates of ischemic burden](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06414-x)** · *Critical Care* ·
**published 2 November 2026** · open access

**A secondary analysis of both TTM trials together — all 2,789 adult out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients
from their primary analyses**, median age 65 (IQR 56–73), 79.9% male. **The question is the one the field has
been asking since the trials were neutral: is there a subgroup that benefits from 33 °C?** The method is
deliberately heavy: **causal forests and Bayesian additive regression trees to estimate individualised
treatment effects**, plus a treatment-effect-modifier variable importance analysis to rank candidate
modifiers. From the abstract:

> Variation in six-month mortality ITEs estimated from ischemic burden correlates and initial neurological
> findings was minimal, and no significant heterogeneity of treatment effect was identified across all methods
> and analysis populations. In the pooled analysis, estimated conditional mean mortality risk difference ranged
> from - 1.2% to 2.1% in causal forest (p = 0.99), and from - 0.5% to 1.5% in BART, with posterior
> probabilities for hypothermia benefit ranging from 42% to 53%.

**Read the numbers rather than the conclusion.** **Across the entire patient population, the estimated
individualised mortality risk difference spans 3.3 percentage points in the causal forest and 2.0 in the
BART** — **that is the total range, from the patient predicted to benefit most to the one predicted to be
harmed most.** And **the posterior probability of benefit runs from 42% to 53%, straddling the coin flip.**
Overall mortality was **49.4% at 33 °C against 47.9% at controlled normothermia, a difference of 1.5 points in
the wrong direction.**

**This is the third consecutive brief in which a paper constructs a subgroup story and then refuses it** —
after the organ-failure score revision and the fluid-after-echocardiography emulation on 3 November. **In all
three the refusal is the contribution.** **Here it is the strongest of the three, because the analysis was
built specifically to find heterogeneity and used the methods best suited to finding it.**

**My reading.** **The honest summary is that the search is now close to exhausted on these data.** Two
randomised trials, 2,789 patients, two modern causal machine-learning methods, a prespecified modifier-ranking
step, and the range of individualised effects is smaller than the noise in any single trial. **A clinician
hoping that the cold-temperature subgroup is out there and merely unidentified should read the 42-to-53%
posterior band as the answer: on these variables, in this population, there is nothing to find.** **What that
does not exclude is a modifier nobody measured** — and the authors' phrasing, *"available clinical correlates
of ischemic burden or initial neurological findings"*, is carefully limited to what the trials recorded.
**It is an absence of evidence within a well-specified search space, which is a much stronger claim than an
ordinary null.**

## 2. What case-enriched development does to absolute risk

**[Temporal Validation of Machine-Learning Models for Predicting Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation After Valve
or Coronary Artery Bypass Surgery](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11295554/v1)** · **preprint, Research
Square** · **posted 3 November 2026** · **not peer reviewed**

**A case-enriched retrospective development cohort of 347 patients with 189 events — 54.5% — frozen and
applied to a prospective temporal cohort of 167 consecutive patients with 44 events, 26.3%.** Eleven
classifiers, repeated stratified cross-validation with fold-contained preprocessing, then discrimination,
calibration, decision-curve net benefit and six-month prognosis. From the abstract:

> LAVI was the strongest perioperative predictor (adjusted odds ratio per SD, 3.19; 95% CI, 1.66–6.12;
> P < 0.001). Random forest had the highest internal AUROC (0.827; 95% CI, 0.783–0.870), whereas the frozen
> preoperative ridge model performed best temporally (AUROC, 0.723; 95% CI, 0.639–0.808). The case-enriched
> development sample caused absolute-risk overprediction (mean predicted 0.442 vs observed 0.263; integrated
> calibration index [ICI], 0.173); intercept-only recalibration improved ICI to 0.048.

**Three findings, each of which this archive has a rule for and none of which is usually demonstrated this
cleanly.**

**First, case enrichment is a calibration defect with a known size.** **A development cohort at 54.5% event
rate applied to a population at 26.3% predicted a mean risk of 0.442 against an observed 0.263 — overstated by
a factor of 1.68.** **Intercept-only recalibration cut the integrated calibration index by 72%, from 0.173 to
0.048.** **That is the same shape as the perioperative infection audit published here on 1 November**, where
calibration-in-the-large of −1.02 meant predicted odds 2.8 times observed and common recalibration fixed it
almost entirely. **Two independent papers in five days showing that the defect is in the intercept and the
ranking is sound.**

**Second, the internally best model lost.** **Random forest at 0.827 internally; the frozen preoperative ridge
model at 0.723 temporally — and the ridge model is the one that transported.** **This is the transportability
finding the archive logged on 2 November, now with a head-to-head: eleven classifiers ranked one way inside
the development sample and another way under temporal shift.** **The authors predicted this in advance**, and
say so: they hypothesised that a chronic-substrate marker would transport better than perioperative stress
variables and that regularised models would generalise better than complex ones. **A preprint that states its
hypothesis about generalisation and then confirms it is doing something different from one that reports the
best number it found.**

**Third, the substrate marker is the transportable signal.** **Left atrial volume index, an adjusted odds
ratio of 3.19 per standard deviation with a log-symmetric interval of 1.66 to 6.12.** **Chronic atrial
remodelling predicts postoperative atrial fibrillation better than the perioperative insult does** — which is
mechanistically unsurprising and operationally useful, because it is measurable before the operation.

**Two cautions.** **The six-month prognostic claim rests on eight events: atrial fibrillation recurrence in
7 of 43 against 1 of 112, an odds ratio of 21.58 with no interval given**, and a composite hazard ratio of
3.22 whose interval runs from 1.00 to 10.34 — a 10.3-fold span with a lower bound exactly at unity and a
stated p of 0.050. **Nothing there is established.** **And this is a preprint**; the Platt scaling fitted in
the temporal cohort is labelled exploratory by the authors, correctly, since fitting a recalibration in the
cohort used to evaluate it is circular.

**My reading.** **The paper's own conclusion is the right one and unusually modest for a machine-learning
preprint:** the preoperative ridge model *"requires recalibration and independent multicenter validation
before deployment."* **What the archive should carry is the general result rather than the model:
case-enriched development inflates absolute risk predictably, intercept recalibration fixes it, and the
algorithm that wins on internal cross-validation is not the one to deploy.**

## 3. Early warning scores at ICU discharge: twenty studies, seventeen positive, no trials

**[The relationship between early warning scores at intensive care unit discharge and risk of intensive care
unit readmission in adult and pediatric populations: a scoping
review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03211-6)** · *Canadian Journal of Anesthesia* · **published
2 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**7,977 records, 4,824 after de-duplication, 58 full texts, 20 included studies** — 14 adult and 6 paediatric,
summing exactly. Three databases searched to 14 December 2025 with a medical librarian, screening and
extraction in duplicate with third-reviewer adjudication, QUADAS-2 applied. From the abstract:

> Of the 20 studies, 17 reported a significant association between higher EWS values at or near ICU discharge
> and increased risk of unplanned ICU readmission, while three found no significant association. Across
> studies, readmission rates and EWS thresholds varied, and there was considerable heterogeneity in
> populations, settings, and outcome time frames. … All were observational (retrospective or prospective) or
> pre-post intervention designs; no randomized controlled trials were identified.

**The framing matters more than the count.** **Seventeen of twenty is 85% positive, and it is 85% positive
for an association nobody should doubt: patients who look sicker on the day they leave intensive care come
back more often.** **The question that would change practice is not whether the score is associated with
readmission but whether using the score to decide discharge readiness reduces readmission — and the review
answers that explicitly: no randomised trials exist.** **Twenty observational studies and zero trials is the
finding.**

**Two things the review does well.** **It names the three negative studies rather than burying them**, and
**it reports that risk of bias was most often high or unclear in patient selection and index-test blinding** —
which are precisely the two domains that manufacture a positive association between a severity score and a
severity-driven outcome. **The authors draw the right inference: the evidence base "limits causal inference."**

**One scope note.** **The review excludes patients discharged anywhere other than a ward and excludes
interfacility transfer** — a reasonable restriction that also removes the patients for whom a discharge-readiness
score would matter most, those going home or to another hospital directly from intensive care.

**My reading.** **The practical conclusion the authors reach — that early warning scores "may serve as useful
adjuncts to clinical judgement" — is as far as the data go, and it is a weaker claim than the tools are
usually sold with.** **A score that is associated with readmission at a published threshold in one unit does
not transport to another**, and this archive has spent the week accumulating exactly that lesson from
calibration and temporal-validation studies. **The 1 to 12% global readmission rate the review opens with is
itself the warning: a six-fold spread in the outcome across settings means the threshold cannot be shared.**

## 4. One index, two cohorts, same day: a 63-fold difference in size and the same hazard ratio

**[Association Between the Endothelial Activation and Stress Index and All-Cause Mortality in Critically Ill
Patients with Stage 4 Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11595897/v1)**
and **[Association between the endothelial activation and stress index and all-cause mortality in patients
receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11570748/v1)** · **both
preprints, Research Square, both posted 3 November 2026** · **neither peer reviewed**

**Two preprints on the same biomarker, from the same database, posted on the same day, with near-identical
titles — and they are not duplicates.** EASIX is lactate dehydrogenase times creatinine divided by platelet
count, three routine bedside values. One study is in **9,766 patients with stage 4 cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic
syndrome**; the other in **155 patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation**. **Both drawn from MIMIC-IV,
one with external validation in eICU.**

| | Stage 4 CKM | ECMO support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patients | **9,766** | **155** |
| Median EASIX | **2.1** (IQR 1.0–5.0) | **12.14** (IQR 4.04–55.94) |
| Primary endpoint | 30-day mortality, 2,278 (23.3%) | 28-day mortality, 80 (51.6%) |
| HR per doubling | **1.243** (1.182–1.306) at day 1, falling to 1.131 (1.079–1.185) by day 30 | **1.169** (1.070–1.277) |
| Highest vs lowest group | Q4 vs Q1 **1.660** (1.273–2.164) | T3 vs T1 **3.347** (1.750–6.403) |
| External validation | 1,103 patients, 173 deaths (15.7%), HR **1.121** (1.045–1.203) | none |
| Non-linearity | **present**, p 0.002 to < 0.001 | **absent**, p = 0.745 |

**Every proportion reconstructs and every interval is log-symmetric.**

**The convergence is the finding.** **Cohorts differing 63-fold in size, with median index values differing
5.8-fold and baseline mortality differing by a factor of 2.2, return hazard ratios per doubling of 1.243 and
1.169.** **A biomarker whose effect size is that stable across populations that different is measuring
something general** — which for a ratio of lactate dehydrogenase and creatinine over platelets is plausible:
it is a composite of tissue injury, renal function and consumption, and all three track dying.

**The divergence is also informative, and it cuts the other way.** **The large cohort finds a non-linear
association that attenuates with time since admission** — the hazard ratio falls from 1.243 at day one to
1.131 at day thirty, with a formally estimated decay — **while the 155-patient ECMO cohort finds a
monotonically linear association with no decay.** **At 155 patients and 80 deaths the second study cannot
detect either non-linearity or time-varying effect, so its "linear and constant" is an absence of power
rather than a contrary finding.** **The two papers would be read as disagreeing, and they do not disagree.**

**And the large study's own numbers argue against the use it proposes.** **E-value 1.40, with 1.29 for the
confidence bound** — meaning **an unmeasured confounder associated with both EASIX and death by a risk ratio
of only 1.40 would explain the whole thing away.** **That is a very low bar in a critically ill cohort where
illness severity is associated with everything**; compare the fluid emulation published here on 3 November,
whose E-value was 2.60. **Quartiles 2 and 3 did not differ significantly from quartile 1**, so the signal is
confined to the top quartile, which is also where unmeasured severity concentrates.

**My reading.** **EASIX is cheap, built from three values every ICU already has, and reproducibly associated
with death. None of that makes it useful.** **The question for any such index is what it adds to the severity
scores already recorded**, and neither preprint answers it: there is no comparison with SOFA, no incremental
discrimination, no decision-curve analysis. **The ECMO study's claim that it "can serve as an auxiliary tool
for early risk stratification" rests on 155 patients with no external validation and no incremental
comparison.** **What the pair genuinely demonstrates is methodological: the same database, the same index and
the same analytic recipe will generate a publishable association in almost any critically ill subpopulation
anyone cares to define** — and two appeared on one day.
