## A prediction published here yesterday, falsified overnight

**Yesterday this archive published an inference and a prediction that followed from it. Both are wrong.**

The inference, from two readings of one bucket, was that **the index-date stamping lag is a property of the
source** — preprints stamped same-day, MEDLINE and PMC stamped into the previous day's bucket. The prediction,
written into yesterday's notes, was: *expect today's bucket of 456 to grow by roughly a MEDLINE batch
overnight.*

**It did not grow at all.**

| Bucket | 2 Nov reading | 3 Nov reading | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **2 November** | **456** (MED 0) | **456** (MED **still 0**) | **0** |
| 1 November | 3,897 | **3,527** | **−370** |
| **3 November (today)** | — | **7,687** (MED **5,188**) | — |

**Today's own bucket took a full MEDLINE batch of 5,188 on the day**, decomposing exactly as 5,188 + PMC 1,609
+ preprints 889 + 1 clinical-trial record. **And 2 November still holds not one MEDLINE record** — a calendar
day with zero, between a day before holding 3,342 and a day after holding 5,188. **That is a hole, not a
lag.**

**So the correction is specific.** **What happened on 1 November was that a MEDLINE batch was stamped to
1 November and seen a day later. What happened on 2 November was that no batch was stamped at all. What
happened today is that the batch was stamped same-day.** **Three days, three behaviours.** **The lag is not a
property of the source; it varies day to day, and "MEDLINE and PMC stamp into the previous day's bucket" was
a generalisation from two observations.**

**This is the sixth member of a family of errors this archive has recorded** — inferring a stable rule from a
small number of readings — and the correction is the same one as before, now with a sharper form: **a bucket
reading describes that bucket at that instant. It does not license a rule about how stamping works, and it
does not license a prediction about tomorrow.** The only durable statement remains the one made on 1 November:
**a first-index date is restamped, so a bucket is the current contents of a date label.**

**What does survive, and is worth more than the discarded rule, is restamping caught in motion.** PMC's total
rose 281 overnight — and PMC's count in the 31 October bucket fell from 1,215 to **389**, and in the
1 November bucket from 395 to **29**. **Those two buckets lost 1,192 PMC records between them while PMC's
total went up, and today's bucket holds 1,609 of them.** **Records are being moved forward, not added.**
I cannot close that arithmetic exactly without scanning every bucket, and I am not going to assert a figure
I have not measured.

**Whole database 49,189,209 at 14:05:53, residual zero, up 6,330** on yesterday's end-of-run reading —
MEDLINE +5,176, preprints +874, PMC +281, Bookshelf **−2**, clinical trials +1, the other six unchanged, and
the five changes sum to 6,330 exactly. **Bookshelf's trail is now 5, 8, 9, 6, 4.** Windows fell again:
1–30 September **178,411**; 1–7 October **27,845**; **October month 266,346 — down 974 overnight and 1,383
from the 267,729 this archive published on 1 November.** **Two days of drift on a month-end aggregate is the
clearest possible vindication of Sunday's rule to date every aggregate and say it will not reproduce.**

## 1 and 2. Two numbers that mean less than they look

**These two papers each report a headline figure that is correct and misleading, for different reasons.** The
first reports a probability that its own interval contradicts; the second reports a survival rate conditional
on an event that most patients do not reach.

### 1. Sodium bicarbonate for metabolic acidosis: 72.1%, and a 97.2% that does not add up

**[Sodium bicarbonate therapy for metabolic acidosis in critically ill patients: a Bayesian systematic review
and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08602-3)** ·
*Intensive Care Medicine* · **published 2 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Four randomised trials, 1,544 patients** — a mean of 386 per trial — comparing intravenous sodium
bicarbonate with placebo or usual care in adult ICU patients with metabolic acidosis. MEDLINE, Embase and
CENTRAL from inception to December 2025, updated 2 April 2026. PROSPERO registered. From the abstract:

> Sodium bicarbonate did not show a clear short-term mortality benefit (RR 0.94, 95% CrI 0.73-1.22), with a
> posterior probability of benefit of 72.1%. For RRT initiation, sodium bicarbonate was associated with a
> lower risk (RR 0.74, 95% CrI 0.59-0.98), with a posterior probability of benefit of 97.2%.

**The mortality result is the honest use of a Bayesian analysis and should be read carefully.** **A 72.1%
posterior probability of benefit is what a "non-significant" result looks like when it is stated as what it
is**: rather more likely than not that bicarbonate helps, nowhere near enough to act on. **A frequentist
report of the same data would have given p ≈ 0.64 and the word "no difference", which is a worse description
of the same evidence.**

**The renal result does not add up, and the discrepancy is small but real.** **A 95% equal-tailed credible
interval whose upper bound is 0.98 — below 1 — places more than 97.5% of the posterior below 1 by
construction. The stated posterior probability of benefit is 97.2%, which is less.** **Three explanations are
available and the abstract adjudicates none of them:** the interval may be a highest-density rather than an
equal-tailed interval, in which case the tails are not split evenly; "benefit" may be defined against a
threshold other than no effect, such as a minimal important difference; or both figures may be rounded in
directions that happen to cross. **The credible interval is also asymmetric on the log scale by a factor of
1.24**, which tells us the posterior is skewed and makes the first explanation the most likely — **but it
remains an inference, and a Bayesian paper should state its interval type and its benefit threshold.**

**One limit matters more than the arithmetic.** **Four trials and 1,544 patients is the entire randomised
evidence base for a therapy that has been given for decades.** **And the largest recent bicarbonate trial is
not in it:** this archive published the *JAMA* randomised trial of sodium bicarbonate in in-hospital cardiac
arrest on [16 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-16/) — 913 randomised, null, with alkalosis and
hypernatraemia — **and it appeared on 1 August 2026, four months after this review's updated search closed.**
**That trial is a different indication, so its exclusion is correct**, but a reader holding both now has the
field's two 2026 data points: **nothing for arrest, and probably fewer renal replacement starts for metabolic
acidosis.**

**My reading.** **The actionable content is the renal outcome, and it is the one the authors hedge least.**
Fewer renal replacement starts at a risk ratio of 0.74 is a plausible, mechanistically coherent effect, and
97.2% — whatever its exact derivation — is a high probability. **The mortality finding should not be reported
as a negative result; it should be reported as 72.1%, which is what it is.** The authors' closing sentence is
the right one: *"Further trials should define the patients most likely to benefit."*

### 2. Five-year survival after ECPR: two-thirds, conditional on one in three surviving to discharge

**[Long-Term Survival Following Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation: A Two-Center Five-Year
Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2026.111379)** · *Resuscitation* · **published
2 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**728 patients treated with ECPR for refractory VT/VF out-of-hospital cardiac arrest** at the University of
Minnesota and Charles University, Prague, January 2013 to December 2022. From the abstract:

> Among 728 patients treated with ECPR, 211 (29.0%) survived to discharge; 138 from Minnesota and 73 from
> Prague. Median follow-up was 3.9 years; 68 patients (32%) completed 5 years. No patients were lost to
> follow-up. Among hospital survivors, 76.9% and 67.2% were alive at 1 and 5 years, respectively, with most
> deaths occurring within 90 days of discharge. CPC 1-2 was present in 78.7% at discharge and remained high
> among survivors beyond 1 year (93.4%-94.2%). Five-year survival declined by age from 91.6% for those between
> 18-39 years to 52.9% for >70 years.

**138 plus 73 is the stated 211, and 211 of 728 is 28.98%.** **Now compose the two fractions the abstract
keeps apart.** **29.0% survive to discharge; 67.2% of those are alive at five years; so unconditional
five-year survival from the moment of ECPR is 19.5% — about one in five.** At one year it is 22.3%.
**"Approximately two-thirds alive at 5 years" is true and it is conditional on an event that seven patients
in ten never reach.** **The composed number is the one a patient, a family or a commissioner needs, and it is
not in the abstract.**

**The neurological figures are survivorship, not recovery, and the abstract's verb conceals it.** CPC 1–2 was
**78.7% at discharge** and **93.4% to 94.2% among survivors beyond one year** — and the abstract says it
*"remained high"*. **Nobody's cerebral performance category improved. The patients with worse categories
died**, most of them within 90 days of discharge, which the same sentence tells us. **A proportion rose
because its denominator was selected by mortality, and that is this archive's recorded rule about survivorship
changing a proportion while nothing changes in any patient.**

**Three things the paper does genuinely well.** **No patients were lost to follow-up** across up to nine
years and two countries, which is remarkable and should be said. **The five-year figure rests on 68 of 211
patients — 32.2% — who had reached five years**, the rest being administratively censored by the 2022
enrolment close rather than lost, so Kaplan–Meier estimation is appropriate. **And the age gradient is the
paper's most usable output: 91.6% five-year survival at 18 to 39 against 52.9% over 70, a ratio of 1.73**,
measured in hospital survivors.

**My reading.** **The paper's conclusion — that benefit is durable among selected patients in established
systems — is supported, and every word of that sentence is doing work.** *Selected*: refractory VT/VF,
two high-volume academic centres, 29% reaching discharge. *Established systems*: this is not a generalisable
estimate of what ECPR achieves where it is new. **What the archive should carry forward is the pair of
numbers, not one of them: 29% to discharge, two-thirds of those to five years, 19.5% overall, and almost all
of the survivors neurologically independent.** **The last clause is the reason the first number is worth
pursuing.**

## 3. Three coagulation-fibrinolysis phenotypes in sepsis, and a hazard ratio that grew on validation

**[Trajectories of Coagulation-Fibrinolysis Balance Reveal Hemostatic Heterogeneity in Sepsis: Derivation and
Multicenter Validation](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2994-8962)** · *Thrombosis and Haemostasis* ·
**published 2 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**A single-centre training cohort of 1,672 and a prospective multicentre external validation cohort of
1,393 — 3,065 septic ICU patients in total.** Daily prothrombin time and D-dimer over the first seven days
were put through joint group-based trajectory modelling. From the abstract:

> Joint modeling reveals three phenotypes: Compensate (Class 1, 51.9%; preserved balance), Coupled (Class 2,
> 35.5%; intensive but balanced coagulation-fibrinolysis activation), and Consumptive (Class 3, 12.6%;
> profound coagulation factor consumption with blunted fibrinolysis). The Consumptive phenotype exhibited the
> most severe systemic inflammation, organ failure, and the highest 28-day mortality (52.1%). Multivariable
> analysis confirmed Class 3 as an independent predictor of 28-day mortality in both the training (adjusted
> HR 2.69) and validation cohorts (adjusted HR 3.78).

**The three classes sum to 100.0% exactly.** **The Consumptive phenotype is 12.6% — roughly 211 patients in
training and 176 in validation — and carries 52.1% mortality at 28 days.**

**The reporting defect is the one this archive keeps logging, and here it is at its starkest.** **Two adjusted
hazard ratios are given as bare point estimates: 2.69 and 3.78, with no confidence intervals and no
p-values.** **A primary comparison reported as a number with no precision cannot be weighed at all** — and
this is now the fifth instance in nine days of a named outcome whose result is given without the quantity that
makes it interpretable.

**And the direction of the discrepancy between the two cohorts is the opposite of the usual one.** **A model's
effect estimate almost always shrinks on external validation** — that is what optimism means. **Here it grew
by a factor of 1.41, from 2.69 to 3.78.** **Three readings are available and none can be chosen from the
abstract:** the validation cohort may have a different case mix in which the phenotype separates more
sharply; the adjustment sets may differ between cohorts; or, with roughly 176 Consumptive patients in
validation, **the estimate may simply be imprecise — which is exactly what the missing intervals would tell
us.** **A hazard ratio that rises on validation is not reassuring; it is unexplained.**

**What is genuinely new here.** **The archive has been accumulating ways to read sepsis coagulopathy and now
holds two 2026 proposals that disagree about which measurements matter.** **SIC-2**, routed to the guidelines
watch on [1 November](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-11-01/), **removes the organ-failure score and
relies on platelet count and prothrombin time alone** — a deliberate simplification to a static, two-variable
score. **This paper goes the other way: prothrombin time plus D-dimer, measured daily for seven days, modelled
as trajectories, and reduced to a derived Z-balance score.** **One proposal makes the measurement simpler and
one makes it longitudinal, and they share exactly one variable.**

**My reading.** **The phenotypes are plausible and the Consumptive one is clinically recognisable** — the
patient consuming factors with a blunted fibrinolytic response, which is the classical overt disseminated
intravascular coagulation picture arrived at by a data-driven route. **The external validation in 1,393
prospectively collected patients is a real strength and most trajectory-phenotype papers do not have one.**
**But a prognostic classification whose hazard ratios are published without intervals is not yet usable, and
the Z-balance score needs its discrimination reported before anyone should compute it.** **The practical
question the paper raises and does not answer is whether knowing a patient is Consumptive on day three
changes anything** — there is still no anticoagulant therapy for sepsis with established benefit, a point the
SIC-2 document makes about its own score as well.

## 4. The measured cost of a nerve block: four times the unscheduled contact on day one

**[Perioperative Single-Shot Regional Blocks and Associated Rebound Pain-Related Unscheduled Utilization of
Health Care After Distal Radius Surgery](https://doi.org/10.1177/15589447261496339)** · *Hand* ·
**published 2 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**619 patients having distal radius fracture fixation at one academic centre, 2013 to 2022; 417 included
after exclusions** — minors, inpatient and revision procedures, insufficient records and pathological
fractures, which removed 202 patients, 32.6%. The outcome is **unscheduled healthcare use for pain: emergency
department returns, pain-related phone calls, and messages sent to providers through the electronic health
record.** From the abstract:

> The adjusted relative risk of using unscheduled health care for pain was 4.0 (95% CI, 1.3-12.1; P = .01) on
> postoperative day 1 for patients who received perioperative blocks compared with those who did not.
> Similarly, the adjusted relative risk within 2 days of surgery was 3.9 (95% CI, 1.4-10.5; P = .007) and
> within 3 days was 3.2 (95% CI, 1.4-7.5; P = .008).

**This is the sixth rebound-pain item in this archive and the first that measures a system consequence.** The
earlier entries describe the phenomenon, its mechanism and its management; **this one counts what it costs in
contacts.** **A single-shot block, given to improve the first twelve hours, quadruples the chance that the
patient contacts the service unscheduled on day one.**

**Three cautions, and the first is the largest.** **This is observational, and the decision to place a block
is not random.** The adjustment set named in the abstract is age, gender, mechanism, time to surgery and
intraoperative local anaesthetic — **and it does not include fracture severity, baseline pain, or the
surgeon's expectation of postoperative pain.** **Patients who get a block are plausibly the ones expected to
hurt more**, which would produce this result with no rebound effect at all. **Confounding by indication here
runs in the same direction as the finding, and nothing in the abstract excludes it.**

**Second, the intervals say how few events there are.** **The day-one interval spans 9.3-fold, from 1.3 to
12.1**, narrowing to 7.5-fold and 5.4-fold as the window widens and more events accrue. **All three are
log-symmetric, and their implied p-values — 0.015, 0.0081 and 0.0066 — sit close to the stated 0.01, 0.007 and
0.008.** **A relative risk of 4.0 whose lower bound is 1.3 establishes a direction, not a magnitude.**

**Third, the three estimates are not three findings.** They are nested cumulative windows over the same
patients: day 1, within 2 days, within 3 days. **The apparent attenuation from 4.0 to 3.2 is the dilution of
a day-one signal by two quieter days, not a decaying effect**, and reporting it as three results makes the
evidence look broader than it is.

**My reading.** **The direction is almost certainly right and the magnitude is almost certainly overstated.**
Rebound pain is real, this archive has five prior entries establishing it, and a patient whose block wears
off at 2 a.m. on the first night will call somebody. **What this paper adds is that the call is measurable and
frequent enough to show up in a 417-patient series** — and the useful response is not to withhold blocks but
to warn the patient when the block will fail and to have oral analgesia in their hand before it does.
**The study's own framing supports that: the outcome is phone calls and messages, which is to say a failure of
anticipatory guidance as much as a failure of analgesia.**

## 5. Wearable arrest detection: sensitivity reported tightly, false alarms across four orders of magnitude

**[Wearable-based automated cardiac arrest detection and alerting: A systematic
review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2026.111378)** · *Resuscitation* · **published
2 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**6,344 records screened, 13 publications included** — 0.2% — with participant numbers from **18 to 1,423**,
a 79-fold range. Wristbands in nine, photoplethysmography as the primary sensor in nine, with accelerometry,
mechanical plethysmography, electrocardiography, sound and a nonlinear resonant circuit making up the rest.
QUADAS-2 applied; PRISMA followed; protocol on OSF. From the abstract:

> Sensitivity ranged between 43.8 and 100%, with ten studies reporting a sensitivity of ≥ 90%. False positives
> ranged between multiple per day and one per twenty-one user-years. Wearable devices have demonstrated
> promising sensitivity for detecting cardiac arrest. However, (external) validation and real-life data are
> scarce, and the sensitivity-false alarm trade-off requires further investigation.

**The two reported ranges are not comparable in kind, and that is the whole finding.** **Sensitivity spans
43.8% to 100% — a factor of 2.3 — and ten of thirteen studies cluster above 90%.** **The false-alarm rate
spans "multiple per day" to "one per twenty-one user-years."** **Taken at one alarm a day against one in
twenty-one years, that is a ratio of about 7,700; at two a day, about 15,300.** **Four orders of magnitude,
in the quantity that determines whether a device can be worn.**

**And the asymmetry is the wrong way round for deployment.** **A device that detects 95% of arrests and cries
wolf twice a day is unusable and will be taken off.** **A device that detects 95% and alarms once in two
decades is a public health intervention.** **The literature reports the first number precisely and the second
as a phrase.** **This archive's rule applies and extends: discrimination is a statement about individuals and
calibration a statement about groups — and for an alerting device a third quantity governs, the false-alarm
burden per user-year, which no amount of sensitivity can compensate for.**

**Two further observations.** **Ten of thirteen studies reporting sensitivity above 90% in a field of
device-development studies is itself a datum about the literature**, not only about the devices; the authors
say external validation and real-life data are scarce, which is the same observation from the other side.
**And the sensor mix tells you how early this is**: nine of thirteen rely on photoplethysmography, an optical
pulse signal that motion, perfusion and skin tone all degrade, and only one uses electrocardiography.

**My reading.** **The honest summary is that nobody yet knows the false-alarm rate of wearable arrest
detection, and that is the only number that matters next.** **Sensitivity is close to solved in development
cohorts and unvalidated in life.** **This touches, without testing, a prediction this archive recorded on
[29 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-29/)** — that PROPER's absolute postoperative atrial
fibrillation rates will not be comparable with PALACS's, because PROPER adds wearable electrocardiographic
patch monitoring where the guideline trials used routine surveillance. **The question there is detection of
arrhythmia, not of arrest, so this review does not settle it** — **but it supplies the general mechanism:
a wearable changes what is detected, and its performance in a development cohort tells you very little about
what it will report in the field.**
