## 1. Both arms have a median of 25 days

TEACH is a cluster randomized type 2 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial in **12 Intermountain Health
hospitals** in Utah and Idaho, enrolling intubated, mechanically ventilated ICU patients aged 16 or over
from 1 December 2022 to 31 July 2025. Telehealth hospitals received **real-time oversight from telecritical
care nurses and respiratory therapists** for adherence to coordinated spontaneous awakening and breathing
trials, on top of a standard audit and feedback implementation. Control hospitals received the audit and
feedback alone.

**4,544 patients at telehealth hospitals, 6,438 at control hospitals, and 1,789 preimplementation common
baseline patients.** Median age 62, 37.8% female.

**The adherence co-primary is unambiguous.** Baseline adherence of 43.9% rose to **90.3%** at telehealth
hospitals and **77.6%** at control hospitals; odds ratio for the change in telehealth against the change in
control **2.87**, absolute difference **11.4% (95% CI 8.8% to 14.3%)**.

**Two things belong next to that number.** First, the test was **one-sided by prespecification** — the paper
reports a "1-sided 95% lower confidence limit" of 2.44 — which is a legitimate choice that has to be named,
because it is not the same evidential bar as a two-sided test. Second, **the control arm improved by 33.7
percentage points on its own**, from 43.9% to 77.6%, with nothing but audit and feedback. **The telehealth
increment is 11.4 points on top of that**, so the comparator is not inaction but a cheap intervention that
did most of the work.

**The ventilator-free-days co-primary needs reading slowly.** From a common baseline median of 24 days
(IQR 0 to 26), the median rose to **25 days at telehealth hospitals and 25 days at control hospitals.**
**The medians are identical.** The difference lives in the distribution — IQR 10 to 27 against 1 to 27 —
and is reported as a proportional-odds ratio of **1.21 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.45, P = .04)** with an absolute
difference of **1.02 days (95% CI 0.05 to 1.96)**.

**A lower confidence bound of 0.05 days is 72 minutes.** The trial's own framing, that telehealth
"increased ventilator-free days", rests on an interval whose lower end is an hour and a bit, from a model
on a distribution whose medians coincide. **The adherence result is strong and the outcome result is
marginal, and those are different claims.** Where the real effect sits inside that interval is not
established by this trial.

## 2. Nineteen thousandths of an AUROC, at p below 0.001

SOFA-2 was introduced in October 2025. This study compares it with the original score, called SOFA-1 here,
in older critically ill patients: **26,104 patients from MIMIC-IV version 3.1 as the primary cohort and
53,697 from the eICU Collaborative Research Database as independent validation.** Ages 65 to 89, because
both databases top-code ages above 89; those patients were included in sensitivity analyses.

**In MIMIC-IV, SOFA-2's AUROC for ICU mortality was 0.749 against SOFA-1's 0.730, P < 0.001**, and the paper
itself calls the absolute difference modest. The pattern was largely replicated in eICU-CRD, and in fully
adjusted models SOFA-2 gave significantly higher C-indices for the total score and for the respiratory and
haemostasis domains.

**The number to hold onto is 0.019 of AUROC on 26,104 patients.** At that sample size a difference of
almost any size clears p < 0.001, so the p-value carries no information about whether the gain matters at
the bedside. **The paper is careful about this and the summary of it elsewhere probably will not be.**

**The operating-characteristic trade is the practically useful finding, and it points both ways.** SOFA-2
had **higher sensitivity and negative predictive value**; SOFA-1 had **higher specificity, positive
predictive value and positive likelihood ratios.** So the new score is better at ruling out and the old one
better at ruling in. **A score that is more sensitive and less specific is not an improved score, it is a
differently calibrated one**, and which you want depends on whether the cost of a missed deterioration
exceeds the cost of a false alarm.

**This archive has now seen SOFA-2 twice in one week from opposite directions** — on Monday as the outcome
criterion a sepsis fluid-trajectory study was built on, and today as the instrument under test.

## 3. A lactate clearance of 15.9% against 0.7%, and the caveat that governs it

**93 casualties with penetrating combat abdominal trauma** and ATLS class II to IV haemorrhage, undergoing
emergency laparotomy at **Role 2 forward surgical teams in Ukraine in 2022 to 2023.** Retrospective,
clinician-selected: ketamine-based total intravenous anaesthesia in 45, or sodium oxybate with
analgesic-dose ketamine in 48.

The regimens as stated: the ketamine group had 1.0 to 2.0 mg/kg induction and **1.5 to 2.0 mg/kg/h**
infusion. The sodium oxybate group had the same induction, then a 50 to 70 mg/kg loading dose with repeated
25 to 50 mg/kg boluses every 40 to 60 minutes, and ketamine at **0.2 mg/kg/h** for analgesia.

**Primary endpoint, the time-weighted average deficit in mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg: 3.86
(3.05 to 4.71) against 4.58 (3.61 to 5.69) mmHg, P = .004**, surviving a regression sensitivity analysis
including noradrenaline exposure and transfusion volume. Noradrenaline was used as often in both groups,
24.4% against 29.2%, but among those who received it **total exposure was 7.05 against 14.75 micrograms per
kilogram, P < .001.**

**Then: lactate clearance 15.88 plus or minus 3.20 per cent against 0.71 plus or minus 8.19 per cent,
P < .001.** That is a very large difference, and the dispersions are worth as much attention as the means —
**a standard deviation of 8.19 around a mean of 0.71 in the comparison group against 3.20 around 15.88 in
the treated group**, a two and a half fold difference in spread alongside a control mean of essentially zero
clearance in haemorrhaging patients receiving transfusion.

**The authors name the confounder that governs the whole paper, and it is decisive: they cannot distinguish
the effect of sodium oxybate from that of the reduced ketamine exposure that came with it.** The
intervention group received roughly a tenth of the ketamine. **So this is not a comparison of two hypnotics
at equal analgesia; it is a comparison of two whole regimens**, and the honest conclusion is the one they
draw — that prospective work is needed to separate the two. They also note that the primary endpoint rests
on **intermittent non-invasive blood pressure measurement**, which limits the precision of a time-weighted
average by construction.

## 4. A fifth less stroke, a half more bleeding, in a gap the guidelines leave open

**195,619 carotid endarterectomies in the Vascular Quality Initiative, January 2003 to September 2024**,
stratified by preoperative antiplatelet regimen and compared with aspirin alone as reference, using
propensity scores and inverse probability of treatment weighting, adjusted for intraoperative protamine use
and drain placement.

The paper states the gap it is filling: **current vascular society recommendations are limited, dual therapy
is recommended in acutely symptomatic patients for secondary stroke prevention, and for asymptomatic
patients there is no clear recommendation** though aspirin alone is common practice.

Distribution: 53% aspirin only, 30% dual therapy, 11% no antiplatelet, 6% clopidogrel only, 1% dual therapy
with an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor. Unadjusted in-hospital stroke or death: 1.3%, 1.2%, 1.7%, 1.2% and
1.6%.

**Adjusted, against aspirin alone, in-hospital stroke or death was lower with clopidogrel alone, RR 0.81
(95% CI 0.67 to 0.97), and with dual therapy, RR 0.79 (0.72 to 0.87).** In the asymptomatic patients — the
group with no recommendation — **clopidogrel alone gave RR 0.69 (0.52 to 0.90) and dual therapy RR 0.73
(0.64 to 0.83).** In symptomatic patients, where dual therapy is already recommended, the result was only a
trend: **RR 0.88 (0.76 to 1.00).**

**And the cost is explicit: bleeding complications were higher with dual therapy, RR 1.49 (1.35 to 1.66).**

**The shape of this is worth stating.** The benefit was clearest exactly where guidance is absent, and
weakest where guidance already exists. **Registry data with propensity weighting cannot establish that
switching an asymptomatic patient to dual therapy would produce that benefit** — regimen choice is made by
clinicians who know things the covariates do not — but on a question where the alternative is no evidence
at all, a 195,619-patient trade of a fifth less stroke or death against half again as much bleeding is a
usable starting point.

## 5. Plus or minus three centimetres of water

A prospective physiological validation of the Mixed Integer Multi-Effort method, which estimates
inspiratory muscle pressure from standard ventilator signals, against the oesophageal pressure reference,
in adults with acute hypoxic respiratory failure **within 48 hours of their first transition from controlled
ventilation to pressure support.**

**62 independent model-fitting windows, 1,240 breaths, 18 patients.** Median inspiratory muscle pressure
7.5 cmH2O (IQR 6.0 to 9.1) by the method against 6.9 (5.9 to 8.9) by oesophageal pressure.

**Breath by breath: mean difference 0.4 cmH2O, limits of agreement −3.4 to +4.2.** Averaged within each
recording set: **mean difference 0.4, limits of agreement −2.2 to +3.0.**

**Those limits are the result, not the mean difference.** A bias of 0.4 cmH2O is negligible and a
single-breath agreement band of nearly eight cmH2O wide is not, on a quantity whose clinically interesting
range runs from about 5 to about 15. Averaging over breaths narrows the band to about five, which is the
paper's own point: this is a tool for **longitudinal** assessment rather than breath-level decisions.

Discrimination was good for both extremes — **AUROC 0.83 for high effort and 0.88 for low** — and the
comparison against end-expiratory occlusion pressure splits: **similar for detecting low effort, with
occlusion pressure better for detecting high effort.** The authors ask for validation in broader
populations and a dedicated assessment of convergence and computational performance before bedside
real-time use, which is the right order.

## Also in today's sweep

**A burns study where the exposure is perfectly separated by heart rate, which makes the comparison
uninterpretable as a treatment effect** (`10.3390/ebj7040055`, European Burn Journal). 31 mechanically
ventilated burn patients with vasopressor-dependent sepsis, mean burn area 40.2%, ICU mortality 35.5%.
Landiolol went to 41.9%, and **every treated patient was in sinus tachycardia at 111 to 140 beats per
minute while every untreated patient had a heart rate below 95.** No outcome differed, all
multiplicity-adjusted p values at or above 0.97. **With zero overlap in the indication, a null result is
what the design can produce and nothing else**; the paper calls itself preliminary and exploratory, which
is accurate.

**A natural experiment on resident absence** (`10.1016/j.spinee.2026.11.023`, The Spine Journal), using the
2024 South Korean resident walkout: 258 consecutive elective thoracolumbar or lumbar operations by one
surgeon, 129 before and 129 during. **Nothing differed after adjustment** — operative time +6.3 minutes,
blood loss +27.0 mL, stay +1.6 days, composite complications OR 0.99 — and no sensitivity analysis
dissented. **But ASA class 3 patients were 46.5% of the with-resident period and 25.6% of the without, and
the surgeon's operative volume fell 30%.** A null on a cohort that got healthier and smaller is weaker
evidence than a null on an unchanged one.

## The instruments

### A closing parenthesis, and the second time the title fallback has saved a page

**FISSH, published in yesterday's brief, came back in today's sweep and the dedupe log caught it by TITLE,
not by identifier.** That should not happen for a paper whose DOI is on the page.

**The cause, verified before being reported.** The DOI pattern excluded `)` outright, so that it would stop
at a markdown link's closing bracket. **Lancet-family DOIs contain a parenthesised year.** So
`10.1016/s2213-2600(26)00347-4` was harvested as `10.1016/s2213-2600(26` and could never match the index's
DOI. **Four distinct Lancet-family DOIs already in this archive were affected, and today's sweep alone
holds four more papers of that form.**

**The pattern now allows parentheses and trims a trailing one only when it is unbalanced**, which keeps the
behaviour the exclusion was there for. Verified on four cases, including the markdown-link case:

| Input | Harvested |
| --- | --- |
| `https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-2600(26)00347-4"` | `10.1016/s2213-2600(26)00347-4` |
| `[x](https://doi.org/10.1234/abc)` | `10.1234/abc` |
| a backticked `10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155834` | unchanged |
| `see 10.1016/s0140-6736(26)01554-0.` | `10.1016/s0140-6736(26)01554-0` |

FISSH now enters the log by identifier. **This is the same shape as the 22 November backtick hole: a
character excluded for a markdown reason that legitimately occurs inside DOIs.** Two of the three identifier
faults found in this archive have been punctuation excluded for the sake of the page's own syntax.

### Yesterday's partial-day question is answered: there is no stable figure

**25 November, re-read today: the title route gives 155 against yesterday's 144 at about 14:05, so that day
was 92.9% complete at that hour. The same figure for 24 November was 10.7%.** Two consecutive days, the
same clock time, **a nine-fold difference in completeness.**

**So the 10.7% cannot be used as a correction factor, and yesterday's two disagreeing measurements were not
measuring a constant.** The whole-database count for 25 November tells the same story and now **agrees**
with the scoped route: 6,431 at 14:05 against 6,730 at 14:09 today, 95.6% complete, against the title
route's 92.9%. **Yesterday those two measures differed by a factor of two for 24 November; today they are
within three points for 25 November.** The disagreement was a property of one day.

**Today's own partial day returned 418 title-route records at 14:04, against 144 and 17 on the two previous
days at the same hour — a 24-fold range.** The whole-day count reads **17,083**, against 6,431 and 1,615.
**Batch timing is no more a function of the clock than batch size is of the calendar**, which is the same
conclusion the calendar rules reached on 26 October and the reason they stay retired.
