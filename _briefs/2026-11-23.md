## The route, and what it does not do

**Today's sweep returned 39 in-scope records and 33 of them carry no abstract.** Not a short abstract — no
`abstractText` field at all. The six that have one are Research Square preprints. **This morning that made
the day close to unreportable.**

**One record's search entry said `inEPMC=Y` and `isOpenAccess=Y`.** That is the search index stating that
the full text is in Europe PMC while depositing no abstract of its own. **So the full text was fetched
directly** — `https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13788852/fullTextXML` — **and its
`<abstract>` element yielded 1,836 characters.**

**That route is now `claude/tools/epmc_fulltext.py`**, in the repository rather than in a memory, taking a
PMC identifier, a DOI to resolve, or a whole sweep file.

### Measured on both populations, because one success is not a route

| Population | Attempted | Abstract recovered | Why not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Today's abstract-free PMC records | **33** | **15 (45%)** | 15 × `fullTextXML` HTTP 500; 2 full texts with no `<abstract>` element |
| This archive's standing named gaps | **6** | **0** | 5 are `inEPMC=N` — not in Europe PMC at all; 1 returns HTTP 500 |

**The counterweight is the more important half.** The named gaps are exactly what this route was wanted for
— the local-anaesthetic maximum-dose scoping review, the PUMA extubation editorial, the frailty assessment
Delphi, the NICE guideline review, the GLP-1 fasting letter — **and it recovered none of them.** Five report
`inEPMC=N`: they are publisher deposits that Europe PMC indexes but does not hold. **A route that works on
open-access PMC deposits and fails on paywalled publisher deposits does not solve the named-gap problem; it
solves a different problem that happened to be today's.**

**The discriminator is free and readable before any fetch: `inEPMC`.** So the rule is to read that field
first rather than fetch blind, which is what the tool does.

**One thing is not characterised and is recorded as not characterised.** Fifteen of the 33 returned
**HTTP 500**, and one of them was retried twice more — **500 on both attempts.** That is weak evidence for a
structural property of those particular deposits rather than a transient service fault, **and it is one
identifier tried three times, which is not the same as the fault being characterised.** The named gap that
also returned 500 is a different record and was tried once. **The yield is 45% today and may be a different
number tomorrow.**

## 1. The right denominator for the fear about ECPR

**[Long-term outcomes and quality of life of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survivors treated with
extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC13788592)** · 406
ECPR-treated patients at one tertiary centre, 2013–2024

> "Among 406 ECPR-treated patients, 63 (16%) were discharged alive. At a median follow-up of 3.1 (IQR
> 0.6–6.9) years, 38/62 (61%) were alive, of whom 31 (82%) had favourable neurological outcome. Only 7 of
> all 406 treated patients (1.7%) were alive with poor neurological outcome. Five-year mortality was higher
> in patients discharged with poor versus favourable neurological status (86% vs. 13%, p < 0.001)... Mild
> cognitive impairment was found in 6/16 survivors and poor sleep quality in 10/17. Anxiety symptoms were
> more frequent among caregivers than survivors (6/18 vs 1/17)."

**Interpretation — the authors chose the right denominator and it is the whole argument.** The standing
objection to extracorporeal CPR is that it manufactures survivors with severe neurological disability.
**The number that answers that objection is not 7 of 38 survivors; it is 7 of all 406 patients treated —
1.7%.** Most papers would report the survivor-based figure. **Putting the denominator at the population
offered the intervention is the correct framing for a question about whether the intervention creates
harm**, and it is in the abstract.

**And the second structural finding is that discharge neurological status is close to deterministic for
long-term survival: five-year mortality 86% against 13%.** A patient leaving hospital with a poor
neurological outcome after ECPR is very unlikely to be alive five years later. **That is bleak and it is
also what makes the 1.7% figure stable rather than a snapshot** — the poor-outcome group does not
accumulate.

**The quality-of-life half of the paper rests on very few people and the numbers should be read that way.**
EQ VAS 70 against 80, described as "preserved and comparable", comes from **17 survivors and 19
caregivers** — out of 406 treated and 63 discharged alive. **No intervals are given, and on 17 people none
would be narrow.** Mild cognitive impairment in 6 of 16 and poor sleep in 10 of 17 are counts, not rates.

**The one asymmetry worth acting on is the one that involves people nobody is following up: anxiety symptoms
in 6 of 18 caregivers against 1 of 17 survivors.** The survivor is the patient; the caregiver is not in
anyone's clinic. **Seven of fifteen previously employed survivors returned to work and all caregivers did**
— so the caregivers are carrying anxiety and working. The conclusion asks for follow-up programmes "for
both survivors and their families", which follows from its own data.

## 2. Thirty-nine per cent of ventilated patients do not respond, and eight predictors collapse to one

**[Determinants of quadriceps responsiveness to neuromuscular electrical stimulation in mechanically
ventilated patients](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC13788745)** · secondary analysis of DETECT
(NCT04722445) · 75 patients

> "Among the 75 patients included in the analysis, 29 (39%) were classified as NMES non-responders (maximum
> stimulation intensity reached without achieving knee extension, with heel lifted: 120 mA)... NMES
> non-responders had greater RF thickness... higher BMI... higher fluid accumulation (6.5 [2–8] vs. 1
> [−1.5−2.5] kg; p < 0.001) and SOFA scores... a larger proportion were diabetic... the sepsis rate was
> also higher... Aminoglycoside exposure (41.4% vs. 4.4%; p < 0.001) and vasopressor dose... were also
> higher in non-responders. After multivariable analysis, only fluid accumulation remained associated with
> NMES non-responsiveness (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.04–1.63; p = 0.02)."

**Interpretation — the headline is the 39% and its consequence for an entire trial literature.** Neuromuscular
electrical stimulation has been tested repeatedly against ICU-acquired weakness with heterogeneous results.
**If 39% of ventilated patients cannot be made to extend a knee at the maximum deliverable intensity, then
a trial that randomises to NMES without verifying a visible contraction is allocating roughly a third of
its intervention arm to a sham.** That is a mechanism for heterogeneity that needs no biology, and it is the
most useful thing here.

**The analytic shape is the second finding and the authors report it cleanly: eight univariable
associations, one survivor.** Rectus femoris thickness, BMI, fluid accumulation, SOFA, diabetes, sepsis,
aminoglycoside exposure and vasopressor dose all separate the groups in univariable comparison, **and after
adjustment only fluid accumulation remains (OR 1.30 per kilogram, 1.04–1.63).** The eight are not eight
mechanisms; they are eight markers of severity and oedema, and adjustment says so.

**Which makes the conclusion's last sentence a mild over-reach, and worth naming.** It states that thickness,
BMI, diabetes, severity, sepsis, aminoglycosides and vasopressor dose "could also decrease quadriceps
responsiveness" — **after reporting that none of them survived adjustment.** The hedge "further research is
needed to determine the actual impact" is doing a lot of work. **The paper's own analysis supports one
determinant.**

**And the surviving one is plausible and actionable: fluid between the electrode and the muscle.** Median
accumulation of 6.5 kg against 1 kg is a large difference in a quantity clinicians already measure and
already try to reduce. **It also predicts that NMES should work better after de-resuscitation than during
it, which is testable.**

**The bound is the sample: 75 patients, 29 non-responders, and a multivariable model that could support
very few terms.** An interval of 1.04 to 1.63 on the one surviving predictor has its lower bound close to
unity.

## 3. AUROC 0.91 and AUPRC 0.05, in the same table

**[Machine learning-based prediction of 30-, 60- and 90-day postoperative mortality and major
complications](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC13788783)** · 17 Hong Kong public hospitals · training
43,601 emergency and 151,016 elective, external validation 16,143 and 37,586

> "In temporal validation, the perioperative full models achieved AUROCs of 0.89, 0.89, and 0.89 for 30-day,
> 60-day, and 90-day mortality after emergency surgery and 0.91, 0.90, and 0.90 after elective surgery.
> Corresponding AUPRCs were 0.27, 0.36, and 0.41 for emergency surgery and 0.05, 0.10, and 0.13 for elective
> surgery... Calibration assessment indicated variable agreement between predicted and observed risks,
> supporting the need for recalibration before clinical implementation... Logistic recalibration improved
> Brier scores and calibration indices across all full and reduced models without changing AUROC or AUPRC."

**Interpretation — this is the starkest version yet of a point this archive has now made on three
consecutive days, and it is in a single pair of numbers: AUROC 0.91 and AUPRC 0.05 for 30-day mortality
after elective surgery.** Thirty-day death after elective surgery is very rare, so the negatives are
overwhelming and easy, which is what an AUROC of 0.91 measures. **An AUPRC of 0.05 says that among the
patients this model flags as highest risk, almost none will die.** A reader shown only the AUROC would
conclude the model is excellent at identifying patients who will die after elective surgery. It is not.

**The emergency-surgery figures show the same mechanism from the other side and confirm it: identical
AUROCs of 0.89 with AUPRCs of 0.27 to 0.41**, five to eight times higher than the elective ones, because
the event is commoner. **The AUROC cannot tell the two settings apart; the AUPRC separates them by a factor
of five.**

**This is the third instance in four days and the reporting quality has been ascending.** On 20 November an
appraisal found AUPRC reported only as a direction. On 22 November a renal-replacement model reported it
alongside every AUROC and revealed a 0.17 spread across hospitals. **Today's reports AUROC, AUPRC, Brier
score, calibration intercept and slope, the integrated calibration index, E50 and E90** — and says in the
abstract that calibration was variable and recalibration is needed before implementation.

**Then it does something the other two did not: it recalibrates and reports that this improved Brier and
calibration "without changing AUROC or AUPRC".** That sentence is a clean experimental demonstration that
**discrimination and calibration are independent properties of a model** — you can fix one without
touching the other. Most papers assert that; this one shows it in its own results.

**Two bounds.** **124 candidate predictors reduced to 67, then to 13 for the preoperative model**, and the
abstract says the 13-predictor version "retained useful discrimination, although performance was lower" —
without saying how much lower, which is the number a clinician would need. **And the validation is
temporal, 2022–2024 against 2014–2021 training, within the same 17 hospitals** — a real test of drift and
not a test of transportability to anywhere else.

## 4. A three-cluster typology in which one cluster is four people

**[Characterizing relatives who provide surrogate consent for clinical research in the ICU
(PICTURE-ICU)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40560-026-00963-7)** · *Journal of Intensive Care* · 14 French
ICUs, 125 relatives

> "Cluster 1 (n = 4, 3.2% of the sample) comprised non-professionally active or unemployed individuals who
> reported lower levels of knowledge and ambivalence regarding their legitimacy to consent. Cluster 2
> (n = 59) consisted of older spouses and retirees (aged 51–75 years) characterized by high trust in the
> healthcare team and a strong sense of legitimacy, despite reporting significant stress... Cluster 3
> (n = 62) was composed of younger, professionally active children of the patient (aged < 50 years) who
> reported higher healthcare knowledge, felt competent to judge risks and benefits, and prioritized the
> clarity of information received. Overall, HADS scores indicated moderate to high anxiety and depression
> across all clusters, though this was not directly attributed to the research participation itself."

**Interpretation — the abstract calls this "a robust typology" and reports a cluster of four.** Clusters of
59 and 62 against one of 4 is, in substance, **a two-cluster finding with four outliers**, and the paper's
own description of cluster 1 — lower knowledge, ambivalent about legitimacy — is the group most worth
understanding and the one it has least data on. **Three per cent of a 125-person sample cannot support a
profile.**

**The two-cluster finding underneath is genuinely useful and needs no clustering algorithm to state.**
**Older spouses consent with high trust and high stress; adult children consent with more health knowledge
and want clearer information.** Those are different communication tasks, and the practical implication —
tailor the explanation to who is in front of you — follows.

**The most important sentence is the disclaimer, and it should not be skipped.** HADS indicated moderate to
high anxiety and depression **across all clusters**, "though this was not directly attributed to the
research participation itself." **The distress belongs to having a relative in intensive care, not to being
asked for consent.** That matters because the obvious misreading of this paper is that research consent
distresses families. **The data say families in the ICU are distressed, and some of them are also asked to
consent.**

**Bound: an exploratory, self-reported questionnaire in 125 relatives who had already consented.** Everyone
in the sample said yes. **There is no comparison with relatives who declined**, which is the group whose
perceptions would most change how consent is sought.

## 5. Sixty-five per cent are PGY5 or above, and half have never done a blunt-dissection drain

**[A mixed methods evaluation of pleural procedural experience and training in intensive care
trainees](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC13786554)** · four New South Wales ICUs serving 1.2 million
people · 40 of 70 doctors (57%)

> "26/40 (65%) of survey respondents were PGY 5 or above... 19/40 (47.5%) had received formal training in
> pleural interventions, and 14/40 (35%) had received formal training in thoracic ultrasound. In the 12
> months prior, 19/40 (47.5%) of respondents had not performed a chest drain of any type. Of those who had,
> 3/40 (7.5%) had completed more than 5. Across their careers 13/40 (32.5%) had not performed a Seldinger
> chest drain and 15/40 (37.5%) had performed five or fewer. Half of respondents had not performed a
> blunt-dissection chest drain in their career. 15/40 (37.5%) of respondents felt that they were confident
> to independently insert a chest drain. Almost all (38/40, 95%) felt they would benefit from
> simulation-based training."

**Interpretation — the juxtaposition is the finding and the paper does not spell it out.** **Sixty-five per
cent of respondents were postgraduate year 5 or above, and half had never performed a blunt-dissection
chest drain in their careers.** Seniority and procedural experience have come apart. **Almost half had
performed no chest drain of any kind in the preceding twelve months**, in units covering 1.2 million
people.

**The confidence figure is the one that should be read as reassuring rather than alarming: 37.5% felt
confident to insert a drain independently**, which is close to the 42.5% who had placed any drain in the
past year. **Self-assessed confidence is tracking actual recent experience, which is the correct
calibration and the opposite of the usual finding in this genre.** The problem is the experience, not the
self-assessment.

**And 95% asking for simulation training is near-unanimity**, which in a 40-person survey is about as
strong a signal as the design can produce.

**Two bounds, and the first is the one that governs.** **A 57% response rate on a self-reported survey about
a procedural skill is precisely the configuration where non-response is least likely to be random** — the
doctors who place the most drains and the doctors who place none both have reasons to answer or not, and
**the direction of the bias is unknown.** With 40 respondents, each one is 2.5 percentage points.
**Second, all volumes are self-reported, not audited against procedure logs**, and recall of "more than
five in twelve months" is the kind of quantity people estimate.
