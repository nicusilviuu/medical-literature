**Three days ago this watch had never reported a single document from SFAR, SRLF or SPILF — the three
societies at the head of its own list. All three are now open.** SFAR on 30 September, SRLF on 1 October,
and SPILF today, as item 4.

The first two items are a pair that arrived by coincidence: **two national procedural sedation documents,
one French and one Spanish, on a topic this archive has never covered.** Read together they say the same
thing about the evidence, by two different methods, and the French one puts a number on it.

---

## 1. SFAR / SFMU — procedural sedation in emergency medicine settings

**Published:** society date **25 June 2026**; the page went live 11 September 2026. Type: **Recommandations
Formalisées d'Experts**, SFAR with the French emergency medicine society. **Thirty-year literature search**,
PRISMA, GRADE grid voting, conducted **independently of industry funding.**
Douillet, Bouzat, Bijok, Blancher, Bouhours, Clavier, Combes, Devauchelle et al.

> **Objectif :** La Société Française de Médecine d'Urgence (SFMU) et la Société Française d'Anesthésie
> Réanimation (SFAR) ont collaboré pour proposer un référentiel sur la sédation procédurale en structure de
> Médecine d'Urgence.
>
> **Méthodes :** Quatre champs ont été définis : 1) **Indication de la sédation procédurale en situation
> d'urgence** ; 2) **Patients à risque et condition de réalisation : quand faire ou ne pas faire ?** ; 3)
> **Conditions de sécurité** ; 4) **Modalités de réalisation.** … une recherche bibliographique extensive
> sur les **30 dernières années** … selon les recommandations PRISMA.
>
> **Résultats :** … ont abouti à **33 recommandations** concernant la sédation procédurale en contexte
> d'urgence. Après 2 tours de votes et trois amendements, un accord fort a été obtenu pour 33
> recommandations. Parmi ces recommandations, **une a un niveau de preuve élevé (1 GRADE 1), trois ont un
> niveau de preuve faible (3 GRADE 2) et 29 sont des avis d'experts.** Enfin, **pour 3 questions, aucune
> recommandation n'a pu être formulée.**

*(My translation of the results: 33 recommendations, strong agreement on all 33 — **one at high level of
evidence, three at low, and 29 expert opinion** — with three further questions on which no recommendation
could be formulated.)*

**Interpretation.** **Twenty-nine of 33 recommendations are expert opinion. One rests on high-quality
evidence.** Across every formalised French recommendation set this archive has now catalogued, **this is the
thinnest evidence base of all:**

| Document | Recommendations | High evidence | Expert opinion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SFAR/SFMU procedural sedation, 25 Jun 2026** | 33 | **1 (3.0%)** | **29 (87.9%)** |
| [SRLF ICU nutrition, children, 15 Jul 2025](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-01/) | 29 | 1 (3.4%) | 23 (79.3%) |
| [SFAR in-hospital emergencies, 3 Sep 2026](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) | 36 | 0 | 28 (77.8%) |
| [SRLF ICU nutrition, adults](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-01/) | 34 | 3 (8.8%) | 19 (55.9%) |
| [SFAR airway in theatre, 3 Sep 2026](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) | 27 | 1 (3.7%) | 13 (48.1%) |
| [SRLF/SFC cardiogenic shock, 31 Mar 2026](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-01/) | 41 | 7 (17.1%) | 17 (41.5%) |
| [SFAR perioperative + ICU nutrition, 3 Sep 2026](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) | 40 | 11 (27.5%) | 16 (40.0%) |

**Thirty years of literature yielded one high-certainty recommendation.** That is the finding, and it is not
a criticism of the panel — procedural sedation in an emergency department is given to a patient who needs a
reduction or a drain now, by whoever is available, under time pressure. **The population is unselected, the
consent window is minutes, and the comparison most worth randomising — this drug versus that drug for this
procedure in this patient — is precisely the one nobody will randomise at scale.**

**The second field is the most interesting one and the most honest:** *"Patients à risque et condition de
réalisation : quand faire ou ne pas faire ?"* — **risk patients and the conditions for proceeding: when to
do it and when not to.** A guideline whose second of four sections is devoted to when *not* to sedate is
describing a procedure whose main hazard is being undertaken in the wrong place. **Field 3 is security
conditions and field 4 is how to do it, so three of four fields are about safety and setting rather than
about the sedation itself.**

Note also the arithmetic, which is now a routine check here: **1 + 3 + 29 = 33**, matching the stated total,
with the three unanswered questions reported separately. That is the "graded counts close on their own"
variant of [the convention worked out yesterday](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-01/).

[Read the recommendations](https://sfar.org/sedation-procedurale-en-structure-de-medecine-durgence/)

## 2. A Spanish national Delphi on paediatric procedural sedation in emergency departments

**Published:** *Emergency Medicine Australasia*, **1 October 2026** (**new this week**). Modified two-round
Delphi, **28 clinicians from 23 centres across Spain**, consensus threshold **≥70% agreement**. Open access
in PubMed Central.
Cahís, Sánchez-Manubens, Baena, Eslava, Marín, Ranera, Velasco, Corripio

> **Objective** To develop a national expert consensus on procedural sedation and analgesia practices in
> paediatric emergency departments in Spain and identify priority areas for standardisation, safety and
> implementation.
>
> **Methods** … Participants rated key domains related to **patient selection, fasting, monitoring,
> staffing, pharmacological strategies, training and safety standards.** We used a predefined threshold of
> **≥ 70% agreement** to establish consensus in both rounds.
>
> **Results** The panel reached strong consensus across core domains … including **minimum safety
> requirements, monitoring standards, staff competencies and organisational priorities.** Strong agreement
> supported the standardisation of procedural sedation processes. **Areas of residual disagreement
> highlighted persistent variation in implementation and resource availability across departments.
> Pharmacological preferences differed according to the clinical scenario; ketamine was preferred for more
> painful procedures, but drug preferences for less invasive procedures or imaging procedures varied.**
>
> **Conclusion** … **Although the study included only centres in Spain**, it addressed operational and
> clinical challenges common worldwide…

**Interpretation.** Two national documents on one topic within four months, by two different methods, and
**they agree about where the agreement runs out.**

The French panel put a number on it — 29 of 33 recommendations expert opinion. The Spanish panel reached
consensus on **safety requirements, monitoring, staff competencies and organisation**, and failed to reach
it on **which drug to use for less painful procedures and for imaging.** Those are the same boundary
described twice: **the profession agrees on who should be present, what should be monitored and when not to
proceed; it does not agree on what to give.** Ketamine for the painful procedures is the one drug choice
both documents' evidence appears to support.

**Three things about the method deserve stating, because a Delphi consensus is a weaker instrument than a
GRADE-graded recommendation set and should not be read as equivalent.** A **≥70% threshold** means a
recommendation can carry with nearly a third of the panel dissenting. There is **no evidence grading at
all** — the output is what 28 clinicians agreed, not what the literature supports; by contrast the French
document tells you explicitly that almost none of it is evidence-based. And **"strong consensus" in a Delphi
is a statement about a panel, not about patients.**

To the authors' credit they say the limitation themselves — **"the study included only centres in Spain"** —
and they report the residual disagreement rather than presenting a clean sweep. **A consensus study that
names where it failed to converge is doing the useful half of the job.**

**One cataloguing note.** This is a **Spanish national panel published in an Australasian journal**, and the
abstract does not name a sponsoring society. It is reported as a national expert consensus rather than as a
SEDAR or SEMES document; **do not file it under either without checking the full text.**

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1111/1742-6723.70344)

## 3. AHA — undertreated antithrombotic therapy

**Published:** *Circulation*, **1 October 2026** (**new this week**). Scientific Statement from the AHA
Council on Peripheral Vascular Disease. Not in PMC; abstract only.
Rudd, Bessada, Barnes, Bronas, Budd, Goot, Montanez, Trujillo

> **Antithrombotic therapy undertreatment includes omission, nonadherence, underdosing, and clinical
> circumstances that result in subtherapeutic antithrombotic levels.** … Due to **deviation from
> evidence-based clinical practice, misperceptions of bleeding versus thrombotic risk, and inequities in
> healthcare access**, many patients with **atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, or peripheral
> artery disease** remain untreated or receive suboptimal anticoagulant dosing. Antithrombotic
> undertreatment has been shown to **increase rates of ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, recurrent
> thrombosis, and mortality.** Real-world data continue to highlight substantial gaps in care, **which
> appear to be magnified in patients with inequity in social determinants of health. The emergence of
> antithrombotic stewardship** … represents a transformative opportunity to address these gaps…

**Interpretation.** The useful move here is the **definition**, not the exhortation. Bundling **omission,
non-adherence, underdosing and circumstantial subtherapeutic levels** into one named entity — *antithrombotic
undertreatment* — makes a measurable target out of four failures that are usually audited separately or not
at all. And **"antithrombotic stewardship"** is an explicit transplant of the antimicrobial stewardship model
onto anticoagulation, which is a concrete organisational proposal rather than a sentiment.

**It is a scientific statement, so the caveat recorded yesterday applies and applies sharply: no
recommendation count, no grading scheme, no evidence-certainty statement.** The language is
"has been shown to" and "represents a transformative opportunity." Set that next to item 1, which states
that 29 of its 33 recommendations are expert opinion. **The French document is far more cautious in tone and
far more informative about its own evidence.** That contrast is a reason to read instrument type before
content.

**For an anaesthetic or intensive care reader the relevance is inverted from the statement's own framing**,
and worth naming because the statement does not: this archive's traffic in antithrombotics is about
**stopping** them safely — [the ESAIC/ESRA antithrombotic guideline for neuraxial
procedures](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-06/) and the SFAR RFE on anticoagulant management for
planned invasive procedures, still unworked. **A statement about the harms of undertreatment is the other
side of every perioperative interruption decision**, and the two literatures rarely cite each other. The
genuinely useful question it raises for perioperative practice — **how much of the "undertreatment" it
describes is iatrogenic, created by procedural interruptions that were never restarted** — is one the
abstract does not address.

[Read the statement](https://doi.org/10.1161/cir.0000000000001474)

## 4. SPILF / SPLF and four endorsing societies — community-acquired pneumonia in adults

**Published:** *Respiratory Medicine and Research*, **25 February 2025** — **nineteen months old**, 3
citations. **The first SPILF document ever reported in this archive.** Co-published the same day in
*Infectious Diseases Now* (10.1016/j.idnow.2025.105034, 6 citations).
Dinh, Barbier, Bedos, Blot, Cattoir, Claessens, Duval, Fillâtre, Gautier, Guegan, Jarraud, Le Monnier,
Lebeaux, Loubet et al.

> Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) of presumed bacterial origin is a common condition with varying
> severity, **requiring either outpatient, hospital, or even critical care management.** The French
> Infectious Diseases Society (SPILF) and the French Language Pulmonology Society (SPLF), in collaboration
> with the French Societies of **Microbiology (SFM), Emergency Medicine (SFMU), Radiology (SFR), and
> Intensive Care Medicine (SRLF)**, along with representatives of general practice, have coordinated an
> update of the previous management guidelines, **which dated back to 2010.**
>
> From a therapeutic perspective, the updated recommendations define **the choice of initial empiric
> antibiotic therapy, indications for combination therapy, the use of anti-Pseudomonas beta-lactams,
> antibiotic treatment duration, and the indications and modalities for prescribing systemic
> corticosteroids.** On a biological level, **indications for biomarkers and microbiological investigations
> have been refined.** Regarding imaging, the role of different modalities has been reassessed, **including
> chest X-ray, pleuropulmonary ultrasound, and thoracic CT scan.**

**Interpretation.** **Six societies and a fifteen-year gap.** The previous French CAP guidance dated from
2010 — before the corticosteroid trials in severe CAP, before procalcitonin-guided duration was tested
properly, and before lung ultrasound was routine. **Each of those three is named in the update**, which
tells you what changed without the abstract having to say so.

Three items on that list matter most for the readers of this archive, and all three are live questions here:

- **Antibiotic treatment duration.** [*AJRCCM* published a paper on duration of antibiotic therapy in sepsis
  and severe infections on 29 September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/), logged but not yet written
  up. A 2025 national guideline that defines CAP duration and a 2026 review asking the same question in
  sepsis belong together.
- **Systemic corticosteroids.** The single biggest change in severe CAP management in the last decade, and a
  guideline that specifies "indications and modalities" is making a stronger commitment than most.
- **Pleuropulmonary ultrasound reassessed alongside chest X-ray and CT.** Imaging recommendations in an
  infection guideline are unusual, and [the *Journal of Critical Care* paper on lung ultrasound in ARDS under
  the 2023 global definition](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-01/), also logged this week, is the adjacent
  question.

**What the abstract does not give is a single recommendation, a count, or any grading** — it lists the
domains that were revised. So this is reported as **found, in scope, and scope-only**, exactly like the SRLF
renal replacement document yesterday. **Unlike the AHA/ACC guideline below, the DOI resolves (HTTP 200)**, so
a full read is a realistic next step rather than a blocked one.

**One mechanical finding worth recording.** The guideline was **co-published in two journals on the same
day**, and **only one of the two records carries an abstract** — *Respiratory Medicine and Research* has
1,253 characters, *Infectious Diseases Now* has zero. The *Infectious Diseases Now* version is the more
cited of the two (6 versus 3). **A co-published guideline must be checked in both records; the better-known
venue is not necessarily the one with the abstract**, and a search that found only the *Infectious Diseases
Now* record would have concluded this document deposits nothing.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resmer.2025.101161)

### The 30 September unresolved item: a real development, and it narrows the question

On 30 September this watch reported the **2026 AHA/ACC perioperative cardiovascular guideline** as found but
unresolved, because its deposited abstract is the 2024 edition's with the year changed, and noted that a
JACC co-publication appeared to exist but was **not indexed.** It was re-checked yesterday and was still
absent. **It appeared in the index today**, and it settles part of the question.

| Record | DOI | First publication | Abstract length | Abstract checksum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **2026, *Circulation*** | 10.1161/cir.0000000000001472 | 28 Sep 2026 | 1,214 | `8d624859f384` |
| **2026, *JACC*** (newly indexed) | 10.1016/j.jacc.2026.06.017 | **1 Sep 2026** | 1,214 | **`8d624859f384`** |
| 2024, *Circulation* | 10.1161/cir.0000000000001285 | 24 Sep 2024 | 1,214 | `f8ee1aef7cc5` |

**The two 2026 records are byte-identical to each other and differ from 2024 only by the year.** Two things
follow.

**What is now settled:** this is a **real 2026 document with its own co-publication**, carrying its own DOI,
PMID and journal, first published in *JACC* on **1 September 2026** — four weeks before the *Circulation*
record. **The "duplicate deposit of the 2024 record" explanation is effectively ruled out**, because a
deposit artefact does not produce a second journal's co-publication.

**What is now worse:** the stale abstract is **not a one-off deposit error at one publisher.** The same text
— literature search ending **March 2023**, superseding the **2014** guideline, no mention of the 2024 edition
— went out through both *Circulation* and *JACC*. **A clinician who reads the abstract in either journal is
told the evidence review stopped three and a half years ago.**

**Still unresolved: whether the recommendations changed.** Both publisher pages return **HTTP 403** and
neither record is in PMC, so the document itself remains unread. **It stays at the top of the backlog, and
nothing about what changed from 2024 can be stated.** Recorded here because the archive said it would
re-check, and because a narrowed open question is worth more than a repeated one.
