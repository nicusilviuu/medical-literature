**No new guidelines from tracked societies today.** The guideline-titled sweep across 19–21
September returned 17 hits and **nothing in scope**: a Delphi consensus on reporting cognitive
training trials, radiofrequency ablation for knee pain in rural communities, a Chinese
rhino-orbital endoscopy consensus, a Turkish medical education framework, an AFPBN consensus on
daytime sleepiness in depression, dermatology certification quality indicators, and a **correction
notice to the KDIGO 2026 anaemia in CKD guideline** (no abstract, out of primary scope).

**The French sweep returned four *Infectious Diseases Now* items, all previously seen** — the HPV
school campaign report, the phage therapy piece, the Roman palaeopathology paper and the Nipah
preparedness article. **SFAR, SPILF and SRLF are now twenty-one days without guideline output.**

The society-acronym sweep returned twelve hits, all already handled or out of scope, **including
one the dedupe caught cleanly**: the AJRCCM correspondence *"Preoxygenation Recommendations in the
2026 ATS Guideline"*, which was already chased down on
[17 September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-17/) and traced to the ATS noninvasive
respiratory support guideline [covered on 28 August](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-08-28/).
No gap — recorded again so the second look is on the record too.

## The audit that followed, and what it found

Chasing that correspondence meant running a general search on airway-management guidance, and the
**scope-coverage audit rule** — check the archive against the *stated* scope, not against the
existing society list — turned that into a proper look. **Airway management is as central to this
project's primary scope as anything gets, and the archive has covered exactly two airway documents:
the PUMA tracheal extubation guideline and the ATS noninvasive respiratory support guideline, both
in late August.**

A search for airway guidelines and consensus documents since 2022 returned **167 records**. Three
are essential and none has ever appeared here.

## 1. DAS 2025 — the guidelines, and the one this archive most obviously should have had

**Society · Published:** **Difficult Airway Society**. *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, **first
published 7 November 2025**, January 2026 issue — **ten and a half months old**. **Cited 70 times.**
Indexed as both a **Practice Guideline** and a **Consensus Statement**
Ahmad, El-Boghdadly, Iliff, Dua, **Higgs**, Huntington, Mir, Nouraei, O'Sullivan, Patel, Rivett,
**McNarry**

The scale of the work, from the abstract:

> We conducted a **systematic review and a three-round Delphi process**… We invited **20 external
> experts** to guide recommendations on emergency front-of-neck airway and engaged DAS members and
> **16 international airway experts** to provide feedback. **Twelve members of the guidelines group
> participated in 65 meetings over 3 years, and 1241 papers were included** after a systematic
> review.

What it covers:

> **Sixty-five recommendations** were made in relation to tracheal intubation. These included
> **airway assessment and planning; peroxygenation** [sic: the deposited abstract's spelling]**;
> postintubation care; rapid sequence induction and intubation; the physiologically difficult
> airway; obesity; human factors; point-of-care ultrasound; documentation; and education and
> training.**

The algorithm is unchanged in structure:

> We maintain the concept of a **linear algorithm** encompassing **tracheal intubation (Plan A);
> supraglottic airway device ventilation (Plan B); facemask ventilation (Plan C); and emergency
> front-of-neck airway (Plan D).**

And the stated priorities:

> The recommendations prioritise **continuous oxygen delivery throughout airway management;
> maximising the likelihood of successful tracheal intubation at the first attempt; confirmation of
> ventilation with waveform capnography;** progressing through the algorithm in the event of
> failure; multidisciplinary teamwork; and ongoing education and training.

The framing sentence, which is the most quotable thing in the document:

> Overall, we emphasise the importance of **maximising the chances of success, rather than the
> avoidance and management of failure**, to improve outcomes for patients undergoing airway
> management.

**Interpretation.** This is the sixth structural gap found this month and the most conspicuous of
them: **the current difficult-intubation algorithm for a large part of the world, in a priority-list
journal, cited 70 times in ten months, and this archive had never mentioned it.**

**The reframing in that last sentence is the substance, and it is a real change rather than a
rhetorical one.** The 2015 guidelines were organised around failure — what to do when Plan A does
not work, how to avoid the can't-intubate-can't-oxygenate scenario. The 2025 document keeps the same
four plans but reorients the effort toward **making the first attempt work**: optimal position,
preoxygenation, neuromuscular blockade, first-pass device choice. That is a different instruction to
a trainee. Failure management is what you need when the front end has already gone wrong.

**Two of the listed sections did not meaningfully exist in the 2015 guidance**, and both pull this
document toward intensive care rather than the elective theatre: **the physiologically difficult
airway** — the patient whose problem is hypoxaemia, hypotension or acidosis rather than a difficult
larynx — and **point-of-care ultrasound**. So does the human-factors section. **An airway guideline
that devotes named sections to physiology, ultrasound and team behaviour is describing ICU and
emergency intubation, not induction on a fasted patient.**

**It connects directly to two things already in this archive.** The
[PUMA tracheal extubation guideline](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-08-28/), covered on 28
August, shares authors — **Higgs** in both — so intubation and extubation guidance from the same
international community arrived within a year. And the emphasis on **waveform capnography to confirm
ventilation** is the live argument behind a pair of duelling correspondence titles that surfaced in
this archive's sweep of 18 September and were **logged as letters rather than written up** —
*"Measuring mask ventilation: capnography is better"* against *"Classifying mask ventilation: current
evidence supports the MASCAN score, not capnography."* **Neither deposits an abstract and neither has
been read here**, so nothing is claimed about their arguments beyond their titles; but the exchange
is evidently live, and a 65-recommendation guideline has already taken a side on the underlying
question.

*Access note: the abstract lists the domains and the algorithm but **none of the 65 recommendations**.
Same limitation as the ERC sections and the AHA statements. **The recommendation set is the document
and it has not been read here** — this now joins the SECTCV/SEDAR arch consensus and the SPAQI SGLT2
statement at the top of the full-text list.*

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2025.10.006) · PMID 41203471

## 2. A multi-society push for universal videolaryngoscopy — that says its own evidence is weak

**Societies · Published:** Airway Section of **SEDAR** (Spanish Society of Anaesthesiology,
Resuscitation and Pain Therapy), the **Spanish Society of Emergency Medicine (SEMES)**, the **Latin
American Federation of Emergency Medicine (FLAME)** and an international airway group. *European
Journal of Anaesthesiology*, **18 June 2025**, October 2025 issue — **fifteen months old**, cited 16
times
Gómez-Ríos, Van Zundert, **McNarry**, Law, **Higgs**, De Jong, Jaber, Karamchandani, Hansel,
Saracoglu, Leach, Guimaraes, Abad-Gurumeta, Gómez-Ríos, Michalek, Berkow, Fernández-Vaquero and
others

The aim, quoted: to *"establish multidisciplinary recommendations **advocating for the universal use
of videolaryngoscopy (VL)** in both emergency and planned care settings."*

Six domains — **clinical benefits; infrastructure and accessibility; clinical guidelines and
protocols; teaching and clinical training; dissemination and promotion of clinical benefits;
innovation, sustainability and research** — each with PICO questions, a PRISMA-R literature search
and **GRADE** appraisal, **conducted independently of industry funding** (stated explicitly, and
worth stating for a document advocating universal adoption of a device).

The result, and the sentence that makes it interesting:

> The synthesis process resulted in **12 recommendations. Due to the low quality of available
> evidence, most recommendations were formulated based on expert opinion.**

> The experts achieved **strong consensus**, formulating 12 recommendations to support strategies
> aimed at universalising the use of videolaryngoscopy.

**Interpretation.** Report this one for its honesty as much as its content.

**A guideline advocating universal adoption of a technology, which states in its own results section
that most of its recommendations rest on expert opinion because the evidence is of low quality, is
doing something unusual and good.** The declaration of independence from industry funding belongs in
the same sentence: both are the document telling you exactly how much weight to put on it.

**The tension with DAS 2025 is instructive rather than contradictory.** DAS prioritises
first-attempt success without mandating a device; this document argues the device is how you get
first-attempt success, and that the barrier is infrastructure, training and promotion rather than
evidence. **Strong consensus on weak evidence is a fourth pattern for this archive's
guideline-divergence thread** — not societies disagreeing (ESC versus ACC/AHA, 18 September), not
societies diverging on management (aortic arch, 19 September), not a regulator against the societies
(SGLT2, 20 September), but **a field agreeing firmly about something it admits it has not
demonstrated.**

**And SEDAR appears here for the second time in three days.** The [SECTCV/SEDAR aortic arch
consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-19/) was found on 19 September, four and a half
years after publication. **A Spanish anaesthesia society has now produced two documents squarely in
this project's primary scope that the archive found only by targeted search** — that is a society
list problem, not a coincidence, and SEDAR is now on the sweep.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1097/eja.0000000000002210) · PMID 40534455

## 3. Airway management in patients living with obesity — SOBA

**Society · Published:** **Society for Obesity and Bariatric Anaesthesia (SOBA)**. *Anaesthesia*,
**5 June 2025**, September 2025 issue — **fifteen months old**, cited 21 times. Indexed as a
**Practice Guideline** and a **Systematic Review**
McKechnie, Iliff, Black, **Ahmad**, Chesworth, Chesworth, Davis, Griffiths

> Patients living with obesity present specific challenges for airway management. They have been
> **overrepresented repeatedly in studies looking at complications associated with airway
> management.** Whilst generic airway guidelines offer assistance, **there are no evidence-based
> guidelines specific to this group of patients**.

Method: expert multidisciplinary working party, **systematic review followed by a three-round Delphi
process.**

> **Forty-three recommendations** are made… covering **pre-assessment; planning; peroxygenation**
> [sic]**; tracheal intubation; supraglottic airway devices; tracheal extubation; training; and
> organisational responsibilities.**

And a deliberate limit: *"They are **not intended to replace** current airway management
guidelines."*

**Interpretation.** The least headline-grabbing of the three and possibly the most immediately
useful, because it addresses a population every anaesthetist meets weekly.

**The justification is a clean epidemiological argument**: patients living with obesity are
repeatedly overrepresented in airway complication series — NAP4 being the obvious reference point —
and generic guidance has never been specific to them. **Forty-three recommendations across
pre-assessment to organisational responsibility is a wider remit than an algorithm**, and the
inclusion of *organisational responsibilities* puts obligations on departments, not just on the
person holding the laryngoscope, which is the part most likely to be ignored.

**Note the overlapping authorship again**: **Ahmad** leads DAS 2025 and is here; **Iliff** and
**McKechnie** appear in both, and McKechnie is also an author of the [multidisciplinary consensus on
GLP-1 and SGLT2 drugs](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/) found yesterday. **A small,
highly productive UK airway and perioperative community is generating a great deal of the guidance in
this archive's primary scope, and until this week the archive had almost none of it.**

[Read the recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16647) · PMID 40468902
