## Both sweeps run, a fourth distinct pattern in four days, and a guideline this watch has missed

**Today's MEDLINE batch landed — 6,087 records, as the brief published two hours ago recorded — so unlike
yesterday there was material to find.**

**The guideline-publication-type sweep returned 2 hits, 2 returned, and neither was in scope**: WHO guidelines
on the integrated management of obesity in children and in adolescents. **The title-keyword sweep returned 4
hits, 4 returned, and carried every one of today's four items.**

**That is the fourth distinct pattern in four days.** On 20 October the keyword sweep carried everything and
the publication-type sweep would have produced an empty day. On 21 October the keyword sweep alone found two
items. On 22 October the reverse — the publication-type sweep found the only new document and the keyword sweep
missed it. **Today the publication-type sweep found nothing in scope at all.** **Four days, four patterns, and
the only safe conclusion remains the one already recorded: run both, because neither is a superset of the other
in either direction.**

**And one of today's items names a guideline this watch has never reported.** **The acute pain position paper
is built on "the 2026 EUSEM guidelines" — the European Society for Emergency Medicine's acute pain
recommendations — and a grep of this entire archive returns no mention of EUSEM at all.** **EUSEM is not on
the tracked society list, which is the proximate reason, but acute pain management in the emergency department
is squarely within this archive's primary scope.** **The 2026 EUSEM acute pain guidelines go on the backlog as
a named target, and the fact that a derivative document was needed to find them is the point: a document that
implements a guideline will name it, which makes implementation papers a route to guidelines the society list
misses.**

## 1. The ICU nutrition guidelines agree on four things and disagree on six, and the reason is in the evidence base

**Society · Published:** *Clinical Nutrition ESPEN* — **ESPEN's own journal**, and ESPEN is a tracked society —
**21 October 2026**, **two days old**, 0 citations. **Narrative review comparing current international
guidelines for nutritional support in critically ill adults.** Authors Pardo, Gobé and Thibault.
**Scope-checked in with its status named: this appraises guidelines rather than issuing one**, the same class
as the ESPNIC comparison still on the backlog — **but where that one compares national adherence, this one
maps the substantive disagreements between the guidelines themselves, which is guideline-tracking in the
strictest sense.**

> Recent randomized trials have **challenged the traditional objective of early full feeding and shifted
> practice toward progressive, phase-adapted, and tolerance-based nutrition.** …
>
> **Guidelines broadly agree that nutrition should be initiated early after clinical stabilization,
> acute-phase overfeeding should be avoided, energy and protein delivery should be increased progressively,
> and refeeding syndrome should be anticipated. However, important differences remain regarding acute-phase
> energy targets, protein dosing, enteral versus parenteral nutrition, supplemental parenteral nutrition,
> indirect calorimetry, and enriched enteral formulas. These discrepancies reflect differences in methodology,
> healthcare context, and interpretation of evidence largely derived from mechanically ventilated patients
> during the first ICU week.** …
>
> **Several clinically important situations remain insufficiently addressed, including prolonged critical
> illness, obesity, sarcopenia, post-extubation nutrition, post-ICU rehabilitation, and nutrition after major
> surgery.**

**Interpretation.** **This is the most useful kind of document this watch reports and the rarest: not a
guideline, but an account of where the guidelines disagree and why.** **Four agreements and six
disagreements, each named.** A clinician who reads only this knows which parts of ICU nutrition practice are
settled and which depend on which society's document their unit happens to follow.

**The explanation offered for the disagreements is the finding, and it is structural rather than
political: the evidence is "largely derived from mechanically ventilated patients during the first ICU
week."** **So the guidelines diverge precisely where they must extrapolate — and the list of insufficiently
addressed situations is a list of everything outside that window.** **Prolonged critical illness, obesity,
sarcopenia, post-extubation nutrition, post-ICU rehabilitation, nutrition after major surgery.** **Every one
of those is either a longer time horizon or a different body composition than the trials recruited.**

**This is the same shape as the pattern this watch has spent the week documenting from the opposite
direction.** Three French-speaking societies issued 48 recommendations of which 10 were gradeable; SFAR and
SFPC reclassified a whole document because no study was powered to answer its primary question. **Here the
divergence is not between evidence and expert opinion within one document but between documents that each
extrapolated differently from the same narrow base.** **Both are the same underlying fact — the trials do not
cover the questions — surfacing in two different places.**

**Indirect calorimetry being on the disagreement list deserves separate note, because this archive has a live
thread on exactly that.** **A trial of indirect-calorimetry-guided nutrition during weaning
(`10.1186/s13054-026-06272-7`) has been on the brief queue for over a week**, and the monitoring thread's
question is whether measuring something more precisely changes an outcome. **Here is the guideline-level
answer so far: the societies cannot agree whether to measure it at all.** **That is a stronger statement about
the state of the evidence than any single trial, and it is the reason the queued trial is worth reading
carefully rather than quickly.**

**The limitation is the document's own class.** **It is a narrative review by three authors, not a systematic
comparison with a protocol, and it does not say which guideline says what.** **A reader learns that the
societies disagree on protein dosing; they do not learn from the abstract whether ESPEN or ASPEN or the
Chinese Society of Critical Care Medicine sits where.** **For that, the full text — and this is one of the few
documents this month where the full text would plainly repay the effort.**

[Read the appraisal](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnesp.2026.105200) · *Clinical Nutrition ESPEN*

## 2. A ten-domain toolkit for doing what the acute pain guidelines already say, and the guideline it names is one this watch had missed

**Society · Published:** **position paper**, in *Pain Research & Management*, **indexed 22–23 October** with a
deposited publication date of **1 January 2026** — **a placeholder date, flagged rather than repeated as
fact.** 0 citations. **Proposes a 10-domain cognitive and operational toolkit for implementing the 2026 EUSEM
acute pain guidelines in the emergency department. Six domains core, four context-triggered.**

> Although pain is among the most common reasons for emergency department (ED) attendance, **oligoanalgesia
> remains a persistent global issue. While the 2026 EUSEM guidelines provide evidence-based pharmacological
> recommendations, translating these into the crowded ED environment is often constrained by systemic
> overcrowding and a traditional reliance on technical skills, with limited integration of human factors and
> nontechnical skills relevant to suffering.** …
>
> The domains comprise: (1) **establishing psychological safety and therapeutic engagement**; (2)
> multidimensional assessment …; (3) shared goals and care planning; (4) context-triggered caregiver
> integration; (5) **severity-matched, mechanism-informed multimodal analgesia**; (6) **individualised opioid
> stewardship for opioid exposure, tolerance and Opioid Use Disorder**; (7) **an independent palliative and
> end-of-life domain, including proportional palliative sedation only for appropriately assessed refractory
> suffering**; (8) contextual assessment of broader palliative and social needs; (9) anticipatory workflow,
> reassessment and rescue planning and (10) handoff and continuity of care. **Domains 1, 2, 3, 5, 9 and 10 are
> core; domains 4, 6, 7 and 8 are context-triggered.**

**Interpretation.** **The premise is the honest part and it is stated without hedging: the pharmacological
recommendations exist, and patients in emergency departments are still under-treated for pain.**
**Oligoanalgesia is not an evidence problem. It is an implementation problem, and the paper says so in its
first two sentences.**

**This is the implementation end of a thread this watch opened on 19 October and thought it had closed
yesterday.** The HRS prior-authorization statement described payers denying guideline-indicated procedures; the
Canadian transcatheter update described eligible patients never being referred; and
[yesterday](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-22/) a Canadian spinal-procedures guideline **dissolved the
middle of that pathway by seating the provincial funder on the panel that wrote the recommendations.** **Today
adds the last link and it is the shortest one: the clinician at the bedside who knows the recommendation and
does not act on it.** **Four documents now describing four different places a recommendation fails to become
a treatment, and only one of them is about money.**

**The structural choice worth copying is the core-versus-context-triggered split.** **Six domains apply to
every patient; four activate only when relevant, and the paper says all ten are "briefly considered" while
specific tools are "activated only when clinically relevant."** **That is an unusually realistic design for a
document aimed at an overcrowded department** — most implementation frameworks present every element as
mandatory and are therefore ignored wholesale. **A framework that tells a clinician which six things always
matter is more likely to be used than one that insists on ten.**

**Domain 7 is the one that will attract disagreement and it is correctly fenced.** **"An independent palliative
and end-of-life domain, including proportional palliative sedation only for appropriately assessed refractory
suffering."** **Putting palliative sedation inside an emergency-department acute-pain framework is a
substantive claim: that some patients arriving in pain are dying, and that the framework should say what to do
rather than defer.** **The qualifiers — proportional, only, appropriately assessed, refractory — are all
doing work**, and they echo the SFAR and SOFMER recommendations on withholding and withdrawing treatment this
watch reported on 19 October, which attempted eleven questions and graded three.

**The limitations are the document's class and its date.** **This is a position paper proposing a framework, not
a guideline and not a trial: there is no evidence here that using the toolkit reduces oligoanalgesia**, and the
paper does not claim there is. **And the deposited publication date of 1 January 2026 is a placeholder — the
same defect this archive has logged repeatedly — so its real date is unknown from the record.** **Ten domains
synthesised by consensus is a hypothesis about implementation, and the thing it exists to fix is measurable,
so the trial is possible.**

[Read the position paper](https://doi.org/10.1155/prm/9154388) · *Pain Research & Management*

## 3. A review of the 2024 AHA and ACC perioperative guideline, which is the readable predecessor of the 2026 one this watch cannot open

**Society · Published:** *Advances in Anesthesia*, **18 August 2026** — **ten weeks old**, 0 citations.
**A review, from an anaesthesia journal, of the 2024 AHA and ACC Guideline for Perioperative Cardiovascular
Management for Noncardiac Surgery**, itself an update of guidelines published in 2014.

> This article is a review of the **2024** American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Guideline
> for Perioperative Cardiovascular Management for Noncardiac Surgery, **an update of previous guidelines
> published in 2014.** The current epidemiology of cardiovascular disease in the US surgical population is
> discussed followed by sections exploring **risk factors for the occurrence of major adverse cardiac and
> cerebral events (MACCE), specific recommendations for preoperative evaluation, perioperative management, and
> postoperative surveillance for MACCE and myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery.**

**Interpretation.** **The reason to report a ten-week-old review of a two-year-old guideline is specific, and
without it this item would not belong here.** **On 30 September this watch found the
[2026 AHA/ACC/ACS/ASNC/HRS/SCA/SCCT/SCMR/SVM guideline](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) for the
same subject — nine co-signing societies, published in *Circulation* on 28 September — and has been unable to
read beyond its 1,214-character abstract on every check since.** **The obstacle is recorded: a Cloudflare
JavaScript challenge on both publisher pages, no shell route, and a monthly re-check scheduled for
7 November.** **This review is the most recent readable account of the document the 2026 guideline updates.**

**Two things have to be said plainly about it.** **First, it is about the 2024 guideline and is therefore
already superseded — it was published on 18 August 2026, six weeks before the 2026 version appeared, and its
author could not have known.** **A reader must not take it as current guidance.** **Second, its abstract is
604 characters and names its own section headings rather than its findings**: epidemiology, risk factors for
major adverse cardiac and cerebral events, preoperative evaluation, perioperative management, postoperative
surveillance. **It establishes the 2024 guideline's structure and nothing about its content.**

**What it is good for is the question the 2026 guideline raises and cannot answer from behind its paywall:
what changed.** **The 2024 document was itself a decennial update of 2014, and a 2026 revision arriving two
years later with nine co-signing societies implies a substantial change rather than a refresh.** **Knowing the
2024 guideline's architecture — the MACCE framing, the explicit postoperative myocardial-injury surveillance
section — gives a reader the baseline against which the 2026 recommendations can be read once anyone can read
them.** **It is scaffolding, not content, and it is reported as such.**

**The clinically substantive detail the abstract does preserve is worth one line: postoperative surveillance
for myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery is a named section.** **Myocardial injury after noncardiac
surgery is common, usually silent, and strongly associated with mortality, and whether to screen for it
routinely is one of the genuinely open questions in perioperative medicine.** **That the 2024 guideline
devoted a section to it tells a reader where to look first in the 2026 version.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aan.2026.05.004) · *Advances in Anesthesia*

## 4. The fifth Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 document to reach this archive, and its abstract contains no recommendation

**Society · Published:** *Journal of Hospital Medicine*, **21 October 2026** — **two days old**, 0 citations.
**A "clinical guideline highlights for the hospitalist" summary of the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 adult
guidelines.**

> **Guideline title:** Surviving Sepsis Campaign: International Guidelines for Management of Sepsis and Septic
> Shock (2026). **Release date:** 2026. **Prior version(s):** Initial guidelines released in 2004 with major
> updates in 2008, 2012, 2016, and 2021; the 2026 report is the latest update. **Developer:** Surviving Sepsis
> Campaign (Society of Critical Care Medicine [SCCM] and European Society of Intensive Care Medicine [ESICM]).
> **Funding source:** SCCM and ESICM; **no industry funding.** **Target population:** Adults with suspected or
> confirmed sepsis or septic shock in hospital and acute care settings.

**Interpretation.** **That is the entire abstract — 584 characters of masthead.** **Title, release date,
version history, developer, funder, target population, and not one recommendation.** **This is the journal's
standard template for the series and the actual clinical content sits in the body, so it is a deposit-format
observation rather than a failure by the authors** — **but the effect on anyone reading the index is that a
summary of the world's principal sepsis guideline arrives carrying no clinical information at all.**

**It is the fifth Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 document this archive has logged, and the count is worth
setting out because it says something about how guidelines actually circulate.** **The adult and paediatric
primary guidelines were reported on [1 September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-01/)** — the adult
version published 23 March 2026. **On [13 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-13/) the brief reported
the dissenting opinion and the companion controversies-and-future-directions piece, published the same day as
each other and both paywalled beyond the abstract.** **Today's is the fifth.** **One primary pair, two
documents arguing about it, and now one restating its cover page.**

**The two facts this document does contribute are both about provenance, and one of them matters.** **"Funding
source: SCCM and ESICM; no industry funding"** — stated explicitly, which this watch noted only yesterday is
rare enough in guideline abstracts to be worth recording, and which the primary guideline's own abstract did
not carry into this archive. **And the version history — 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2021, 2026 — establishes a
four-to-five-year revision cycle**, so the 2026 edition arrived on schedule rather than early, which is useful
context for how much should be expected to have changed.

**It earns its place on those two facts and on the structural point, and not on its content, which is
absent.** **A reader wanting to know what the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign actually recommends is still
better served by the dissenting opinion, which at least argues with specifics.**

[Read the summary](https://doi.org/10.1002/jhm.70512) · *Journal of Hospital Medicine*
