## No new guidelines from tracked societies today

**No new or updated guidance appeared from SFAR, SPILF, SRLF, ESAIC, ESC, ESPEN, ESICM, SCCM, IACTS, EACTS,
STS or ASA.** The guidance sweep for 10 to 17 November, run under the new title-and-journal filter, returned
**12 records, 12 collected, shortfall zero** — nine already held, one a comment on an oral oncology paper,
and **two new and in scope, reported below.**

---

## 1. The classifier: built, wrong twice, then right enough to use

**The 15 November society-name audit returned 176 records of which 85 were commentaries, corrections,
adherence studies and language-model evaluations rather than guidance.** The filter that separated them was
a hand-written regular expression assembled in the moment, and that entry recorded plainly that it **"is not
a durable instrument"** and had to be rebuilt before the November month audit. **It was built today.**

**Two decisions about it are worth stating before the results.** **It lives in the repository, at
`claude/tools/classify_guidance.py`, not in a scratch directory** — the Europe PMC helpers this archive
relies on have survived every container by luck, and a tool needed in December should not depend on that.
**And it was tested against the set it was designed for, not assumed to work**, which is the same discipline
that validated yesterday's scope filter.

### The first version was wrong in four identifiable ways

**Round one agreed with the 15 November hand regex on 157 of 176 records, 89.2%.** The nineteen
disagreements were inspected one at a time, and four were bugs rather than judgement calls:

| Bug | Example | Cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Titles beginning "Commentary" escaped the apparatus filter | "Commentary on: Treatment of Antithrombotic-Associated Intracranial Hemorrhage" | `^comment\b` does not match "Commentary" |
| An appraisal of a guideline was classified as the guideline | "Appraisal of Clinical Practice Guideline: SCCM Guidelines on Caring for Older Adults" | the publication-type check ran before the exclusion check, and the index types an appraisal like its target |
| "Application of" had been dropped from the exclusion list | "…Application of the Society of Critical Care Medicine Clinical…" | present in the old regex, absent from the new one |
| A "position paper" was recognised and a bare "statement" was not | "…: A Statement of the American Heart Association" | the pattern listed compound forms only |

**The second and third are the instructive ones.** **The appraisal bug is the same shape as the problem this
archive found on 15 November from the other direction: a publication type is positive evidence that a record
is guidance and is not evidence that it is the guidance rather than a paper about it.** **The fix was to run
a small set of overriding phrases — appraisal of, application of, commentary on, implications for,
adherence, concordance — before the publication type is consulted at all.**

### After two rounds of fixes

| | Round 1 | Final |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agreement with the hand regex | 89.2% | **95.5%** |
| Disagreements | 19 | **8** |
| Classified PRIMARY / SECONDARY / OTHER | 115 / 51 / 10 | **116 / 57 / 3** |
| **The five EACTS October documents** | 5 of 5 PRIMARY | **5 of 5 PRIMARY** |
| The two EACTS corrigenda | — | **correctly SECONDARY** |

**Six of the eight remaining disagreements are cases where the new classifier is right and the hand regex was
wrong** — it now correctly demotes "key updates and surgical implications for cardiac surgeons", "Updated
insights from the 2025 ERC-ESICM guidelines", "Reconciling the 2025 ESC/EACTS Guideline With ACC/AHA
Guidance", and an artificial-intelligence study of ASA physical status classification. **One is a new false
positive created by the last fix**: adding bare "statement" to the guidance pattern recovered two documents
and swept in a qualitative study of surrogate decision-makers' *perspectives on* a policy statement.
**Net better; not clean, and recorded as not clean.**

### The residual limitation, demonstrated by one document in two journals

**The honest limit of any title-based classifier is a guidance document whose title names no guidance word
and whose publication type omits it.** **Today that limit has a worked example.**

**The statement on environmental stressors and cardiovascular health is deposited in at least three
journals.** **Its *Circulation* deposit is titled "…: A Statement…" and the classifier calls it PRIMARY. Its
*European Heart Journal* deposit is titled "Environmental stressors and cardiovascular health: acting
locally for global impact in a changing world." — no statement, no consensus, no recommendation — and the
classifier calls it OTHER.** **The same document, the same month, two titles, two classifications.**

**That is not a bug to fix; it is the ceiling.** **No amount of pattern work recovers a signal the record
does not carry, which is exactly why this archive runs a society route alongside the index route and why
15 November concluded that neither is redundant.** **The classifier's job is to make the index route cheap
enough to run wide, not to replace reading the societies.**

**One more thing it changes about the December audit.** **The 15 November classification counted 85 of 142
unreported records as not primary; the classifier run on the full 176 puts 60 in the SECONDARY and OTHER
buckets with a stated reason attached to each.** **A reason beside every exclusion is the part a
hand-written regex could never give, and it is what makes the next audit's numbers checkable rather than
asserted.**

## 2. Maximum dose recommendations for local anaesthetics — identified, and unreadable

**[Maximum dose recommendations for local anesthetics: a scoping
review](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2026-108172)** · *Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine* · **published
16 November 2026** · paywalled, **and no abstract is deposited**

**This is reported as identified and not summarised, and it is the most frustrating entry of the week.**

**The maximum doses of local anaesthetics are among the most-quoted numbers in anaesthesia and among the
least-evidenced.** Every trainee learns a figure in milligrams per kilogram for lidocaine with and without
adrenaline, for bupivacaine, for ropivacaine; the figures appear on wall charts and in formularies; and
their provenance is largely untraceable. **A scoping review of where those recommendations actually come
from is precisely the document this archive would want to read, and it deposits zero characters.**

**Both routes were run and both failed.** **The index has no abstract. The title-stem search for
"maximum dose" with "local anesthetics" or "local anaesthetics" returns exactly one record — this one — so
there is no sibling deposit, no companion editorial and no earlier edition to read instead.** **That is a
clean negative for the title-stem route, which has three demonstrated uses in this archive and none of them
applies when a document is the first of its kind.**

**Named as a gap, with the routes attempted and the reason stated.** **The one thing that can be said without
reading it is what its existence implies: a scoping review is commissioned when a field suspects its own
numbers, and this one is in the journal that publishes the regional anaesthesia guidance.**

## 3. Half of resected lung adenocarcinomas are not tested, and two numbers that cannot be what they say

**[Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor-Mutation Testing in Patients with Resected Stage IB-IIIB Lung
Adenocarcinoma: Are Guidelines Followed?](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.athoracsur.2026.10.053)** · *Annals of
Thoracic Surgery* · **published 16 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**National Cancer Database, 2021 to 2023. 25,124 patients with stage IB–IIIB lung adenocarcinoma undergoing
resection, at 906 hospitals. Hierarchical multivariable logistic regression producing risk- and
reliability-adjusted hospital testing rates compared across quintiles.**

> Of 25,124 stage IB-IIIB patients with lung adenocarcinoma undergoing surgery, **14,061 (56.0%) patients at
> 906 hospitals were tested for EGFR mutations. There was significant variation in testing rates between
> hospitals (20.8% lowest quintile vs 89.7% highest, p<0.001).** Hospitals were more likely to perform EGFR
> testing if they were academic centers (40.2% vs 38.6%, p=0.009) and reside in a metropolitan area (84.2%
> vs 81.8%, p<0.001). Patients were more likely to be tested if they were female (aOR 1.13, p<0.001), Asian
> (aOR 1.45, p<0.001), and never smokers (aOR 1.22, p<0.001). **Only 1 in 2 patients … are tested … Further
> quality initiatives are needed … to ensure guideline-concordant care.**

**The headline reconstructs exactly: 14,061 of 25,124 is 55.97%, so 11,063 patients who should have been
tested were not.** **The quintile spread is 68.9 percentage points, a 4.31-fold difference between the best
and worst fifth of hospitals** — on rates that are already risk- and reliability-adjusted, so the variation
is not explained by case mix.

**It belongs in this watch rather than the brief because its subject is whether guidance is followed**, which
is the routing rule applied four times in two days. **And it is the cleanest example of the genre so far:
testing for an EGFR mutation after resection is not a judgement call — adjuvant osimertinib depends on it —
so a hospital at 20.8% is not practising a different philosophy, it is failing to order a test.**

**Two parenthetical figures cannot be what the sentence says they are, and this should be flagged rather than
interpreted.** **"Hospitals were more likely to perform EGFR testing if they were academic centers (40.2% vs
38.6%)" and "reside in a metropolitan area (84.2% vs 81.8%)".** **Neither pair can be a testing rate: the
overall rate is 56.0% and the hospital quintiles run from 20.8% to 89.7%, so a 40.2%-versus-38.6%
comparison, differing by 1.6 points and reaching p = 0.009, is not describing testing.** **They read like
the proportion of academic or metropolitan hospitals within some other grouping.** **The claim may well be
true; the numbers offered in support of it do not support it as written, and the abstract gives no way to
resolve which quantity is being reported.**

**The patient-level findings are small and consistent with a known bias.** **Female (adjusted odds ratio
1.13), Asian (1.45) and never-smoking (1.22) patients were more likely to be tested** — and those are
precisely the groups in whom EGFR mutations are most prevalent. **The guideline says test everyone
regardless of demographics, and the data say clinicians are still testing by phenotype.** **An odds ratio of
1.45 for Asian patients is the clearest single number in the paper for that claim.**

**Two limits.** **A registry cannot see a test ordered and not recorded, or sent and not resulted**, so 56%
is a floor for documented testing rather than a measurement of intent. **And 2021 to 2023 is early in the
adjuvant-osimertinib era**, so a repeat for 2024 to 2026 would say whether the gap is closing — which is the
study the authors' call for "further quality initiatives" implies but does not propose.
