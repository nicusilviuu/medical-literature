## 1. The check fixed yesterday caught a real defect today

**Yesterday this archive corrected `epmc_all` so that its shortfall is computed from the de-duplicated count
rather than from the running page-sum, and said plainly that the harmless direction had fired while "the
dangerous direction ... has never fired".** It fired today.

**The title-word sweep reported `hitCount=462 pages=461 unique=461 SHORTFALL=1`.** Under the old
computation this would have been invisible or, with one duplicate page, would have read as zero.

### Three hypotheses, and the two that were wrong were tested rather than assumed

**(a) The index shed a record mid-run.** Plausible on today's evidence of all days — the literature brief an
hour earlier recorded `PMC` shedding 269 records inside eight minutes. **Refuted: the hit count was re-read
twice and reads 462 both times.** The window still holds 462.

**(b) De-duplication removed a real record.** **Refuted by the tool's own output: `collected 461` and
`unique 461` are equal, so nothing was de-duplicated.**

**(c) The paging dropped it. Confirmed, and the paging log carries the signature:**

```
page: +61  total=461 / 462
page: +0   total=461 / 462
```

**The final page returned nothing while the count was still one short — the cursor advanced past a record
and then ran out.**

### The window was split by day, and all 462 came back

| Index date | hitCount | unique collected |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 13 Nov | 65 | 65 |
| 14 Nov | 47 | 47 |
| 15 Nov | 35 | 35 |
| 16 Nov | 25 | 25 |
| 17 Nov | 47 | 47 |
| 18 Nov | 67 | 67 |
| 19 Nov | 38 | 38 |
| 20 Nov | 138 | 138 |
| **Sum** | — | **462** |

**Every day matched its own hit count exactly, and the eight days sum to 462 — the whole-window total.**
Diffing the per-day union against the whole-window set identified the dropped record precisely:
**`10.1002/erv.70199`, *European Eating Disorders Review*, "Adverse Event Reporting in Trials of Digital
Eating Disorder Interventions".**

**It is out of scope, so nothing was lost from today's output. That is luck, not design, and it is recorded
as luck.** Had the dropped record been the delirium consensus statement reported below, this entry would
have missed the week's only new primary guidance and reported "nothing new" instead.

### What this does to the 5 November episode

**On 5 November a sweep reported 107 records and returned 73. Two candidate causes were noted, neither
confirmed, and the source notes record the cause as never identified; the cause-agnostic check was adopted
in its place.** Today narrows it: **`cursorMark` paging over a multi-day window can silently drop records,
and per-day sub-queries recover them.** That does not prove it was the 5 November cause — a 34-record loss
is a different order from one — but it is the first confirmed instance of the mechanism in this archive, and
it is now the leading candidate.

**The remedy is written into the tool rather than into a memory.** `epmc_all` now prints, on any positive
shortfall, the instruction to split the window into single-day queries and re-collect. **A check that
detects a fault and does not say what to do about it costs the next run the diagnosis again.**

## 2. First new primary guidance from a tracked-scope society in a week

**[International Expert Consensus Statement on Contemporary Prevention and Management of Postoperative
Delirium in Cardiac Surgery](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.athoracsur.2026.10.056)** · *Annals of Thoracic
Surgery* · **published 19 November 2026**, pubType Practice Guideline · 14 consensus statements, modified
Delphi, PICOT framework across four perioperative phases

> "Postoperative delirium is among the most frequent neurocognitive complications of cardiac surgery, with
> reported incidence ranging from 14% to more than 50%... Preoperatively, routine screening with the
> Montreal Cognitive Assessment and Clinical Frailty Scale, and multicomponent optimization are recommended.
> Opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia is recommended, whereas perioperative dexmedetomidine in place of
> benzodiazepines, cerebral oximetry, processed electroencephalography-guided anesthesia depth monitoring,
> and optimized oxygen delivery may be considered. In intensive care, validated delirium screening paired
> with sedation assessment, daily awakening and breathing trials, structured multicomponent care bundles,
> correction of reversible precipitants, and circadian-supportive environments are central."

**Interpretation — the recommendation strengths are the content.** Read the verbs: **screening,
multicomponent optimisation and opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia are "recommended"; dexmedetomidine
instead of benzodiazepines, cerebral oximetry, processed EEG depth monitoring and optimised oxygen delivery
"may be considered".** The panel has put every *device and drug* intervention in the weaker tier and every
*process* intervention in the stronger one. **That is a defensible reading of this evidence base and it is
also the opposite of where commercial and research attention sits.**

**The incidence range it opens with undermines any single-number framing of the problem: 14% to more than
50%, "depending on patient population and operative complexity".** A fourfold spread means the denominator
of any delirium-prevention claim in cardiac surgery is institution-specific, and this archive has now seen
delirium as an endpoint on seven of the last twelve days.

**What is not in the abstract is the thing a guideline is usually judged on: there is no grading of evidence
certainty, no vote distribution, and no consensus threshold.** The method is named as a modified Delphi and
**the agreement required for a statement to pass is not stated.** By the 15 November rule the panel size
belongs beside every threshold, and here **neither is given** — the abstract says "an international
multidisciplinary expert panel" without a number. **That is the thirteenth entry in this archive's threshold
catalogue and the third in a row to state no threshold at all.**

**Two statements worth noting for what they commit to.** The intensive-care section pairs delirium screening
with **sedation assessment** rather than treating it as a standalone, which is the correct dependency, and
it names **circadian-supportive environments** — a structural intervention most institutions cannot
retrofit. And the post-ICU section asks for **continued ward screening and structured post-discharge
cognitive follow-up**, which extends a cardiac surgery guideline into territory no cardiac surgical service
owns. **Recommending follow-up that no one is funded to deliver is where consensus statements usually go
unimplemented, and the document's own closing call to make this "a core institutional priority" is an
acknowledgement of that.**

**And it bears on a question this archive logged two days ago.** The guidelines watch recorded that the
pubType guidance route had returned **zero in-scope records across 18 and 19 November** and deferred a
keep-or-drop decision to the December audit. **Today the pubType route returned 34 records of which exactly
one survived the scope filter — and that one is this document, the week's most important find.** Three days
of data now give the route a 1-in-70 in-scope yield that includes the week's lead. **A route with a 1.4%
yield that delivers the lead document is worth keeping, and the December decision can be taken on that
basis rather than on the two-day zero.**

## 3. A four-society consensus statement, published in January, first indexed today

**[Clinical practical advice for anaesthetic management of adults with congenital heart disease undergoing
non-cardiac surgery](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjvshd/xwaf005)** · *European Heart Journal: Valvular and
Structural Heart Disease* · **published January 2026, first indexed 20 November 2026** · a clinical
consensus statement of the **ESC Working Group on Adult Congenital Heart Disease, the European Society of
Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (ESAIC), the European Association of Cardiothoracic Anaesthesiology and
Intensive Care (EACTAIC), and the International Society of Adult Congenital Heart Disease**

> "While 2020 ESC guidelines comprehensively address most aspects of ACHD care, they do not cover this
> specific area. Furthermore, although risk assessment for non-cardiac surgery is included in the 2022 ESC
> Guidelines on Cardiovascular Assessment and Management of Patients Undergoing Non-Cardiac Surgery, the
> unique pathophysiology of ACHD patients necessitates tailored considerations... A group of experienced
> professionals from 4 cardiology and anaesthesiology scientific societies ... have evaluated the available
> scientific data and reached expert consensus in areas of gaps in evidence."

**Interpretation.** **ESAIC is a tracked society and this document has never been reported here.** It is a
genuine four-society consensus statement in this archive's primary scope — anaesthesia for a growing
high-risk population undergoing non-cardiac surgery — and it was published ten and a half months ago.

**The document states its own reason for existing with unusual precision, and it is a gap between two
guidelines rather than a gap in evidence.** The 2020 ESC ACHD guidelines do not cover non-cardiac surgery;
the 2022 ESC non-cardiac surgery guidelines do not cover ACHD physiology. **Two comprehensive guidelines
with a seam between them, and the patients live in the seam.** That is a pattern worth naming: **a
consensus statement whose justification is the boundary between two existing documents is doing editorial
work the guideline programme should have done, and it only appears when a working group notices.**

**And it is the eleventh-hour arrival of a January document that makes the retrieval point.** First indexed
today, and its journal companion — the 2025 ESC/EACTS valvular heart disease guidelines
(`10.1093/ehjvshd/xwag001`, published October 2025, zero characters deposited) — **was also first indexed
today.** Two records from the same ESC journal appearing on the same day ten and twelve months after
publication is the index backfilling a journal, not a publication event. **The practical consequence: a
journal's back catalogue can enter the index in a single day, so a date-windowed sweep will surface old
society documents in bulk and the actual publication date must be read every time.**

**What the abstract does not give is any content.** It states the aim, the societies and the method and
**not one recommendation** — "reached expert consensus in areas of gaps in evidence" is the whole of the
findings. **No statement count, no threshold, no panel size.** Reported as identified with its provenance,
not summarised, because there is nothing in the record to summarise. **Named as a gap: the content of this
statement.**

## 4. A national society on procedural sedation, and it draws one hard line

**[Safe Administration of Minimal-to-Moderate Procedural Sedation and Analgesia: Practical Recommendations
From the Taiwan Society of Anesthesiologists](https://doi.org/10.6859/aja.202611/pp.0002)** · *Asian
Journal of Anesthesiology* · **published 16 November 2026**

> "(1) Risk stratification: High-risk patients require careful evaluation and planning by
> anesthesiologists, particularly those with an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status
> >= III, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, an anticipated difficult airway, severe cardiovascular disease,
> advanced age, or organ failure. (2) Pharmacological stewardship: ... Close vigilance is required when
> sedatives and opioids are co-administered because of additive respiratory depression. Given its narrow
> therapeutic window and the potential for rapid, unintended progression to deep sedation, propofol should
> be restricted to personnel trained in advanced airway rescue. (3) Continuous monitoring: ... continuous
> waveform capnography is recommended during moderate sedation... (4) Post-procedural recovery: ...
> validated criteria, including the Modified Aldrete Score and Post-Anesthetic Discharge Scoring System."

**Interpretation — the document's first sentence is its argument and the rest follows from it.**
"Procedural sedation exists on a dynamic continuum. Some patients may rapidly and unintentionally progress
to deep sedation or general anesthesia." **Every recommendation here is a consequence of treating sedation
depth as a continuum that drifts rather than a level that is chosen**, which is why rescue capability rather
than drug choice is the organising requirement.

**The hard line is on propofol, and it is a scope-of-practice statement dressed as a pharmacology
observation: "propofol should be restricted to personnel trained in advanced airway rescue."** That is the
most consequential sentence in the document and it is contested internationally — it is the position
anaesthesiology societies take and non-anaesthesiologist sedation providers resist. **A national society
stating it plainly is the news here, not the capnography recommendation.**

**Waveform capnography "recommended during moderate sedation" is the second substantive line**, and it
matters because moderate sedation is precisely the band where hypoventilation is common and pulse oximetry
lags. Pulse oximetry and cardiovascular monitoring are "routinely performed" — the weaker verb — which
reads as describing practice rather than raising it.

**Two bounds.** **This is a practical-recommendations document, not a graded guideline: no evidence levels,
no voting method, no panel size, and no consensus threshold are stated** — the fourth consecutive guidance
document in this archive to omit all four. **And the risk-stratification list is a list of risk factors
rather than a threshold rule**: "ASA ≥ III, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, anticipated difficult airway,
severe cardiovascular disease, advanced age, or organ failure" describes a large fraction of the patients
who receive procedural sedation, so "require careful evaluation and planning by anesthesiologists" is either
a substantial expansion of anaesthetic involvement or a statement that will be applied selectively.

## 5. A critique of the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign's newest suggestion

**[Surviving sepsis guideline suggesting Selective digestive decontamination is
unsafe](https://doi.org/10.1093/jac/dkag373)** · *Journal of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy* · **published
1 November 2026, first indexed today**

> "The 2026 Surviving sepsis guidelines suggest that selective digestive decontamination (SDD) be used in
> mechanically ventilated adults with sepsis or septic shock. This suggestion, new to the 2026 guidelines,
> is based on summary evidence of mortality prevention derived from >30 randomized controlled trials (RCT)
> including three cluster randomized trials (CRT). There are numerous paradoxes in the study evidence. Most
> notably, this benefit is confined to old RCTs with concurrent control groups with unusually high
> ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) incidences. It is not seen in recent large CRTs, which have
> non-concurrent groups... The contrary inference that increased concurrent control group VAP or mortality
> results from spillover effects is equally supported by the evidence and remains on the table."

**Interpretation — this belongs here rather than in the brief because its subject is a guideline
recommendation, per the 30 October routing rule, and it is the sharpest methodological objection this
archive has recorded against a specific new recommendation.**

**The argument is about where the benefit lives, and it is a structural one.** The mortality benefit appears
in **old trials with concurrent controls** and disappears in **recent large cluster trials with
non-concurrent controls**. Those two design features are confounded with each other and with era. **The
author's point is that the same data support an inverted causal reading: instead of SDD lowering VAP in the
treated, antimicrobial spillover may be *raising* VAP and mortality in concurrent controls sharing the
unit** — which would manufacture a between-arm difference without any benefit to the treated patient.

**That inversion is the whole of it, and it is not refutable from summary evidence**, which is why the
author says it "remains on the table". **A design in which the control group can be contaminated by the
intervention cannot distinguish "treatment helps" from "control is harmed", and pooling thirty such trials
does not resolve it — it averages it.** This is the same shape as the archive's standing warning about
cluster designs and the one on 16 November about an era effect credited to a guideline, arriving here as a
criticism of a live recommendation.

**One number does the work and it is an absence: the unusually high VAP incidence in the old control
groups.** A control arm with an atypically high event rate is the classic signature of either a different
population or a contaminated comparison, and it is precisely where a pooled relative effect will look
largest.

**The closing sentence is the one for a guideline committee: "The new suggestion fails to consider whether
SDD is safe for ICU populations."** An intervention that works by suppressing gut flora across a shared
unit has a population-level antimicrobial-resistance question that a patient-level mortality meta-analysis
does not address, and **a recommendation that is new in 2026 arriving without that assessment is a fair
criticism.**

**Bounds, stated because this is a single-author commentary.** It is 994 characters of abstract, it is a
critique rather than a reanalysis, **it presents no new data and no quantitative reanalysis of the pooled
estimate**, and the contrary inference it advances is offered as equally supported rather than demonstrated.
**It should be read as a well-aimed objection, not as a refutation.**

## 6. Reported as what it says it is

**[Optimising glycaemic control in cardiac surgery: narrative review and author
recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04617-5)** · *Journal of Cardiothoracic Surgery* ·
**published 24 August 2026, first indexed today**

> "Despite this, specific guidance on peri-operative glycaemic management during cardiac surgery is lacking
> in United Kingdom (UK) and Europe... Our recommendations include:- evaluating HbA1c in all patients
> referred for cardiac surgery, regardless of diabetes status; HbA1c target of 58 mmol/mol (7.5%) for
> patients with diabetes having elective cardiac surgery, maintenance of intra-operative blood glucose
> between 6-10 mmol/L (110-180 mg/dL) for all patients, and moderate rather than intensive peri-operative
> glycaemic control."

**Why it is reported and how it is labelled.** **This is not society guidance. Its own title says "author
recommendations", and the item list on this page says so too.** It is here because it is the only document
addressing a gap the authors establish — no UK or European perioperative glycaemic guidance for cardiac
surgery exists — and because a reader searching for that guidance will find this and may not notice the
distinction.

**The numbers are specific enough to be actionable, which is the risk.** HbA1c target 58 mmol/mol,
intraoperative glucose 6–10 mmol/L, universal HbA1c screening regardless of diabetes status, **moderate
rather than intensive control** — concrete thresholds with no grading, no consensus process, no external
panel and no voting. **A narrative review with numbered targets reads like a guideline and has none of a
guideline's machinery.**

**The one recommendation worth separating from the rest** is "moderate rather than intensive peri-operative
glycaemic control", which is the conclusion the intensive-insulin literature settled on across critical
care generally, so it is the best-supported line in the document and the least novel.

**And a note on the classifier.** It called this PRIMARY, on the word "recommendations" in the title. **That
is the sixth instance in three days of the bare-word fault**, after `standard`, `Guidelines`,
`guideline-defined`, bare-`statement` and `Guideline` in "Assessing the Evidence Base for the ... Guideline".
**Not fixed today, for the reason given on 18 November: the deferred regex fixes are scored out-of-sample at
the December audit.** The December list gains a sixth item: **`recommendations` in a title alongside
"author", "narrative review" or "our" is not society guidance.**

## Two observations about what the index did this week

**Eight 2020 documents arrived in this window.** All Spanish, all from SEMICYUC and SEDAR, all COVID-era:
ethical recommendations for difficult decisions in intensive care, perioperative management of suspected or
confirmed infection, cardiopulmonary resuscitation in suspected or confirmed infection, elective and urgent
thoracic surgery during the pandemic, "do and do not" recommendations for critically ill patients, and three
SEMICYUC consensus documents. **Their publication dates run from January to September 2020 and they were
first indexed between 13 and 20 November 2026 — six and a half years later.**

**They are real society documents and they are not news.** This is the aggregator habit the brief skill
warns about, at a scale this archive has not previously recorded: **eight documents from one country's
societies, all from one year, entering the index inside one week.** **Reported as an index event with their
actual dates stated, which is the 30 October rule — never blur new and old — applied to a bulk arrival
rather than a single paper.**

**And the classifier's one change from 18 November fired on a real record for the first time.**
`10.1007/s00101-026-01765-0`, a *Die Anaesthesiologie* editorial on why Germany lags international standards
in labour epidural analgesia, is typed `Editorial` with zero characters deposited, and **the
`APPARATUS_PUBTYPE` stage correctly demoted it to SECONDARY on the strength of its type alone.** Until today
that stage had only ever moved a record in a re-run of the day it was written. **It is still recorded as
unscored** — one correct demotion is not a score — but the design change is doing the work it was added for.
