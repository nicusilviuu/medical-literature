## Gap 2, resolved by verification: there is no missing ATS preoxygenation guideline

This morning's brief flagged an AJRCCM correspondence title — *"Preoxygenation Recommendations in
the 2026 ATS Guideline: Concerns About Trial Characterization and Evidence Asymmetry"* — and asked
whether a 2026 ATS guideline containing preoxygenation recommendations had been missed.

**It had not.** A search of all 2026 ATS guidelines returns eleven records, and the document in
question is the **Noninvasive Respiratory Support for Adult Patients with Acute Respiratory
Failure** guideline — DOI 10.1093/ajrccm/aamag302, PMID 42371750 — which this archive
[covered on 28 August](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-08-28/). Preoxygenation before tracheal
intubation falls inside its scope, and the correspondence is contesting that portion of it. No gap.
Recorded as closed.

*The correspondence itself has no abstract deposited and is not characterised here, though the
title's phrase — "evidence asymmetry" — suggests a dispute worth following if the full text becomes
reachable.*

## Gap 1, resolved by finding what was missing: perioperative GLP-1 guidance

This is the substantive one. **This archive has run a GLP-1 thread since 9 September** — the
[BJA adolescent gastric ultrasound study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-09/) finding 80% with
solid gastric contents after a 13-hour fast, and [today's GLIMPSE cohort](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-17/)
finding **1 in 36 UK surgical patients on a GLP-1 agonist and an aspiration or regurgitation odds
ratio of 11.39** — **without ever covering a single society guideline on the subject.** That was a
real failure of the watch, and the reason is now clear: the relevant documents are **not new**.
One is thirteen months old. The daily watch looks back 7–10 days and was never going to see them.

## 1. The ADS/ANZCA/GESA/NACOS recommendations — the anaesthesia college document

**Societies · Published:** Australian Diabetes Society, the **Australian and New Zealand College of
Anaesthetists**, the Gastroenterological Society of Australia, and the National Association of
Clinical Obesity Services. *Anaesthesia and Intensive Care*, **14 August 2025** — **thirteen months
old**
Hocking, Scott, Remedios, **Horowitz**, Story, Greenfield, Boussioutas, Devereaux, Andrikopoulos,
Shaw, Olesnicky

**This is the document that should have been in this archive from the start**, and unusually for a
guideline abstract, **it contains the actual recommendations.**

The premise:

> The physiological actions of endogenous GLP-1, and synthetic GLP-1RAs include **inhibition of
> gastric emptying**. This has peri-procedural implications due to the potential increased risk of
> retained gastric contents which may result in pulmonary aspiration.

What they recommend:

- **Ask every patient** about GLP-1RA and dual GLP-1/GIPRA use before anaesthesia or sedation, and
  **inform them of the benefits and risks**
- **Continue the drug** through the peri-procedural period — **do not stop it**
- **Preprocedural diet modification: a 24-hour clear fluid diet, followed by standard 6-hour
  fasting**, recommended for all patients on these agents
- For patients who have not completed or cannot have the 24-hour liquid diet: **risk-stratify with
  gastric ultrasound or minimally sedated gastroscopy**, and **use intravenous erythromycin**

And — the two things they explicitly **decline** to recommend:

- **"We cannot currently recommend using the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms for risk
  stratification"**
- **"Nor can we recommend an adequate cessation period for GLP-1RAs and GLP-1/GIPRAs to ensure
  gastric emptying has returned to baseline levels"**

**Interpretation.** Three points, and the second is where this collides with today's data.

**"Continue the drug" is the counterintuitive core, and it is right.** The instinct is to stop an
agent that delays gastric emptying. But these drugs treat diabetes and obesity, withdrawal
destabilises glycaemic control, and — critically — **the panel could not identify a cessation
period that restores normal emptying.** Stopping it for a week does not guarantee an empty stomach;
it guarantees worse diabetes. Given that, continuing and managing the stomach directly is the
coherent choice. **Note how much work the second "cannot recommend" does**: without a known
washout, the whole stop-the-drug strategy has no evidential floor.

**The symptom recommendation is now doubly confirmed, and it matters.** *"We cannot currently
recommend using the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms for risk stratification"* — a patient who
feels fine may still have a full stomach. **That exact proposition was the title of two
*Anaesthesia* correspondence items on 14–15 September**, neither of which deposits an abstract:
*"Symptom evaluation cannot exclude retained gastric content in patients taking glucagon-like
peptide-1 receptor agonists"* and its reply. A college guideline said it in August 2025; the
literature is still arguing it thirteen months later. **If you take one thing from this entry: do
not use "no nausea, no bloating, no reflux" as reassurance in these patients.**

**And here is where GLIMPSE bites.** [Today's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-17/) reports
a UK national cohort in which **peri-operative management was variable**, a range of airway
techniques was used, **and the aspiration/regurgitation rate was still 1 in 71 — odds ratio 11.39
against non-users.** This guideline's central mitigation is a **24-hour clear fluid diet**, which is
a substantial ask of an elective patient and essentially impossible for an emergency one. GLIMPSE
does not report how many of its 1,348 GLP-1 patients had 24 hours of clear fluids — the abstract
says only that management was variable — so this is not a refutation. But **a guideline whose main
protection is a day of clear fluids, in a population where events cluster at emergence, is a
guideline whose real-world effectiveness is now an open question rather than an assumption.**

*Access note: the abstract is unusually complete and the recommendations above are quoted from it.
The full text — which will contain the reasoning, the evidence grading and the erythromycin
dosing — was not reachable.*

## 2. Korean Society of Anesthesiologists — preanesthetic gastric ultrasound, with GLP-1 recommendations

**Society · Published:** Clinical Practice Guideline Committee of the **Korean Society of
Anesthesiologists**. *Korean Journal of Anesthesiology*, **30 July 2026** — **seven weeks old**
Bang, Choi, Min, Park, Choi, Hwang, Koo

A broader document — **preanesthetic gastric ultrasound in general** — with a dedicated GLP-1
section. It opens by naming the problem that fasting guidelines do not solve:

> Although contemporary fasting guidelines recommend minimum fasting intervals to reduce aspiration
> risk, **adherence does not guarantee an empty stomach**, particularly in patients with delayed
> gastric emptying or those receiving medications that impair gastrointestinal motility.

It is candid about its own evidential footing:

> **The current evidence base remains insufficient to formally support evidence-graded clinical
> practice guidelines.** The consensus-based recommendations presented in this guideline were
> developed through expert discussion and review of the available literature, **in which robust
> evidence is lacking.**

Coverage: indications for preanesthetic gastric ultrasound, examination principles and
interpretation, integration into perioperative decision-making, **management when gastric ultrasound
is not feasible**, and anaesthetic strategies for the full stomach.

The GLP-1 recommendations, which **align with the ANZCA document**:

- **Continue these medications in most patients**, with individualised risk assessment
- **Consider a 24-hour clear liquid diet**
- **Use gastric ultrasound when delayed gastric emptying is suspected**

Closing caveat: *"These recommendations are intended to support, not replace, clinical judgment and
should be refined through future prospective outcome-based studies."*

**Interpretation.** **Two national anaesthesia societies, eleven months and one hemisphere apart,
reached the same three recommendations.** Continue the drug, 24 hours of clear fluids, gastric
ultrasound when you suspect a full stomach. That convergence is worth more than either document
alone — it is not one panel's idiosyncrasy.

**The more useful contribution is the section nobody else writes: "management when gastric
ultrasound is not feasible."** Every recommendation in this field routes through point-of-care
gastric ultrasound, which assumes a skill and a machine that are not universally available at 3 a.m.
A guideline that plans for its own primary tool being unavailable is doing something the ANZCA
document, on its abstract, does not.

**And the honesty about evidence grade deserves noting.** *"The current evidence base remains
insufficient to formally support evidence-graded clinical practice guidelines"* — so they did not
pretend to grade it. That is the third society document this month to decline a grading it could not
support, after the [ESC consensus on lipid-lowering in VTE](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-10/)
and the [prehospital paediatric transfusion Delphi](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-13/).
**Declining to grade absent evidence is becoming more common, and it is a good trend.**

One note on what has changed since July. This document, and ANZCA's before it, rest on **surrogate
evidence — gastric ultrasound findings, not aspiration events.** GLIMPSE has now supplied the
events: **1 in 71, odds ratio 11.39, in 47,039 patients.** Neither guideline could have used it.
**Both are due for revision, and the revision now has an outcome dataset to work from.**
