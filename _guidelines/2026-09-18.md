## 1. STS 2026 expert consensus — management of oesophageal perforation

**Society · Published:** Society of Thoracic Surgeons. *The Annals of Thoracic Surgery*,
**17 September 2026** — **published yesterday**. Indexed as a **Practice Guideline**
Worrell, Antonoff, Kent, Kolb, Liou, Louie, Rice, Schulman, Sood, Sridhar, Velotta, Hayanga

**The first guideline from a tracked society in four days**, and squarely primary scope —
oesophageal perforation is thoracic surgery meeting critical care, and it reaches the anaesthetist
as an emergency with mediastinitis and septic shock.

Method, which is well specified for once:

- **Multidisciplinary STS panel**; evidence from **systematic PubMed and Embase searches, 2010–2024**,
  structured on the **PICO framework**
- Statements finalised through a **modified Delphi requiring >75% agreement**
- **Ten recommendation statements**, all achieving >75% consensus

The substance:

- **CT oesophagram is endorsed over fluoroscopic oesophagram for diagnosis**
- **Imaging may be omitted altogether in the event of spontaneous pneumomediastinum**
- **"There is a paucity of randomized control trials comparing endoscopic approaches for therapeutic
  management"** — but current literature supports **a range of endoscopic approaches selected on the
  technical features of the perforation and on individual experience and skill**
- The conclusion: **early diagnosis with CT oesophagram and individualised management emphasising
  endoscopic approaches such as stenting or vacuum therapy** optimise outcomes

**Interpretation.** Two recommendations here change what happens in the first hour, and the second
one is the braver of the two.

**CT oesophagram over fluoroscopic oesophagram** is the substantive diagnostic shift. Water-soluble
contrast swallow under fluoroscopy has been the traditional first test and has a meaningful
false-negative rate, particularly for small or contained cervical perforations; CT adds the
mediastinum, the pleural spaces and the collections that determine whether this patient needs
drainage tonight. For a patient too unwell to cooperate with fluoroscopy — which describes many of
them — it is also the only test that works.

**"Omitting imaging altogether in the event of spontaneous pneumomediastinum" is the recommendation
that will surprise people**, and it is a recommendation *against* investigation, which guidelines
issue far less often than they should. Spontaneous pneumomediastinum in a young patient after
vomiting, coughing or exertion is usually benign and self-limiting, and the reflexive contrast study
adds radiation, delay and false positives without changing management. **A society document giving
explicit permission not to image is doing something useful.** The implicit limit is in the word
*spontaneous* — this is not permission to skip imaging in Boerhaave syndrome or after instrumentation.

**The candour about evidence is the third thing worth noting.** *"A paucity of randomized control
trials"*, ten statements, >75% Delphi agreement, and a closing sentence conceding that
**"multidisciplinary, patient tailored decision making… remains essential given the limited high
quality data."** This is the fourth society document this month to decline a grading it could not
support — after the [ESC consensus on lipid-lowering in VTE](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-10/),
the [prehospital paediatric transfusion Delphi](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-13/) and the
[Korean gastric ultrasound consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-17/). **The trend is
now established enough to be worth naming as a trend.**

For the perioperative team: the operative implication is that **endoscopic management — stenting,
endoluminal vacuum therapy — is the emphasis rather than thoracotomy**, which changes the anaesthetic
from a major open case to a series of endoscopic procedures in a septic patient, often repeated.
That is a different staffing and airway problem.

*Access note: the abstract summarises the ten statements' direction but does not enumerate them. The
full text was not reachable, so the individual recommendations — and their strength — are not
reported here.*

## 2. Why ESC and ACC/AHA disagree about non-invasive coronary imaging

**Published:** European Heart Journal – Cardiovascular Imaging, **16 September 2026** — **two days
old**
Bader, Elsherbini, An, **Einstein**, **Tam**

Not a guideline but a **structured comparison of two**, which is why it belongs in this watch rather
than the brief: it is about what the guidelines say and where they part company.

**Where they agree:**

- **Clinical likelihood assessment before testing**
- **Selective use of coronary CT angiography and functional imaging**
- **Avoidance of routine surveillance testing in clinically stable patients**

**Where they diverge — three distinctions:**

1. **The European guideline is more prescriptive and algorithmic**, using **pre-test likelihood
   thresholds** to route patients to deferral, CCTA, functional imaging or invasive angiography. The
   American guideline is **focused on established chronic coronary disease, symptom change,
   functional decline, and whether testing will alter management.**
2. **ESC assigns a stronger early role to CCTA**, particularly at **low-to-moderate likelihood**, and
   specifies **more high-risk anatomic and ischaemic imaging thresholds.**
3. They differ on the **positioning of exercise ECG** and on the evaluation of **ANOCA/INOCA,
   microvascular angina and vasospastic angina.**

The summary line: **"The latest ESC guideline remains more diagnosis-forward and algorithmic, whereas
the ACC/AHA guideline is more management-forward and symptom-driven."**

**Interpretation.** That one sentence is the most useful thing in this entry, because it explains a
pattern rather than describing an instance.

**Two committees looking at the same evidence produced different documents because they were
answering different questions.** ESC asks *does this patient have coronary disease, and what is the
most efficient route to knowing?* ACC/AHA asks *this patient has coronary disease — will a test
change what I do?* Neither is wrong. They generate different behaviour: the European approach tests
more people earlier with CCTA, the American approach tests fewer people and demands a management
consequence first.

**This archive has now assembled three documents on how guidelines diverge, and they say different
things.** The [ILCOR architecture described in ERC 2025](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-12/)
showed ERC and AHA writing separately from a shared evidence synthesis, so divergence there is about
thresholds rather than science. The [IACTS position statement](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-07/)
on the CABG downgrade was divergence as open dispute. **This is a third kind: divergence by framing,
where the committees never asked the same question.** It is the least visible of the three and
probably the most common.

**Practical consequence for a perioperative reader.** A patient arriving for non-cardiac surgery with
a CCTA in the notes may have had it for European-algorithmic reasons — low-to-moderate likelihood,
routine pathway — rather than because anyone was worried. **The presence of the test is not itself
evidence of concern**, and reading it as such over-escalates preoperative workup. The ANOCA/INOCA
divergence matters too: a patient labelled with microvascular or vasospastic angina may have been
worked up under one framework and be unrecognisable under the other.

The limitation is that this is **a review by five authors, not a society output**, and its
characterisations of both guidelines should be checked against the source documents before being
quoted. It is a map, not a territory.

[Read the comparison](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjci/jeag259) · PMID 42745612

## 3. Fast-track hip and knee arthroplasty — 106 experts, 63 statements

**Societies · Published:** ESSKA with the **European Knee Associates**, the **European Hip Society**
and the **Asia-Pacific Arthroplasty Society**. *Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy*,
**16 September 2026** — **two days old**
Totlis, Kort N, Kort R, Budhiparama, Carulli, Clarius, Coden, Gamie, Gomberg, Grant, Guenther,
**Haddad**, Kayani, Kenanidis, Królikowska, Liu, Lumban-Gaol, Machado, Nandi, Pérez-Prieto, Prill,
Smith, Tandogan, Tsiridis, van Beek

Orthopaedic societies, but the subject is **perioperative care**, and the document explicitly covers
the anaesthetic phases.

Method, which is unusually well structured:

- **106 experts**: Steering Group (22), Rating Group (35), Peer Review Group (49)
- **37 questions**, statements graded **A (high-level evidence) to D (expert opinion)**
- Independent Rating Group scored each on a **1–9 Likert scale over two rounds**, Formal Consensus
  methodology
- **63 statements**, addressing **preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative** phases
- **39 graded A or B; 24 graded C or D**
- **Mean agreement 7.7 to 8.9**; **56 statements with strong agreement, 7 with relative agreement**
- **Level of evidence: I**

**Interpretation.** The methodology is the reason to take this seriously, and it is better than most
of what this watch sees.

**Separating the Steering Group from an independent Rating Group is the key design choice.** The
people who write the statements do not score them. That removes the most obvious source of
self-confirmation in a Delphi. Compare the
[prehospital paediatric transfusion Delphi](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-13/), where the
same 20-expert panel compiled the candidate items and then voted on them, and the
[DESTINE 2.0 driveline protocol](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-10/), whose abstract
describes **no rating or consensus process at all**. Adding a 49-member Peer Review Group for
transnational applicability is a further step almost nobody takes.

**Reporting the grade distribution honestly is the second strength: 39 of 63 statements at A or B,
24 at C or D.** More than a third of this document is expert opinion or low-level evidence, and it
says so in the abstract rather than burying it. A reader can weight accordingly.

**What it means for an anaesthetist.** Fast-track and ERAS pathways in arthroplasty are substantially
anaesthetic: regional technique, spinal versus general, tranexamic acid, opioid-sparing analgesia,
early mobilisation, nausea prophylaxis. **A consensus with 63 statements across all three
perioperative phases is a de facto anaesthetic protocol authored largely by surgeons**, which is worth
knowing about whether or not you agree with it. The abstract does not enumerate the statements, so
what it actually says about anaesthetic technique cannot be reported here — and that is precisely the
part worth obtaining.

It connects to a thread running through this archive: the
[COX-2 meta-analysis](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-09/) measuring pain by interference rather
than score, and the [rebound pain pair](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-17/) sent yesterday, both
concern exactly the analgesic decisions a fast-track pathway has to specify.
