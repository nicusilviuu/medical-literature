## The named target, found in one query, and it had been sitting in the index since March

**Yesterday this watch recorded a gap in its own coverage: an implementation paper on acute pain in the
emergency department cited "the 2026 EUSEM guidelines", and a grep of this entire archive returned no mention
of EUSEM at all.** **The guidelines went on the backlog as a named target, to be located by title search.**

**One query found them.** **"Updated European Society for Emergency Medicine Guidelines for acute pain
management in emergency departments and prehospital care", *European Journal of Emergency Medicine*,
published 11 March 2026** — **two hundred and twenty-seven days ago, seven and a half months** — **typed by
Europe PMC as a Practice Guideline, with a 2,059-character abstract deposited in full.** **It also found the
two-part 2020 EUSEM guidelines it updates, neither of which this archive has reported either.**

**Nothing about this was hard. The document was indexed, correctly typed, fully abstracted and in primary
scope, and this watch ran daily through all seven and a half months of it.** **The reason is the one recorded
yesterday: EUSEM is not on the tracked society list, and the index sweeps key on publication type and title
keywords within a two-day index window.** **A guideline published in March is outside every sweep this watch
runs, and the society route would have been the only way to catch it.** **So the gap was structural, and the
thing that closed it was a derivative document published seven months later.**

**That is worth stating as a limitation of the whole instrument rather than a one-off miss.** **The two
index-date sweeps find what enters the database; the society list finds what the named societies publish.
A guideline from an unlisted society, published before the sweep window, is invisible to both.** **How many
others there are is not knowable from here — but the method that found this one generalises, and it is now
recorded: documents that implement, summarise or dissent from a guideline name it, and harvesting those names
is a third route.**

**Both sweeps were run today as usual.** **The guideline-publication-type sweep returned 5 hits, 5 returned,
and none was in scope** — autism rehabilitation, the two WHO obesity guidelines again, primary biliary
cholangitis, and ADHD pharmacotherapy. **The title-keyword sweep returned 14 hits, 14 returned, of which five
were documents reported here yesterday and three are today's items.** **Fifth day, and the publication-type
sweep has now produced nothing in scope on two consecutive days.**

## 1. EUSEM updates its acute pain guidelines and opens by saying the last ones did not work

**Society · Published:** **EUSEM, through its European Pain Initiative**, in the *European Journal of
Emergency Medicine* — **the society's own journal** — **11 March 2026**, **seven and a half months old**,
0 citations recorded. **Eight authors. Systematic review following PRISMA, searching MEDLINE, Cochrane, Google
Scholar and EMBASE from January 2020 to May 2025, with evidence levels assigned.** **Updates the two-part 2020
EUSEM guidelines.** **Scope: adults aged 16 and over and children aged 1 to 15, in emergency and prehospital
settings.**

> Acute pain management in European emergency settings faces persistent challenges, including **inadequate
> clinical knowledge, training deficits, heightened emphasis on opioid stewardship, and concerns regarding
> drug-seeking behaviors. Despite previous guidelines, oligoanalgesia remains prevalent, with many patients
> experiencing suboptimal pain control.** The evolving clinical landscape necessitated updating the 2020
> guidelines, **considering emergent technological advances, the ongoing opioid crisis, aging populations, and
> continued pressure on emergency services.** …
>
> The literature findings were integrated with the clinical experience of the EPI panel to reach a consensus on
> **flexible, adaptable guidelines suitable for diverse European settings.** … **The recommendations integrate
> the Channels-Enzymes-Receptors Targeted Analgesia framework with WHO analgesic ladder principles and
> emphasize systematic pain assessment, flexible routes of administration, and patient-specific
> decision-making. Nonopioid and multimodal strategies are prioritized, with opioids reserved for appropriate
> indications.**

**Interpretation.** **The strongest sentence is the second one, and it is an admission: "Despite previous
guidelines, oligoanalgesia remains prevalent."** **A guideline update whose stated justification includes the
failure of its own predecessor to change practice is unusual and creditable.** **Most updates are framed as
new evidence arriving; this one is framed partly as the last document not having worked.** **And the listed
obstacles are not evidential — inadequate clinical knowledge, training deficits, opioid-stewardship pressure,
and suspicion of drug-seeking behaviour. Not one of those is fixed by a better recommendation.**

**Which sets up a tension with the document this watch reported yesterday, and the two abstracts make it
checkable.** **[Yesterday's implementation paper](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-23/) is titled "Beyond the
Ladder" and argues that the pharmacological recommendations exist while patients remain under-treated, because the crowded emergency
department is governed by human factors the guidelines do not address.** **Today's guideline — the one that
paper implements — "integrate[s] the Channels-Enzymes-Receptors Targeted Analgesia framework with WHO
analgesic ladder principles."** **So the implementer titles itself as going beyond the ladder while the source
guideline deliberately retains ladder principles.** **Read together they are not contradictory so much as a
disagreement about where the problem is: EUSEM locates it in knowledge and training, and its implementers
locate it in the ladder's adequacy as an operational model.** **Both diagnoses can be right, and neither
document engages the other.**

**The CERTA framework is the substantive methodological content and is worth explaining because the abstract
assumes it.** **Channels-Enzymes-Receptors Targeted Analgesia selects analgesics by the molecular target
engaged — sodium channels, cyclo-oxygenase, opioid and NMDA receptors — rather than by position on a potency
ladder.** **Pairing it with WHO ladder principles is a hybrid: the ladder answers "how strong", CERTA answers
"which mechanism", and combining them is how a multimodal regimen gets built rather than escalated.** **For a
2026 guideline written under opioid-crisis pressure, a mechanism-first framework is the right instrument,
because it gives a route to adequate analgesia that does not run through dose escalation.**

**Two limitations, and the first is a date problem the abstract does not flag.** **The search closed in
May 2025, the guideline was published in March 2026 — a gap of 284 days — and it is being read here in
October 2026.** **The evidence base is therefore about seventeen months old at the point of use**, which for a
field the guideline itself says is changing through "emergent technological advances" is a real cost.
**Second, and familiar from this month: there is no recommendation count and no grade distribution.**
"Evidence levels assigned" tells a reader that grading happened and nothing about what it produced. **After a
month in which the French-speaking societies reported their grade splits and the UK OPAT recommendations
report exactly what changed, the absence here is conspicuous.**

**And "flexible, adaptable guidelines suitable for diverse European settings" is a design choice with a cost
the document itself documents.** **Flexibility is how a guideline survives contact with thirty health systems;
it is also how it fails to change anything.** **The 2020 version was presumably flexible too, and the first
substantive sentence of the 2026 version records the result.**

**Paediatric scope note: adults 16 and over, children 1 to 15 — so infants under one year are excluded
entirely**, which for a document covering emergency and prehospital settings is a gap worth knowing about
before reaching for it at a cot side.

## 2. The UK OPAT recommendations name exactly what changed, which no other abstract this month has done

**Society · Published:** **the UK OPAT Good Practice Recommendations group**, in the *Journal of Antimicrobial
Chemotherapy*, **1 October 2026** — **twenty-three days old**, 0 citations. **Updated through a systematic
literature review, expert consensus and consultation.** **Secondary scope, stated: outpatient parenteral
antimicrobial therapy is infectious disease and antimicrobial stewardship, and it reaches intensive care
through the patients who leave it still needing intravenous antibiotics.**

> The UK OPAT Good Practice Recommendations (GPRs) have recently been updated … **In this article we outline
> the key changes in the updated GPRs. There are five new recommendations on (i) self-administration, (ii)
> OPAT in hard-to-reach groups, (iii) patient education, (iv) developing monitoring protocols for patients on
> complex oral antimicrobials and on new parenteral administration routes and regimes, and (v) equitable
> provision of care; and two amended recommendations, on (i) thromboprophylaxis, and (ii) early collaboration
> with vascular access teams.** Overall, the literature demonstrates the **increasing confidence of OPAT
> services in managing complex and comorbid patients** …

**Interpretation.** **This abstract does the single most useful thing a guideline-update abstract can do, and
it is the first this month to do it: it says what changed, item by item.** **Five new recommendations, each
named. Two amended, each named.** **A reader who already works to the previous version knows, from the
abstract alone, precisely what to go and look at.** **Compare the four other updates this watch has reported
in October — none of which identified its own changes** — and compare the [2026 AHA and ACC perioperative
guideline](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/), where "what changed" is the open question this archive
cannot answer because the full text is behind a Cloudflare challenge. **Naming the deltas is better than a grade distribution, because it answers the
question an existing user actually has.**

**The content of the changes is the second finding, and it is not about antibiotics.** **Three of the five new
recommendations — hard-to-reach groups, patient education, and equitable provision of care — are about who
gets the service and whether they can use it.** **A fourth, self-administration, is about shifting the
delivery of intravenous antimicrobials to patients themselves.** **Only the fifth, on monitoring protocols for
complex oral agents and new parenteral routes, is clinical in the narrow sense.** **So an antimicrobial
stewardship update has spent sixty per cent of its new recommendations on access and equity**, which places it
squarely in the thread this watch has been building all week about where a recommendation fails to become a
treatment.

**Self-administration deserves a line of its own because it is the most consequential and the least
discussed.** **Teaching a patient to administer their own intravenous antimicrobials moves a hospital task
into a kitchen** — it is how OPAT scales, it is how the service reaches people who cannot attend daily, and it
transfers a line-infection risk and a dosing risk to someone untrained. **A new recommendation on it implies
the evidence base has become good enough to support the practice, and the abstract's framing — "increasing
confidence of OPAT services in managing complex and comorbid patients" — supports that reading.**

**The two amendments are both about physical risk and both point the same way.** **Thromboprophylaxis and
early collaboration with vascular access teams are the two things that go wrong with a long-term intravenous
line in an ambulant patient.** **Amending rather than adding them suggests experience has sharpened existing
advice rather than revealed something new**, which is what a mature service's guideline update should look
like. **A small coincidence worth one line: today's sweep also returned a Vascular Access Society position
paper on access management after kidney transplantation, so vascular access teams appear twice in one day's
reading from two unrelated directions.**

**The limitation is the usual one and it is mild here: no counts beyond the changes, no grade distribution,
and no statement of how many recommendations the document contains in total.** **A reader learns what moved
and not how much there is.** **Given that what moved is the question, this is the right trade.**

## 3. Indian Chest Society home ventilation guidelines, covering an indication the European documents do not

**Society · Published:** **Indian Chest Society**, in *Lung India* — the society's own journal —
**21 October 2026**, **three days old**, 0 citations. **Panel review of international databases, relevant
guidelines and reference lists; recommendations refined through two rounds of consensus and graded using the
Indian Chest Society framework** — **a grading framework new to this archive, after GRADE, the USPSTF scale and
PROSPECT's three-way bin.** **Scope-checked in on ventilation: home non-invasive ventilation is respiratory
and sleep medicine, and it is also the long-term end of the ventilatory support this archive tracks.**

> In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), **home NIV is recommended for eligible patients with
> persistent daytime hypercapnia, including those who remain hypercapnic 2 to 4 weeks after an exacerbation.
> High-intensity bilevel ventilation should be titrated to achieve a meaningful reduction in arterial carbon
> dioxide.** … **For stable obesity hypoventilation syndrome with severe obstructive sleep apnoea, continuous
> positive airway pressure is generally the preferred initial treatment; NIV is appropriate when severe
> obstructive sleep apnoea is absent or continuous positive airway pressure fails or is not tolerated.** The
> guidelines also address heart failure with sleep-disordered breathing, chest-wall deformity, interstitial
> lung disease, bronchiectasis, **post-tuberculosis lung disease**, central hypoventilation syndromes, and
> palliative care. … **Where evidence is limited, recommendations reflect expert consensus and should be
> applied in the context of patient goals, clinical feasibility, and available resources.**

**Interpretation.** **The indication list is the finding, and specifically one entry in it: post-tuberculosis
lung disease.** **It appears in no other home-ventilation guidance this archive has encountered, and it has
never appeared in this archive at all.** **Post-tuberculous destroyed lung, bronchiectasis and chest-wall
fibrosis are a major cause of chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure in India and absent from the European and
American evidence base**, so a national society guideline covering it is not a local variation on an
international document — **it is the international documents having a hole where a large patient population
sits.** **This archive has recorded resource-tiered recommendations as a pattern before; this is the adjacent
and less-noticed case, where the gap is epidemiological rather than economic.**

**The COPD recommendation is the most transferable and is stated with usable specificity.** **Persistent
daytime hypercapnia, including patients still hypercapnic two to four weeks after an exacerbation, and
high-intensity bilevel ventilation titrated to a meaningful carbon dioxide reduction.** **The two-to-four-week
window matters because it excludes the patients whose hypercapnia resolves with recovery, which is the
commonest way home NIV gets started unnecessarily.** **And "high-intensity" is the correct emphasis: the
trials that showed benefit in COPD used pressures high enough to lower carbon dioxide, and the ones that
titrated to comfort did not.**

**The obesity hypoventilation recommendation agrees with international practice and is worth noting as
agreement rather than divergence.** **CPAP first where severe obstructive sleep apnoea is present, NIV where
it is absent or CPAP fails.** **That is the same position the major guidelines take, reached independently
through two rounds of consensus** — and this watch has a European CPAP guideline on its own backlog, so the
pair will be readable together.

**The last sentence is the honest one and it names three things, one of which most guidelines leave out.**
**"Where evidence is limited, recommendations reflect expert consensus and should be applied in the context of
patient goals, clinical feasibility, and available resources."** **Patient goals and clinical feasibility are
conventional. Available resources, stated in the recommendations' own framing rather than a limitations
paragraph, is the thing a document written for a health system with uneven access to home ventilators has to
say** — and saying it is better practice than writing a recommendation that assumes the equipment.

**The limitation is the one now familiar from almost every document this month: the framework is named, the
consensus rounds are counted, and the number of recommendations and their grade distribution are not given.**
**A new grading framework makes that omission worse rather than better, because a reader outside India cannot
calibrate what an Indian Chest Society grade means without seeing how the grades fell.**
