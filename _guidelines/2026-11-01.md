## No new guidelines from tracked societies today

**No new guidelines from tracked societies today.** The French societies first — **SFAR**, **SPILF**,
**SRLF** — then **ESAIC**, **ESC**, **ESPEN**, **ESICM**, **SCCM**, **IACTS**, **EACTS**, **STS** and **ASA**.
Nothing new from any of them.

**What the society searches did return, all of it already held here.** SFAR's medication-error prevention
recommendations with the SFPC surfaced again with the same 24 June 2026 date — **the fourth sighting of that
date in six days, after three consecutive hits on 27 to 29 October and an absence on 30 October**, and the
document is already published in this archive. SPILF's 2026 male urinary tract infection recommendations
surfaced, and `10.1016/j.idnow.2026.105319` is already in the
[31 August entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-08-31/). ESC's 2026 output is the late-August congress
set. ESICM's second 2026 call for guideline applications closed on 5 October, so its next documents are
later. **ESPEN's most recent is the October 2025 surgery update.**

**That is the ninth consecutive day on which the society route produced no lead document.** The standing rule
holds and is worth restating because it is counter-intuitive: **a society search cannot establish that a
document does not exist. Read it for identity, never for status — and keep running it first.** Today it did
identity work on four documents and found nothing new, which is the expected outcome and not a failure.

**Today's own index bucket contains two guideline-class records and both are out of scope** — a preprint on
physical-activity guideline attainment in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, and a critical appraisal
of a NICE consultation draft on obsessive-compulsive and body dysmorphic disorder. **Consistent with this
morning's reading that MEDLINE delivered zero records in twenty-four hours.**

## The October index-date month audit

**This was the dated action due today**, assigned here rather than to the brief because it is guideline-class
work. **It is the aperture test: the society list is what this archive watches, and the index-date sweep sees
around it.**

**It was swept as a range, not day by day** — and after this morning's finding that is not a stylistic
preference. A day-by-day pass would have put 52.56% of the month into three queries and would have
"checked" 18 October while that bucket held nothing at all.

**The sweep returned 71 guideline-class records in scope across all thirty-one October index dates.** Eight of
those are the second deposit of a document the sweep already held — the familiar PMC-and-MEDLINE sibling
pattern, plus one 1984 letter deposited twice. **That leaves 63 distinct documents**, which classify as
follows:

| Classification | n |
| --- | --- |
| **Already reported in this archive** | **39** |
| **In scope, guideline-class, NOT reported — genuine misses** | **8** |
| Guideline-class but out of this archive's scope | 5 |
| Not guideline-class on reading (research, protocol, case report, abstract) | 9 |
| Historical records restamped into October | 2 |
| **Total distinct documents** | **63** |

The five buckets sum to 63 exactly.

**So the in-scope guideline-class population for October is 47 documents, of which this archive reported 39 —
a hit rate of 83.0%.** **September's audit found 18 in scope with 10 reported, 55.6%.** **The miss count is
the same in both months, 8, while the in-scope population went from 18 to 47** — so the improvement is in
coverage, not in having had less to find.

**The single most informative miss is from a tracked society.** The **EACTS** expert consensus on robotic
cardiac surgery, developed with **EACTAIC** and **EBCP**, was indexed on 16 October and never appeared in any
daily society search. **EACTS is on the watch list and the watch list did not find it.** That is the aperture
test returning a positive result on the clearest possible case, and it is the strongest argument yet for
running the index sweep alongside the society route rather than after it.

**Three of the eight misses are abstract-free with no PMCID and not open access**, so they join the named gaps:
the bronchoscopy consensus statement, the AATS mitral annular calcification commentary
(`10.21037/jovs-2026-1-0009`) and the sepsis-guidelines editorial *"The clinical side of Icarus"*
(`10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155758`). **Named gaps therefore rise from 13 to 15** — the bronchoscopy statement is
recovered below from a sibling route and does not count as a gap.

## The audit's methodological find: the 13 October bucket is the twentieth century

**An independent query confirms this morning's restamping finding, and more sharply than the brief could.**
Two of the audit's 63 documents are **European Resuscitation Council Guidelines for Resuscitation** with a
publication date of **1 December 1998**, and **"Consensus development conference: coronary artery bypass
surgery"** with a publication date of **15 December 1984, deposited twice.** All three records carry a
first-index date of **13 October 2026** — the 63,095-record spike that is the largest day of the month.

**So I measured what that day is made of:**

| Publication date | 13 October bucket | 31 October bucket |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before 2000 | **60,856** | 1 |
| 2000–2019 | 1,660 | 3 |
| 2020–2025 | 219 | 22 |
| 2026 | **8** | **7,218** |
| Residual (no usable date) | 352 | 26 |
| **Total** | **63,095** | **7,270** |

**96.45% of the 13 October bucket was published before the year 2000, and eight records in it were published
in 2026.** On 31 October, 99.28% was published in 2026 and one record predates 2000.

**That is the mechanism, not an inference about it.** The largest first-index date of October is a
retrospective backfill of twentieth-century literature, and a normal day is current publishing. **A daily
bucket measures restamping, and this morning's withdrawal of the bucket-as-delivery reading is confirmed from
a query that was not designed to test it.**

**And it explains an aggregator habit this archive has recorded for weeks** — old papers appearing under
today's date. That was always described as the aggregator's behaviour. **It is the same phenomenon as the
spike days, seen one record at a time.**

## 1. EACTS — robotic cardiac surgery minimum standards

**[EACTS Expert Consensus Document on Robotic Cardiac Surgery Minimum Standards for Programme Implementation,
Training and Quality Assurance](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejcts/ezag187)** · **EACTS, with EACTAIC and EBCP** ·
*European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery* · **published 1 October 2026, indexed 16 October** · paywalled

From the published abstract:

> **Robotic cardiac surgery is entering a renewed phase of adoption … implementation remains technically
> demanding and resource-intensive and can expose patients to avoidable harm when initiated without a
> structured training programme, clear technical standards, and rigorous outcome monitoring. … It addresses
> the current regulatory environment for surgical robots and accessories and proposes minimum requirements
> for hardware, software, and instruments for reconstructive intracardiac and coronary cardiac procedures.
> Furthermore, it outlines prerequisites for starting and developing training centres, including surgeon
> experience, multidisciplinary team composition, patient selection, and institutional infrastructure and
> volume considerations. A stepwise, competency-based training pathway is presented that integrates video and
> live case observation, virtual and simulation-based skills acquisition, wet/dry laboratory training, and
> proctored clinical implementation, ideally supported by dual-console systems. The document emphasises the
> need for transparent documentation and outcome benchmarking via a society-driven database.**

**Document class: the consensus statement, and specifically the programme-standards variety.** This archive's
recommendation-structure catalogue has four genres — the guideline, the consensus statement, the risk
classification and the definition. **This is a fifth thing, and the 22 October format-decision rule covers it
exactly: read the design section before scoring the disclosure axis.** There is **no grade distribution and no
evidence-level scheme in the abstract, and none is required**, because the document's object is not a clinical
decision. It sets institutional prerequisites: hardware, team composition, case volume, a training ladder, a
registry. **Scoring it for GRADE would be a category error.**

**Interpretation.** **The clinically significant sentence is the one about avoidable harm**, and it is a
statement about programmes rather than patients: the risk being managed is a hospital starting a robotic
cardiac programme without the volume, the team or the oversight to run it safely. **For anaesthesia this
matters through EACTAIC's co-authorship** — multidisciplinary team composition and patient selection are
jointly owned, and a cardiac anaesthetist in a starting programme now has a society document to point at when
asking what the institutional prerequisites are. **The registry requirement is the part most likely to bind:**
a society-driven database with learning-curve oversight is the mechanism by which minimum standards stop being
advisory.

**What this archive does not know and is not claiming:** the number of recommendations, whether they are
graded, and what the volume thresholds actually are. **The abstract gives the architecture and no numbers.**
The full document would settle it and is paywalled; the user can send the PDF in a Claude Code session for a
fuller read.

## 3. PROSPECT, hallux valgus repair — and what changed since 2020

**[Pain management after hallux valgus repair surgery: an updated systematic review and procedure-specific
postoperative pain management (PROSPECT) recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1097/eja.0000000000002483)** ·
**PROSPECT Working Group** · *European Journal of Anaesthesiology* · **the October record is abstract-free;
content below is from a readable sibling record, and the relationship between the two is stated plainly**

**First, the record problem, because it changes what the date means.** Europe PMC holds **two records with
this exact title in the same journal and the same volume 43**: `10.1097/eja.0000000000002483`, issue 11, print
date 1 November 2026, abstract-free, no PMCID; and `10.1097/eja.0000000000002302`, issue 1, published online
**21 October 2025**, in PMC, open access, with a **2,087-character abstract**. **I cannot establish from the
records which relationship holds** — a print re-deposit under a new DOI, a corrected republication, or two
separate documents. **Neither record is typed as an erratum.** So: **the content below is the October 2025
record's, the October 2026 record is what the index delivered, and the one-year gap between them is
unexplained.** This is a fifth pattern for the publication-date catalogue, alongside the dual-deposit
placeholder, the future print issue and the year-only date.

The 2025 abstract, on what the update found:

> **Of the 375 articles identified, 17 RCTs and 7 systematic reviews/meta-analyses met our inclusion criteria
> (total: 24 publications). Interventions that improved postoperative pain relief included: paracetamol and
> nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or cyclooxygenase-2 selective inhibitors; dexamethasone; ankle block
> and, as an alternative, local anaesthetic wound infiltration; and minimally invasive surgery or percutaneous
> osteotomy. Insufficient evidence was found for the use of perineural magnesium or liposomal bupivacaine. No
> evidence was found for continuous popliteal sciatic nerve block or for the use of the plantar compartment
> nerve block. … there is one important change, minimally invasive surgery or percutaneous osteotomy is
> recommended over open osteotomy.**

**This is the guidelines watch doing what it is for: the 2020 original is also readable, so the change is
measurable rather than asserted.**

| | PROSPECT 2020 | PROSPECT update |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Search window | inception to Dec 2019 | 1 Jan 2019 to 19 Nov 2024 |
| Screened / included | 836 / **56** (55 RCTs + 1 review) | 375 / **24** (17 RCTs + 7 reviews) |
| Surgical technique | **"Conflicting evidence" for percutaneous chevron osteotomy** | **Minimally invasive or percutaneous osteotomy RECOMMENDED over open** |
| Steroid | "systemic steroids" | **dexamethasone**, named |
| First-choice block | ankle block, infiltration as alternative | **unchanged** |
| Extended-release local anaesthetic | insufficient evidence (extended-release bupivacaine) | **insufficient evidence (liposomal bupivacaine)** |
| New "no evidence" entries | — | **plantar compartment block; continuous popliteal sciatic block** |

**Interpretation, and there are three things worth saying.**

**The one substantive change is surgical, not analgesic.** A procedure-specific pain guideline's headline
update is a recommendation about the operation: minimally invasive or percutaneous osteotomy over open. **That
is the pattern this archive recorded on 30 October — guidelines converge on process and diverge on numbers —
seen from the other side: here the analgesic regimen did not move at all in six years while the surgical
recommendation flipped from conflicting to recommended.**

**Liposomal bupivacaine is the measurement that did not happen.** In 2020 extended-release bupivacaine had
insufficient evidence; in a search running to November 2024 **liposomal bupivacaine still has insufficient
evidence.** **Five further years of literature did not settle a question about a product that is marketed on
exactly this indication**, and a reader should take the stability of that verdict as informative rather than
provisional.

**And the evidence base shrank by design, which must not be misread as a weakening.** 24 publications against
56 is not a retreat: the update searched only from 2019, so its 24 are the new literature laid on top of the
2020 base. **The screening yields are nearly identical — 6.4% against 6.7% — which is the honest way to see
that nothing about the field's productivity changed.**

## 4. Continuous glucose monitoring in critically ill patients — 27 recommendations on 12 questions

**[Expert consensus on the clinical application of continuous glucose monitoring in critically ill patients
(2026 edition)](https://doi.org/10.3760/cma.j.cn112137-20260616-01560)** · **Branch of Critical Care Medicine,
China Association for Disaster and Emergency Rescue Medicine** · *Zhonghua Yi Xue Za Zhi* · **published
1 October 2026, indexed 17 October** · Chinese, with an English abstract · paywalled

From the published abstract:

> **Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) technology, which continuously and in real time captures interstitial
> fluid glucose concentrations, provides multidimensional glycemic metrics and offers a novel technical
> approach to overcoming the inherent limitations of conventional intermittent glucose monitoring … However,
> systematic guidance for its standardized application in the intensive care units (ICU) setting remains
> lacking. … This consensus puts forward 27 recommendations addressing 12 questions regarding the use of CGM
> in critically ill patients. The content covers the definition, classification and hazards of glycemic
> abnormalities in critically ill patients, common blood glucose monitoring methods and their clinical
> implications, eligible populations for CGM, data interpretation, quality control and routine care of CGM, as
> well as accessibility and economic costs.**

**27 recommendations across 12 questions — 2.25 per question.** The document names its own gap: no
standardised guidance for CGM in the ICU exists. **The route is the one this archive settled on 6 October:
Chinese society documents are in Europe PMC and the guideline sweep finds them.** This one, unusually for that
route, deposits a structural abstract rather than the recommendations verbatim.

**Interpretation.** **Two things make this worth having.** First, it covers **eligible populations** — which
patients CGM is for — and that is the question a unit actually faces, because interstitial glucose in shock,
on vasopressors, with oedema or during renal replacement is the setting where the sensor is least trustworthy
and the measurement most wanted. Second, it includes **accessibility and economic cost as a domain of the
consensus itself**, which most Western society documents put in a separate implementation annex or omit.
**A consensus that declines to recommend a technology it cannot cost is more useful than one that recommends
it and leaves the cost to the reader.**

**What cannot be read from the abstract: the grade scheme, if any, and the content of the 27 recommendations.**
The 22 October format-decision rule applies — an expert consensus is not obliged to carry a grade
distribution — **but here the abstract does not say either way, so the disclosure axis is unscored rather
than scored zero.**

## 5. Bloodless surgery, Part 3 — and the missing Part 2

**[Brazilian Society of Surgical Oncology Guideline for Bloodless Surgery (Part 3: Postoperative
Strategies)](https://doi.org/10.1002/jso.70348)** · *Journal of Surgical Oncology* · **published
30 September 2026, indexed 1 October** · paywalled

From the published abstract:

> **Postoperative bloodless care should prioritize judicious laboratory testing to reduce iatrogenic anemia,
> targeted iron replacement, selective use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, individualized hemostatic
> therapy, early recognition and control of hemorrhage, and optimization of oxygen delivery. General
> postoperative measures, including mobilization, analgesia, infection prevention, rehabilitation, and
> discharge planning, should be retained when directly related to anemia tolerance, blood conservation, or
> recovery in patients managed without transfusion.** Recommendations were graded **using an adapted evidence
> classification system and validated through expert consensus**, from a multidisciplinary panel working
> **between June and December 2024**.

**The series is incomplete in the index, and I checked rather than assumed.** A title sweep returns
**Part 1: Preoperative Strategies** (`10.1002/jso.70333`, published 12 July 2026, with a PMCID) and
**Part 3**. **Part 2 — which by the stated structure covers intraoperative management — does not appear in a
25-result title sweep of Europe PMC.** **That is recorded as a named gap, not as a claim that Part 2 does not
exist**, under the standing rule that a search establishes identity and not status.

**Interpretation.** **The clinically sharp recommendation here is the first one: judicious laboratory testing
to reduce iatrogenic anaemia.** In a patient who will not be transfused, the blood drawn for monitoring is
itself a loss, and a guideline that opens its postoperative section by naming phlebotomy as a cause of the
problem it is managing has its priorities in the right order. **The archive's own 20 October stress-ulcer
entry and this one meet at the same point** — the harms of routine ICU process, counted.

**What is thin: "adapted evidence classification system" is not a named scheme**, and no grade distribution is
given. **Under the 22 October rule I read the design section before scoring the disclosure axis — and here the
document does claim a grading system, so the absence of its distribution is a withholding rather than a
category difference.** **That is the first clear instance this archive has logged of the withholding branch of
that rule.**

## 6. Inguinal lymphadenectomy — consensus on 51 of 86 statements

**[Project international Delphi consensus recommendations for perioperative management of inguinal
lymphadenectomy in cancer patients](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.urolonc.2026.09.018)** · *Urologic Oncology* ·
**published 16 October 2026, indexed 19 October** · paywalled

**101 experts from 25 countries**, two electronic rounds between September 2025 and March 2026, **86 clinical
statements across 6 domains**, consensus defined a priori as at least 70% agreement. From the abstract:

> **Consensus was achieved for 51 of 86 statements. Agreed recommendations supported standardized approaches
> to preoperative nutritional assessment and anemia correction, prevention and management of lymphatic
> complications, drain placement and management, discharge criteria, antibiotic prophylaxis,
> thromboprophylaxis, multimodal analgesia, and early functional rehabilitation. No consensus was reached
> regarding routine use of immunonutrition, optimal duration of antibiotic therapy, topical treatments,
> muscle relaxants, or hyperbaric oxygen therapy.**

**51 of 86 is 59.3%, and 35 statements failed to reach consensus.** **That ratio is the document's most useful
output and the part a reader is most likely to skip.** This archive's practice is to read the
no-consensus list as data: here it contains **optimal duration of antibiotic therapy** — the single most
consequential stewardship question in the whole document — alongside immunonutrition and hyperbaric oxygen.
**A panel of 101 experts could agree that prophylaxis should be given and could not agree for how long.**

**Interpretation.** **In scope for this archive through its anaesthesia content**: thromboprophylaxis,
multimodal analgesia, preoperative anaemia correction and discharge criteria are perioperative medicine
whatever specialty is holding the knife. **The caution is the one every Delphi carries and this one states
honestly: "Prospective validation is required."** A 70% agreement threshold means a statement can enter the
recommendation set with 30 of 101 experts against it, and the document does not report the agreement
distribution for the 51 that passed. **Where the margins were is not knowable from the abstract.**

## The remaining two misses, and what the audit changes

**The other two in-scope misses are both abstract-free guideline commentary**, and both join the named gaps:
**insights from the AATS 2025 expert consensus on surgical management of mitral annular calcification**
(`10.21037/jovs-2026-1-0009`, *Journal of Visualized Surgery*, indexed 19 October) and
**"The clinical side of Icarus — hubris, overconfidence, and sepsis guidelines"** (`10.1016/j.jcrc.2026.155758`,
*Journal of Critical Care*, indexed 2 October). **The second is the fifth document this archive has found
commenting critically on the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines**, after the overinterpretation
caution, the emergency-medicine harmonisation, the hospitalist highlights and the reinforcement-learning
discordance paper. **A guideline that attracts five commentaries in one month is itself a finding.**

**Three changes to practice come out of this audit.**

**One: run the index sweep alongside the society route, not after it.** The EACTS document settles the
argument. **Nine consecutive days of society searches producing no lead, and a month audit producing a
tracked-society consensus the searches missed, are the same fact seen twice.**

**Two: sweep a month as a range.** Established this morning, confirmed here.

**Three: the audit cadence is right and the aperture is wider than it was.** **39 of 47, against 10 of 18 —
the coverage improved while the population it had to cover grew by a factor of 2.6.** The next audit falls due
at the start of December.
