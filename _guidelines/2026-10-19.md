**Thirteen guideline-class documents entered the database on 18–19 October**, thirteen of thirteen returned.
**Two are in scope and both are published below.** Ingest is running hard — the 19 October index date read
8,717 at 14:04, 12,706 at 14:11 and **18,695 at 14:21**, so these figures are a snapshot of a moving total.

**The eleven out-of-scope documents, recorded so the aperture stays auditable:** an otoneurology myths review
endorsed by SEORL-CCC; a tinnitus clinical practice guideline with its executive summary; the 2026 British
Society for Rheumatology guideline on polymyalgia rheumatica; NCCN pancreatic adenocarcinoma version 4.2026;
the IHPBA global guideline on hepatocellular carcinoma in resource-constrained settings; a bariatric-surgery
artificial-intelligence review; two Polish papers on metabolic risk in patients on antipsychotics; and a
Belgian protocol on unresectable colorectal cancer.

**One in-scope-adjacent document is logged with its defect rather than reported.** The **Society of
Cardiovascular Computed Tomography's 2026 expert consensus update on the interpretation and reporting of
coronary CT angiography** (`10.1016/j.jcct.2026.08.014`, 2 September) **deposits no abstract at all.** The
title establishes that reporting standards were updated; **it does not establish what changed, and this
archive will not guess.** Given that two societies redefined urinary tract infection within the last four
days, a third document altering how a common cardiac investigation is reported is worth having — **it goes on
the needs-a-PDF list rather than into an item.**

## The instrument change, vindicated by the document it found

**On 15 October this watch moved its confirmatory sweep from publication date to index date. Today that
decision found a SFAR guideline, and SFAR is the first society on this watch's list.**

The arithmetic is worth laying out because it is the whole argument. **The guideline was published on
10 October. It was indexed on 18 or 19 October.** The society and acronym sweeps this watch ran in between —
over 1–13 October on the 15th, and 10–14 October the same day — were keyed on **publication** date and
therefore searched a window the document's publication date sat inside, **at a time when no record of it
existed.** **A sweep cannot find a record that has not been created, and a publication-date window gives no
second chance: by the time the record appeared, its publication date was already behind the window.**

**That is now eight consecutive in-scope documents found by the index-date route and none by a society route**
— but the previous seven were from societies not on the list, which made the finding about the list's
coverage. **This one is different and sharper: the list contains SFAR, the watch looks for SFAR first every
day, and it still could not have seen this.** **The blind spot was never only about which societies are
tracked. It was about keying a sweep on a date the database assigns before it has the record.**

## 1. SFAR and SOFMER on withdrawing life-sustaining treatment: nine recommendations, six of them expert opinion on purpose

**Society · Published:** **the French Society of Anaesthesia and Intensive Care Medicine (SFAR)** with **the
French Society of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (SOFMER)**, *Anaesthesia Critical Care & Pain
Medicine*, **10 October 2026** — **nine days old**, 0 citations, typed `Practice Guideline`. **Twenty experts;
a formal conflict-of-interest policy enforced throughout; the process conducted independently of any
industrial funding.** PICO questions, PRISMA search, **GRADE** assessment, **GRADE grid voting.**

> **The potential drawbacks of making strong recommendations in the presence of low-quality evidence were
> emphasized.** … **Methods 3 fields were defined: (1) modalities of the LST decision-making process;
> (2) modalities for implementing an LST decision; (3) Relationships with family members, communication,
> disagreements, and conflicts.** …
>
> **Results** … **9 recommendations. Among the formalized recommendations, 1 was found to have a high level of
> evidence (GRADE 1), and 2 had low levels of evidence (GRADE 2). For 6 recommendations, the GRADE®
> methodology could not be fully applied, resulting in an expert's opinion. 2 questions could not be answered
> due to a lack of literature. After 1 round of rating, strong agreement was reached for all the
> recommendations.**

**Interpretation.** **The sentence to read twice is in the Design section, not the Results: "The potential
drawbacks of making strong recommendations in the presence of low-quality evidence were emphasized."** This
archive flagged exactly that hazard three days ago, when the AAP's paediatric UTI guideline reported two strong
recommendations inside a certainty range running from low to very low and the abstract gave no way to tell
whether those two sat at the bottom of it. **Here a panel names the hazard before starting work — and then,
faced with thin evidence on six of its questions, issues six expert opinions rather than six strong
recommendations.** **It did what it said it would do, and the recommendation table is the proof.**

**That table is also the starkest this archive has recorded.** Of **eleven questions attempted, one produced a
recommendation at a high level of evidence.** Two produced low-evidence recommendations. **Six produced
expert opinion because GRADE could not be applied, and two produced nothing at all.** Set against the
catalogue: EAN and ESCMID reached two graded recommendations from sixteen questions on infectious
encephalitis; the ACCP found no high-certainty evidence at all for inpatient tobacco treatment; the AAP's
certainty ran low to very low. **End-of-life decision-making in intensive care is not a field where
randomised evidence is obtainable, and the honest response is to label the opinions as opinions.** **"Strong
agreement reached for all the recommendations after one round" means the experts agreed — not that the
evidence did**, and the document keeps those two things separate.

**The co-sponsorship is a deliberate and unusual choice that repays attention.** **SOFMER is the French
physical medicine and rehabilitation society.** A guideline on withdrawing life-sustaining treatment in
intensive care could plausibly have been written by intensivists alone. **Bringing in rehabilitation medicine
puts the people who know what survival with severe impairment actually looks like into the room where the
prognosis is being discussed** — which is the single most contested input to any withdrawal decision. **It
connects this document to the rehabilitation set this archive assembled in mid-October** — Korean ICU
rehabilitation guidelines, the AHA stroke rehabilitation guideline, and the Brazilian telehealth trial that
found no measurable quality-of-life benefit among survivors. **A withdrawal guideline co-signed by
rehabilitation specialists and a rehabilitation trial showing nothing measurable in survivors are two views of
the same question**, and the archive now holds both.

**The third field is the one clinicians will recognise: "Relationships with family members, communication,
disagreements, and conflicts."** One of three fields, in a document of nine recommendations, devoted entirely
to conflict. **That is an accurate allocation of effort** — these decisions rarely fail on the medical
assessment and frequently fail on the conversation — and it is the part where expert opinion is most
defensible, because there is no trial design that answers how to handle a family's disagreement.

**The limitation, stated plainly: the nine recommendations themselves are not readable here.** The abstract
gives the structure, the methodology, the counts and the grades, and **not one of the recommendations.** For a
document whose content is nine specific statements about withholding and withdrawing treatment, that is the
whole clinical substance behind the publisher. **What is reportable with confidence is the shape of the
evidence and the discipline of the method, and both are worth knowing.**

## 2. Canada on transcatheter mitral and tricuspid intervention, where the real subject is referral

**Societies · Published:** **Canadian Cardiovascular Society, Canadian Society of Cardiovascular Surgery and
Canadian Association of Interventional Cardiology**, *Canadian Journal of Cardiology*, **12 October 2026** —
**seven days old**, 0 citations, typed `Practice Guideline`. A **clinical practice update**, not a guideline.

> The management of mitral valve and tricuspid valve regurgitation has undergone significant transformation
> during the past decade … **transcatheter MV edge-to-edge repair and replacement and transcatheter tricuspid
> edge-to-edge repair and replacement have become important therapeutic tools that provide a less invasive
> alternative to surgery in appropriately selected patients.** … Key topics include the clinical presentation
> of these valve lesions, **the diagnostic evaluation with emphasis on multimodality imaging and
> hemodynamics, criteria for patient selection, and current data on procedural and long-term clinical
> outcomes.** **A central theme is the role of timely referral and structured assessment to avoid missed
> opportunities for intervention.** Optimal outcomes require **active engagement from referring physicians and
> close collaboration within a multidisciplinary heart team** …

**Interpretation.** **A document from three procedural societies whose declared central theme is referral is
telling you where it thinks the problem is, and it is not in the catheter lab.** "Timely referral and
structured assessment to avoid **missed opportunities for intervention**" is a claim that eligible patients
are not reaching the heart team — and it is aimed at referring physicians, who are not the people reading a
transcatheter intervention update. **The audience the document most needs is the one least likely to see it.**

**And it is the second document in three days whose real subject is the pathway rather than the procedure.** On
[16 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-16/) this watch reported the Heart Rhythm Society's
position statement on prior authorization, which argues that payers block
guideline-indicated electrophysiology care. **That document says the obstacle is downstream of the
indication; this one says it is upstream.** **Together they bracket the gap between a recommendation and a
procedure: a patient has to be referred, and then the referral has to be funded.** Neither obstacle is
clinical, and both are being addressed by procedural societies in their own journals — **which is to say, by
the people who can see the shortfall and cannot fix it.**

**On the clinical content, the honest summary is that this is a narrative update and reads like one.** No
recommendation counts, no grades, no evidence-certainty statements — the hallmarks of the "clinical practice
update" class, which this archive has now seen from ASGE and the CCS within two weeks and which sits between a
review and a guideline. **Tricuspid intervention is where the novelty is**: transcatheter tricuspid repair and
replacement are recent enough that patient selection is genuinely unsettled, and a document emphasising
"appropriately selected patients" and "current data on procedural and long-term clinical outcomes" is
acknowledging that. **Mitral edge-to-edge repair is a mature therapy; tricuspid is not, and the update covers
both as though the evidence were comparable.** That is the thing a reader should hold in mind, and the abstract
does not separate them.

[Read the update](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2026.07.372)
