**No new guidelines from tracked societies appeared in the trailing window.** The acronym sweep over 5–12
October returned **nothing in scope** — a preprint on preeclampsia matched `ISTH`, a trial matched `ATS`, and
`SPLF` matched a political-science paper. The confirmatory pubType sweep returned **zero for the eleventh
consecutive day.**

## The overlap test was run, and it refuted the reason for running it

**On 10 October this watch reported that re-sweeping days already covered on 8 October surfaced two in-scope
documents that had not appeared the first time, and concluded that "the frontier keeps filling in behind a
sweep."** Yesterday's entry set the test: sweep **11 September to 5 October**, entirely inside ground already
covered, and see what appears.

**Forty-four documents, and every one of them had already been seen.** Nothing new at all.

**So the 10 October conclusion was wrong, and the alternative explanation is embarrassing and checkable.**
The sweeps were requesting fewer results than the query matched:

| Sweep | Window | Hit count | Page size requested | Records never printed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8 Oct | 1–20 Sep | **72** | **70** | **2** |
| 9 Oct | 21–30 Sep | 20 | 60 | 0 |
| 10 Oct | 21 Aug – 10 Sep | **83** | **80** | **3** |
| 11 Oct | 1–25 Aug | 77 | 80 | 0 |
| **12 Oct** | 11 Sep – 5 Oct | 44 | 80 | 0 |

**The decisive test: re-running the 1–20 September query at page size 100 returns all 72 records, and the
ISHLT social-support consensus statement — the document reported on 10 October as newly surfaced — is among
them, dated 5 September.** **It was inside the window on 8 October and simply not displayed.**

**Stated plainly: there is no evidence of a fill-in-behind-the-sweep effect. There is evidence that I was
silently truncating my own results**, and the overlap I introduced to catch a phenomenon that does not exist
happened to catch the truncation instead. **The rule that follows is mechanical: request a page size above
the hit count, or paginate, and compare `len(results)` against `hitCount` on every sweep.**

**And one real document was lost to it.** The 10 October sweep dropped three records; re-running it at full
page size and checking every DOI against the archive identifies the one that matters:

## 1. Korean guidelines on rehabilitation in critical illness — and they answer today's brief

**Society · Published:** **Korean multidisciplinary panel** of critical care physicians, rehabilitation
specialists, nurses and allied health professionals, *Acute and Critical Care*, **31 August 2026** — six
weeks old, **open access** (PMC13579131). `Guideline`. **Developed de novo**, 15 prioritised questions,
Medline, Embase, Cochrane and **KoreaMed** to 30 November 2024, GRADE, **consensus requiring at least 70%
panel agreement.** **Recovered today from the truncated 10 October sweep.**
Chung, Yeo, Do, Na, Choi, Kang, Kwon, Kim, Park, Lee, Jang, Kang, Koo, Kim, Kim, Kim, Park, Beom, Seo, Sun,
Son, Shin, Ahn, Lee and colleagues

> **Survivors of critical illness commonly experience persistent physical, cognitive, and psychological
> impairments, collectively known as post-intensive care syndrome.** Although ICU rehabilitation is now
> recognized as an important part of recovery, **rehabilitation practices remain inconsistent, and clinical
> practice guidelines adapted to the Korean healthcare context have not been available.** … The panel
> prioritized **15 key clinical questions addressing adult, pediatric, and neonatal ICU rehabilitation** …
>
> **Results** … **15 recommendations** across **early structured rehabilitation, criteria for withholding or
> discontinuing therapy, adjunctive modalities, rehabilitation frequency, inspiratory muscle training,
> swallowing rehabilitation, cognitive and occupational therapy, nutritional support, neonatal and pediatric
> ICU rehabilitation, and post-ICU follow-up care. Thirteen recommendations were conditional, whereas two
> were strong, mainly because they addressed patient safety.**

**Interpretation. Thirteen of fifteen conditional, and the two strong ones are strong because they are about
safety — which the panel says in the Results sentence rather than leaving a reader to work out.** That is the
fourth and cleanest instance of a pattern this archive has been assembling: **the strongest statements in a
guideline tend to be about what not to do, not about what works.** The Chinese lung-biopsy consensus had
exactly one level 1 recommendation and it was a contraindication list; the Brain Trauma Foundation's four
moderate-strength questions were all diagnostic or prognostic; the SFAR liver resection guidelines put 7 of
40 on high-level evidence. **Here it is stated as the reason.**

**The domain list contains one entry that most rehabilitation documents omit: "criteria for withholding or
discontinuing therapy."** A guideline that specifies when to **stop** mobilising a patient is more usable
than one that only urges starting, and it is presumably where at least one of the two strong safety
recommendations sits. **Worth retrieving in full for that reason alone.**

**Now set it against the trial reported in [today's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-12/) and the
juxtaposition is exact.** That trial — a stepped-wedge study of integrated telehealth rehabilitation across
ICU, ward and post-discharge phases in 20 Brazilian ICUs — **found no difference in quality of life among
survivors** (adjusted difference −0.045, −0.138 to 0.045), with its whole-cohort benefit driven by reduced
mortality. **So the largest recent trial of integrated rehabilitation shows nothing measurable in the people
who lived, and a guideline issues fifteen recommendations for the same intervention, thirteen of them
conditional.** **The guideline's conditional grading is the honest response to exactly that evidence base**,
and the two documents are consistent rather than contradictory — which is only visible if you read the
grades.

**Two notes for the archive's own catalogue.** The **70% agreement threshold** is the lowest recorded here:
the Brain Trauma Foundation algorithms used 80%, STS a modified Delphi above 75%, BOOTStraP 70% at subgroup
but **90% at plenary**. **Thresholds are not comparable across documents and belong in any quotation of
"consensus was achieved."** And the scope spans **adult, paediatric and neonatal ICUs in one document**,
which is unusual — most guidance here splits them.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.4266/acc.006630) — open access.

## 2. A transfusion society on the laboratory process, built partly on a registry of its own failures

**Society · Published:** **SFTS**, the French-speaking Blood Transfusion Society, *Transfusion Clinique et
Biologique*, **25 August 2026** — seven weeks old. `Practice Guideline` with English abstract. **A working
group established in 2018**, GRADE systematic review **plus two national surveys.** **The first SFTS document
in this archive**, and promoted from yesterday's queue because transfusion sits beside the ISTH
factor-concentrate guidance reported on 10 October.
Toly-Ndour, Piccione, Magdelaine, Floch, Vinatier, Minon, Alluin, Guinchard, Delugin, Kimmel, Ratié, Boennec,
Petit, Oubouzar, Laget, Bouton, Bakour and colleagues

> **Based on two national surveys — a questionnaire assessing laboratory practices and a registry of cases
> involving suboptimal management associated with inadequate IH monitoring — as well as a systematic review
> of the literature conducted according to the GRADE methodology**, the working group formulated
> recommendations … Particular emphasis was placed on: **the anti-red blood cells antibody screening
> schedule, including the recommendation to perform screening at six months of gestation, irrespective of the
> patient's D (RH1) status**; **screening panels designed to detect the widest possible range of clinically
> significant alloantibodies associated with obstetric risk, ideally including C<sup>w</sup>+ and Kp<sup>a</sup>+
> red blood cells**; **the indications for implementing quantification techniques beyond standard titration,
> notably anti-D microtitration**; **the importance of providing expert interpretative comments alongside
> laboratory results** …; and **the procedures for timely and effective communication of results —
> particularly abnormal findings — to the sampling laboratory, clinicians, and/or transfusion services.**

**Interpretation. The evidence base is the unusual part: a registry of cases of suboptimal management
associated with inadequate monitoring.** Most guidelines are built from the literature plus expert opinion;
**this one is built additionally from documented failures in its own national practice** — which is a more
direct form of evidence about a process than any trial could provide, and the right design for a question
that is about laboratory workflow rather than about treatment effect.

**The recommendation a critical care or anaesthetic reader should take is the last two.** *"The importance of
providing expert interpretative comments alongside laboratory results"* and *"procedures for timely and
effective communication of results — particularly abnormal findings."* **That is a guideline saying the
laboratory's job is not finished when the number is produced** — it must arrive with its interpretation, and
it must arrive at the people who will act on it. **This archive recorded the same principle from the other
direction yesterday**: a bispectral index value means something different depending on which drug produced
it, so **a number without its interpretive frame is not usable.** Here the obligation is placed on the
laboratory rather than the clinician.

**The clinical specifics are precise and auditable:** screening **at six months of gestation regardless of D
status** — a schedule, not a principle — and panels **ideally including Cw+ and Kpa+ cells**, which is a
procurement-level specification of the kind this archive noted yesterday in the endosonography needle
recommendation. **The most usable guideline sentences keep turning out to be the ones that tell a department
what to buy and when to do something.**

**Two limits.** **It is a laboratory-process document**, so a clinician looking for management of
haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn will find risk communication rather than treatment. And **the
working group was established in 2018 for a document published in 2026** — eight years, with regulatory
anchoring to a 2018 French order, so the regulatory frame it assumes is of that vintage.

**On the archive's threads this is the third obstetric document in six days** — the SFMU/SFAR obstetric
emergency guidelines on 7 October, the two-hospital cardio-obstetric pathway on 10 October, and this. **The
obstetric thread, opened on 7 October with a named hole on maternal cardiac arrest, now has emergency care,
elective cardiac planning, and laboratory surveillance — and still nothing on arrest.**

[Read the recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tracli.2026.08.003) — paywalled beyond the abstract.
