15 September, DOI 10.1161/cir.0000000000001463, PMID 42741845), with a full abstract. AHA is
tracked, so it is reported: it argues the **"Hispanic paradox"** — lower cardiovascular mortality
despite higher risk factor burden — **"is increasingly recognized as an oversimplification that

## 1. ISHLT — primary graft dysfunction after heart transplantation, a ten-year update

**Society · Published:** International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation. *The Journal of
Heart and Lung Transplantation*, **13 March 2026** — **six months old**; the consensus conference
itself was held **9 April 2024**
Kobashigawa, Zuckermann, Potena, Ardehali, Berman, Chang, Esmailian, Farr, Hall, Hsich, Khush,
Kittleson, Leprince, Manla, Martin-Suarez, Moayedi, Segovia-Cubero, Shah, Trahanas, MacDonald and
the Consensus Conference participants

**Yesterday's brief makes this the document of the moment.** The
[UNOS analysis of 19,432 heart transplants](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-15/) sent yesterday
found **severe primary graft dysfunction in 10.3% of DCD recipients versus 5.3% of DBD** — nearly
double — alongside identical five-year survival. This is the society document that defines and
grades exactly that complication, and it was revised because the old definitions were failing.

The definition and the problem:

> Primary graft dysfunction, defined as **ventricular dysfunction occurring within 24 hours after
> heart transplantation in the absence of identifiable secondary causes**, remains a **major
> contributor to early post-transplant mortality.**

What the conference was convened to fix — and this is an unusually frank list of failures:

- **"Existing risk-stratification tools have limitations and perform poorly in predicting PGD in
  the contemporary era"**
- **"Prevention and management strategies of PGD remain variable, largely influenced by
  center-specific practices and the lack of objective data"**
- **"Vasoplegia often co-exists with PGD, and their complex interplay remains incompletely
  understood"**
- The consensus definition and grading were **"established more than a decade ago"** and needed
  reassessment

What has changed in ten years, per the abstract: growing understanding of the **burden** of PGD
and the **clinical relevance of classification and severity grading**; work on **donor-,
recipient- and procedural-related risk factors and biomarkers**; **significant advancements in
donor heart preservation and procurement technologies**; and in the **utilisation and outcomes of
mechanical circulatory support for severe PGD**.

Its own stated modesty: *"The nature of this consensus is to establish a foundation and pave the
way for future studies."*

**Interpretation.** Three points, and the first is the one to act on.

**"Existing risk-stratification tools perform poorly in the contemporary era" is the sentence that
matters, and the reason is visible in yesterday's data.** The era changed: **DCD now supplies
13.3% of US adult heart transplants**, built in roughly six years, and DCD hearts carry nearly
double the severe PGD rate. A risk score calibrated on a DBD-dominated population is being applied
to a population that is one-eighth DCD and rising. That is the same failure this archive documented
in the [STS-ACSD reoperation model](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-11/) — a model meeting a
population it was not trained on — and it is why the conference says its own tools no longer work.

**The vasoplegia point is the one most directly relevant to the anaesthetist in the room.** PGD and
vasoplegia coexist, the interplay is *"incompletely understood"*, and they demand opposite
interventions: inotropy and mechanical support for the failing ventricle, vasoconstriction for the
dilated circulation. Distinguishing them post-bypass in a fresh transplant, on transoesophageal
echo and a pulmonary artery catheter, is a real-time clinical problem that a consensus definition
does not solve — and the document says so rather than pretending otherwise.

**"Mechanical circulatory support for managing severe PGD" is where this joins the ISHLT
perioperative ECLS trilogy** covered [on 14 September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-14/).
That trilogy is about lung transplantation; this is about hearts. Between them, the society has
now produced consensus on extracorporeal support at both ends of thoracic transplantation — and
the 10.3% severe PGD figure from yesterday is the size of the population needing it.

*Access note: the abstract is deposited and read in full. **The consensus statements themselves —
the revised definition, the grading, the specific recommendations — are not in it**, and the full
text was not reachable. What is reported here is what the conference set out to do and what it says
is broken, not what it concluded.*

[Read the summary](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healun.2025.12.029) · PMID 41823895

## 2. ISHLT — frailty in candidates for lung transplantation

**Society · Published:** International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation. *The Journal of
Heart and Lung Transplantation*, **12 August 2026** — **one month old**
Anderson, Arcasoy, Benden, Budev, Cano, Chandra, Clancy, Diamond, Dimarakis, Dragnich, Fuller,
Gerovasili, Godinas, Hashmi, Holm, Koons, Kovacs, Lee, Lowery, Maheshwari, Meloni, Montgomery,
Myers, Rosenheck, Sidhu, Singer, Snyder, Tousheed, Varughese, Walsh, **Wilkey** and others

A short abstract that identifies a specific gap in the society's own prior output:

> The ISHLT Consensus document for the selection of lung transplant candidates **recommends
> frailty assessment as part of a comprehensive candidacy evaluation. Missing from this and other
> recent consensus documents were practical recommendations on which frailty measures to consider
> and what to do with the information once obtained.**

**Interpretation.** That second sentence is worth more than most guideline abstracts, because it
names a failure mode that is extremely common and rarely admitted: **a guideline recommends
assessing something without saying how to measure it or what to do with the answer.**

Frailty is the paradigm case. It appears as a recommendation in the
[ASA 2025 practice advisory on older adults](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-07/) covered on
7 September, where frailty assessment is folded into routine preoperative workup; it has appeared
as a covariate in several papers here, including the
[RAPM do-not-resuscitate analgesia study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-12/) whose central
finding depended entirely on frailty stratification. **In none of those is there agreement on
which instrument to use.** A recommendation to "assess frailty" with a dozen non-equivalent
instruments available is closer to a gesture than an instruction.

This document exists to close that gap for one population, and the framing — *"practical and
logistical issues"* — is the right level. Whether it succeeds is not knowable from a 483-character
abstract, and the actual instrument recommendations are not in it.

For anyone assessing a lung transplant candidate, this is the document to obtain. For everyone
else, the useful part is the diagnosis: **check whether your own frailty pathway specifies an
instrument and an action, or only the word.**

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healun.2026.05.033) · PMID 42622599

## 3. ERC Guidelines 2025 — Systems Saving Lives

**Society · Published:** European Resuscitation Council. *Resuscitation*, **October 2025** —
**eleven months old**
Semeraro, Schnaubelt, Olasveengen, Bignami, Böttiger, Fijačko, Gamberini, Hansen, Lockey,
Metelmann B, Metelmann C, Ristagno, van Schuppen, Thilakasiri, Monsieurs and the ERC Systems
Saving Lives Collaborator Group

The fourth ERC 2025 section backfilled here, after the executive summary, adult ALS,
post-resuscitation care, special circumstances and ethics.

Topics, per the abstract: **the Chain of Survival; advocacy; CPR awareness campaigns; Kids Save
Lives; resuscitation in low-resource settings; social media; first responders; EMS organisation for
cardiac arrest; in-hospital cardiac arrest management; cardiac arrest centres; system performance
improvement; survivors and co-survivors; and new technologies and artificial intelligence.**

**Interpretation.** As with every ERC section, **the abstract is a table of contents** — no
recommendations. But the contents list itself is informative, and two entries connect directly to
papers sent in the last week.

**"EMS organisation for cardiac arrest" and "first responders" are what the
[Swedish dispatcher-assisted CPR study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-13/) sent on 13 September
is about** — 3,263 arrests where the dispatcher had already recognised cardiac arrest, and
**43.7% still had at least one barrier**, most commonly **inability to move the patient**, with
30-day survival at **adjusted RR 0.57** when barriers were present. That is a systems failure
occurring after the system worked, and it sits precisely in this chapter's territory.

**"Survivors and co-survivors" is the entry worth noticing**, because it is the part of
resuscitation that has historically had no owner. *Resuscitation* published a paper on
**structured diagnostic strategies for psychological problems in cardiac arrest survivors** on
14 September, describing it as *"an important knowledge gap"* — it surfaced in this week's sweeps
and is on the outstanding list. The word **"co-survivors"** — the family who watched — is a
relatively recent addition to resuscitation vocabulary and its presence in a guideline chapter
heading is itself the news.

**"New technologies and artificial intelligence"** in a resuscitation systems chapter is worth
flagging given [today's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-16/), which carried a multimodal
AI mortality prediction model validated across eight institutions at AUROC 0.84–0.92 — and noted
that nothing in it shows acting on the prediction changes anything. A guidelines chapter naming AI
among systems interventions is ahead of the evidence, which may be appropriate for a forward-looking
document and is worth reading with that in mind.

reachable.*
