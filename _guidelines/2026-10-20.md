## 1. SRLF — atrial fibrillation in the adult ICU: eighteen recommendations, two of them gradeable

**Society · Published:** **Société Française de Médecine Intensive – Réanimation (the French Intensive Care
Society)**, in *Annals of Intensive Care*, **14 September 2026** — **thirty-six days old**, newly indexed.
**Formalized expert recommendations using GRADE. Twenty-four questions**: 21 on new-onset atrial fibrillation,
1 on pre-existing atrial fibrillation, 2 applicable to both.

> Most existing recommendations for atrial fibrillation are derived from **cardiology settings**. However,
> atrial fibrillation is also highly prevalent among patients admitted to intensive care units and is
> associated with adverse clinical outcomes. … **The assessment encompassed the role of transthoracic and
> transesophageal echocardiography, the management of potentially reversible triggers (optimization of fluid
> status, magnesium supplementation, serum potassium targets, and screening for thyroid dysfunction…),
> strategies for mitigating hemodynamic risk (indications and modalities for rhythm and rate control), and the
> management of thrombotic risk (indications, regimens, and duration of therapeutic anticoagulation).**
>
> **Eighteen recommendations were formulated, all of which achieved strong agreement among the expert panel.
> Only two recommendations were supported by a low level of evidence (GRADE 2+), whereas the remaining sixteen
> relied on evidence that could not be classified within the GRADE framework and were therefore issued as
> expert opinions. For six additional aspects of atrial fibrillation management, the experts concluded that
> the available evidence was insufficient to support the formulation of a reliable recommendation.**

**Interpretation.** **The premise is the valuable part and it is stated in the first sentence: the atrial
fibrillation evidence base was built in cardiology clinics, and the patient in front of an intensivist is not
that patient.** New-onset atrial fibrillation in a septic, hypoxaemic, catecholamine-supported patient is
plausibly a different disease from atrial fibrillation in an outpatient — different triggers, different
thrombotic risk, different competing risks of anticoagulation — and twenty-one of the twenty-four questions
here are about exactly that case. **No existing document addressed it. This one does, and the first thing it
establishes is how little is known.**

**Two of eighteen recommendations could be graded. Sixteen are expert opinions, and the stated reason is not
weak evidence but unclassifiable evidence** — the GRADE framework could not be applied at all. **Six further
questions were abandoned because no reliable recommendation was possible.** So of thirty attempted positions,
**two rest on evidence of low quality, sixteen rest on the panel's judgement, and six were left open.**

**This archive's recommendation-structure catalogue now has two French society documents in two days with the
same shape.** Yesterday's SFAR and SOFMER guidelines on withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment
attempted eleven questions, produced one GRADE 1 and two GRADE 2 recommendations, issued six expert opinions
because GRADE could not be applied, and abandoned two questions. **Today: two graded, sixteen expert opinions,
six abandoned.** **The pattern is not that French societies have weaker evidence than others — it is that both
documents report the grade distribution and the abandonments in the abstract, where most societies report
neither.** A reader of either one knows, before opening the PDF, how much of it is evidence and how much is
judgement. **That is a disclosure practice, and it is rarer than it should be.**

**One caution on "all of which achieved strong agreement".** Unanimity across eighteen recommendations, sixteen
of them unsupported by classifiable evidence, is agreement about what to do in the absence of data — which is
useful, and is not evidence. **The two numbers have to be read together: strong agreement is a statement about
the panel, GRADE 2+ is a statement about the literature, and only one of them transfers to another country's
ICU.**

**Clinically, the four domains are well chosen and the reversible-trigger list is the one most likely to change
practice** — fluid status, magnesium, potassium targets, thyroid screening. **Those are cheap, and treating the
trigger rather than the rhythm is the instinct that cardiology-derived guidance does not supply.** The
anticoagulation domain is where the expert-opinion share will matter most, because the competing risk of
bleeding in a critically ill patient is precisely what the outpatient trials excluded.

**A record note, because it nearly hid the document.** **This guideline reached the index as a PMC deposit with
no DOI, no abstract and a publication date of 1 January 2026.** The real DOI
(`10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100159`), the real date and the full abstract exist only in the PMC full-text XML, which
had to be fetched separately. **A sweep that reads search records alone would have logged a French society
guideline as an undatable, abstract-free stub.** **New recovery route for the catalogue: for an open-access
PMC record, the full-text XML carries metadata the search record does not.**

## 2. ADQI and ELSO — how to run CRRT during ECMO, and an honest absence of counts

**Society · Published:** **joint Acute Disease Quality Initiative (ADQI) and Extracorporeal Life Support
Organization (ELSO) consensus conference**, in *Blood Purification*, **12 October 2026** — **eight days old**,
the newest document in today's reading. Workgroup statements from a consensus meeting on **"ECMO, AKI, Fluid
Management and CRRT"**.

> Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a well-established life support technology. However, among
> patients receiving ECMO, **acute kidney injury (AKI) is common, and a substantial proportion receive
> concomitant support with renal replacement therapy (RRT). There are several techniques for combining RRT
> with ECMO in patients, including the use of in-line hemofilters in the ECMO circuit, integrated RRT machine
> circuits into the ECMO circuit and the use of parallel circuits where ECMO and CRRT are provided by discrete
> vascular access.** Enclosed is a summary of work group findings … which **identified priorities, knowledge
> gaps and research opportunities, along with generating key consensus-based statements to guide best practices
> for performing CRRT during ECMO.**

**Interpretation.** **The clinical question is real and under-written: a large share of ECMO patients need renal
replacement, and the three ways of delivering it are mechanically different enough to have different
failure modes.** An in-line haemofilter in the ECMO circuit is simple and gives poor control of dose and
ultrafiltration; an integrated RRT machine spliced into the ECMO circuit gives control at the cost of pressure
alarms fighting each other; parallel circuits with their own vascular access give full control at the cost of
another large-bore cannula in an anticoagulated patient. **A best-practice document that names all three and
says when each is appropriate is worth having, and the author list — Bagshaw, Ronco, Kashani, MacLaren,
Selewski — is the right one to write it.**

**What the abstract does not say is how many statements there are, how they were graded, or whether any
reached agreement.** It says statements were generated. **Compare today's first item, which puts eighteen,
two, sixteen and six in its abstract.** **So this document cannot be placed in the structure catalogue from
its abstract, and that absence is itself the finding: a consensus document whose abstract reports its topics
but not its output.** The full text is needed to know what was actually recommended.

**The deliverable is explicitly part research agenda — "priorities, knowledge gaps and research
opportunities".** **That is the honest form for a field where the comparative evidence between the three
circuit configurations is close to absent.** **A reader should expect guidance on how to do it, not evidence
that one way is better** — and should not mistake a research agenda for a recommendation set.

**Methodological note on finding it.** **Europe PMC types this document `Review`, not `Guideline` or
`Consensus Development Conference`.** The guideline-publication-type sweep missed it entirely; the
title-keyword sweep caught it. **Both routes are now load-bearing, which is the same lesson the index-date
sweep taught on 19 October in a different dimension.**

[Read the statements](https://doi.org/10.1159/bpu/abpag023) · *Blood Purification*

## 3. A French national Delphi builds a hip-fracture bundle, and anaesthetists are the largest voice in it

**Society · Published:** **a 30-member multidisciplinary panel "representative of major French academic
societies"**, in *Anaesthesia, Critical Care & Pain Medicine* — **the SFAR journal** — **13 October 2026**,
**seven days old**. **Two-round modified Delphi**, 34 items, 9-point Likert, **consensus defined as ≥70% of
raters scoring 7–9 for agreement or 1–3 for disagreement**, run on REDCap.

> **Anesthesiologists play a central role in optimizing perioperative care and improving outcomes for patients
> with hip fractures** … We conducted a nationwide modified Delphi study to establish **French expert
> consensus on key components of a standardized care bundle for patients over 75 years old** with HF. …
>
> **The expert panel included 11 (36.7%) anesthesiologists, 9 (30%) geriatricians, 6 (20%) orthopedic surgeons,
> and 4 (13.3%) hospital pharmacists. Agreement consensus was reached for 29 out of 34 proposed items. A
> shortlist of 12 items that achieved over 90% consensus was identified, comprising 2 organizational, 7
> perioperative, and 3 surgical items.** … **Its impact on patient outcomes warrants further evaluation.**

**Interpretation.** **This is a care bundle, not a graded guideline, and the distinction matters more than the
word "consensus" suggests.** There is no evidence appraisal here and the paper does not claim one: 34 items
drawn from the literature were rated by 30 experts, and 29 cleared a 70% agreement threshold. **Nothing in the
output is graded, so there is no way to tell which of the 29 items rest on trials and which rest on the
panel.** **Set beside today's first item — which separates its two gradeable recommendations from its sixteen
expert opinions — this document's 29 items are all of one kind, and that kind is expert opinion.** That is a
legitimate product for a question about *organisation*; it should not be read as evidence about *treatment*.

**The composition is the most interesting number and it is reported, which is good practice.** **Anaesthetists
are the largest single group at 36.7%, ahead of geriatricians at 30% and orthopaedic surgeons at 20%** — for a
document about a fracture. **The paper's opening sentence claims the central perioperative role for
anaesthesiology and the panel is built to match**; a reader who thinks the surgical items deserved more
surgical voices has the numbers to say so. **Four hospital pharmacists on a panel of thirty is also notable,
and consistent with French practice** — SFAR has worked with the Société Française de Pharmacie Clinique on
medication-error prevention, and pharmacy representation on a perioperative bundle follows from that.

**The 12-item shortlist above 90% is the usable part**, and its split — **2 organisational, 7 perioperative, 3
surgical** — says where the strong agreement actually lies. **Seven of the twelve highest-consensus items are
perioperative.** The five items that failed to reach consensus are not named in the abstract, which is the
usual omission: **a Delphi's disagreements are more informative than its agreements, and are almost never in
the abstract.**

**And the last sentence is the honest one: "its impact on patient outcomes warrants further evaluation".** **A
bundle is a protocol that converts assessment into action, which is the second of the three conditions this
archive uses for a monitored variable; the third — an outcome measured by something other than the protocol
itself — is explicitly not met and the authors say so.** **Bundles are easy to comply with and hard to prove.
This one starts by admitting that.**

## 4. The Chinese Society of Critical Care Medicine issues twenty-three stress-ulcer recommendations and does not say how they are graded

**Society · Published:** **Chinese Society of Critical Care Medicine**, in *Journal of Intensive Medicine*,
**28 September 2026** — **twenty-two days old**. **GRADE methodology, 23 evidence-based recommendations.**

> Stress ulcers is a common complication in critically ill patients, associated with increased mortality risk
> and prolonged intensive care unit stay. … **the lack of standardized protocols for the prevention and
> treatment of stress ulcers in clinical practice underscores the urgent need for authoritative guidelines** …
>
> Following the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology, the
> guideline formulation process involved **proposing clinical questions, literature retrieval and screening,
> meta-analysis and evidence synthesis, initial formation of recommendation items, panel reviews, three rounds
> of remote meetings, four rounds of face-to-face meetings, manuscript revisions, and final approval by the
> Standing Committee** … **The guidelines were finalized in the form of 23 evidence-based recommendations.
> These guidelines cover key aspects of stress ulcer management, including risk factors, prevention, treatment,
> and adverse effects of acid-suppressive drugs.**

**Interpretation.** **Twenty-three recommendations called "evidence-based", from the same framework that let
the French panel grade two of eighteen.** The two documents address different questions, so the comparison is
not a verdict on either — **but the Chinese abstract gives no grade distribution at all, and without one the
phrase "evidence-based" is a label rather than a claim a reader can check.** **This is the gap worth naming:
the process is described in more procedural detail than almost any guideline this archive has reported — three
remote meeting rounds, four face-to-face rounds, Standing Committee approval — and the one number that would
let a clinician weigh the output is absent.** **Process transparency and evidence transparency are different
things, and this abstract has a great deal of the first and none of the second.**

**The scope decision that earns the document its place is the fourth topic: adverse effects of
acid-suppressive drugs.** **That is where the live controversy in stress-ulcer prophylaxis actually sits** —
the question is not whether proton pump inhibitors reduce clinically important bleeding but whether the
pneumonia, *Clostridioides difficile* and mortality signals offset it, and large trials have come down on
different sides. **A guideline that treats the harms of its own principal intervention as one of four named
domains is structured correctly**, whatever its grades turn out to be.

**One more thing, and it is about volume.** **The same society published a 34-recommendation clinical practice
guideline on renal replacement therapy in critically ill patients with AKI six weeks earlier** (*Journal of
Intensive Medicine*, 18 August 2026), also GRADE, also with no grade distribution in the abstract — **57
recommendations on two topics in six weeks from one national society.** **That is a rate of production worth
watching rather than judging: it may reflect a deliberate programme to fill gaps in Chinese critical-care
practice, and it may reflect recommendations issued faster than evidence accumulates.** The RRT guideline is on
the readable backlog and will say more.
