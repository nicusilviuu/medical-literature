## 1. The mechanism this archive has been circling for five days, named

**[The latest guidelines for the perioperative management of the thoracic noncardiac patient: a critical
appraisal of the literature](https://doi.org/10.1097/aco.0000000000001718)** — *Current Opinion in
Anaesthesiology*, **first published 29 October 2026**.

**A routing correction first, because it is mine.** **Yesterday's brief logged this document to the literature
queue, writing that a review of guidelines "belongs here" rather than in the watch. That was inconsistent with
how this archive has always treated such material:** the ESC guideline commentaries, the JCVA summary of what
the 2026 stroke guidelines changed, and the Italian editorial on the ESC cardiac rehabilitation guidelines are
all carried in the **guidelines** backlog, not the brief. **Commentary on guidelines is guideline-tracking
material, and the rule is now stated so it stops drifting: a document whose subject is what guidelines say or
how they have changed belongs to this watch, whatever journal it appears in.** It is published here.

**The problem, as the review states it:**

> The management of patients before, during, and after surgical procedures on the thorax is influenced by **a
> rapidly expanding set of guidelines that are only partially overlapping.** The review is intended for
> clinicians who are interested in understanding **how publication date influences their recommendations**,
> and the points of agreement and divergence between sources.

**And the finding, which is the most useful thing this watch has read all week:**

| | What the guidelines do |
| --- | --- |
| **Converge strongly** | **Process measures** — multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia, **a single chest drain**, early ambulation, **avoidance of reflex ICU admission** |
| **Diverge** | **Every number** — exercise-testing cut-offs, transfusion triggers, drain-removal volumes |
| **Drift with vintage** | The most recent documents shift toward **more permissive values**, and toward frailty, sarcopenia and patient-reported outcomes |

**Interpretation, and this is why it matters beyond thoracic surgery.** **Four of this archive's last five
entries have reported guidelines disagreeing without being able to say what kind of thing the disagreement
is.** On [26 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/) STS and APHRS inverted each other's
recommendation classes for the same two drugs. On
[27 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-27/) a four-society panel wrote a crosswalk whose stated
reason for existing was that society guidelines do not align. On
[29 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-29/) an Italian surgical working group read down a Class I
recommendation its own international society co-authored. **Today's review supplies the decomposition: the
processes agree and the numbers do not, and the numbers move with publication date.**

**That is a far more tractable description of the problem than "guidelines disagree."** **A clinician can act
on it.** If the convergent content is the process — opioid-sparing analgesia, one drain, early mobilisation,
no automatic ICU bed — then those parts of a guideline can be adopted from any recent source. **It is the
thresholds that carry a date stamp**, and the review's instruction is exactly right:

> **It is imperative for clinicians to ascertain the underlying guideline generation for any numerical
> threshold before its implementation.**

**The second quotable sentence is the one that should temper the first.** *"A significant number of
intraoperative decisions are still made based on expert opinion rather than guideline-grade evidence."*
**So the convergence on process measures should not be mistaken for convergence on evidence** — guidelines can
agree because they copied one another, and this archive's structure catalogue exists partly to tell the two
apart. **The review does not resolve that, and does not claim to.**

**One limitation worth naming: this is a narrative critical appraisal, and it names neither the guidelines it
reviewed nor how many.** *"The reviewed documents"* is as specific as the abstract gets. **A paper whose
central finding is that sources diverge should list its sources**, and a reader cannot check the convergence
claim or the vintage claim without them. **The finding is credible and the audit trail is absent.**

## 2. Thirteen graded indications, a third grading scheme, and a systematic search that closed thirteen months early

**Society · Published:** **North American Neuromodulation Society**, *Neuromodulation*, **17 September 2026**
— six weeks old, and never reported here. **Multidisciplinary expert panel with stated conflict-of-interest
governance**; systematic search of Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL and Scopus; modified Delphi;
**graded on the United States Preventive Services Task Force recommendation grades and levels of certainty.**

**The structure is the notable part: recommendations are stratified by anatomical region** — craniofacial and
neck, upper extremity, lower extremity, truncal, pelvic — **and then by indication within region.**

| Grade | Indications |
| --- | --- |
| **A (4)** | **Hemiplegic shoulder pain · lower-extremity post-amputation pain · nonsurgical axial low back pain · low back pain associated with multifidus dysfunction** |
| **B (9)** | Cluster headache · facial herpes zoster-related neuralgia · occipital neuralgia · trigeminal neuropathic pain · shoulder osteoarthritis · chronic post-surgical knee pain · lower-extremity mononeuropathy · cluneal neuralgia · pudendal neuralgia |
| **Insufficient or small net benefit** | *"several other nociplastic and visceral pain conditions"* |

**Thirteen graded indications**, and a condition attached to the offer: implantable PNS *"may be offered …
after conservative measures have failed to provide meaningful relief for at least three months."*

**Interpretation.** **Three things make this a good document and one makes it an uncomfortable one.**

**First, the grading.** **This is the third grading scheme in this archive's recommendation-structure
catalogue** — GRADE, the ACC/AHA class-and-level pairs, and now **USPSTF grades with levels of certainty.**
**And it is used for its proper purpose: discriminating between indications for one intervention rather than
between interventions.** Set against this week's other end of the range — **the four ungraded consensus
documents of [28 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-28/)** — a document that assigns a grade to
each of thirteen named indications and declares several others insufficient is doing the harder and more
useful thing.

**Second, the organising axis.** **Recommendations indexed by anatomy, then by syndrome, is the same
structural idea as yesterday's GIHP-SFAR procedure-specific bleeding-risk tables** — recommendations indexed
by *where* rather than by *what*. **Two documents two days apart choosing anatomy as the axis, and in both
cases for the same reason: the clinician arrives with a location, not a question.**

**Third, the honest negatives.** *"Functional outcomes were reported inconsistently across studies"*;
clinical and methodological heterogeneity observed; **"the durability of relief varied by indication and
device type, limiting the certainty of evidence."** **Durability is the question for an implanted device, and
the guideline says the evidence on it is weak — for a document explicitly written to "inform clinical practice
and payer policy", declaring that is to its credit.**

**And now the uncomfortable detail, which the abstract reports and does not address.** **The systematic
literature search was conducted "through July 1, 2025", with "an additional manual search conducted on
July 30, 2026."** **So the systematic search closed 394 days — thirteen months — before the manual top-up,
and 443 days before publication.** **A manual search on a single day is not a systematic search**, and
thirteen months of a fast-moving device literature was covered by one. **For a guideline whose own conclusion
calls for "neural target- and pain syndrome-specific randomized clinical trials", the trials published in
those thirteen months are precisely the ones most likely to move a grade.**

**Archive context: implantable peripheral nerve stimulation has appeared here once, and in the opposite
direction.** The [17 September brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-17/) recorded the position that a
neuromodulation device must clear a higher bar than a cheap drug — *"a neuromodulation device beats 8 mg of
dexamethasone, and the burden is on the device."* **This guideline is the device side's best case, graded, and
it concedes durability.** **The two together are the honest state of the question.**

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neurom.2026.09.002) · *Neuromodulation*

## 3. A title claiming a consensus that its own text calls a perspective — second instance in two days

**[Teaching Loss of Resistance to Trainees: A Stepwise Expert-Consensus Approach to Developing Tactile
Competence in Epidural Procedures](https://doi.org/10.52965/001c.172702)** — *Orthopedic Reviews*,
**first published 26 October 2026**.

**The problem it addresses is real and specific to this specialty.** Loss of resistance remains the commonest
method of identifying the epidural space, **and its endpoint is tactile** — *"Written descriptions and
procedural demonstrations can explain the concept but cannot fully communicate the sensation of resistance
followed by its characteristic reduction."*

**And the pedagogical argument is a genuinely good one:**

> allowing an inexperienced trainee to **simultaneously advance the epidural needle and interpret an
> unfamiliar tactile endpoint** may unnecessarily combine two distinct learning tasks.

**The staged method:** focused teaching on epidural anatomy and the concept; observation of experienced
operators; then **a transitional stage in which the supervising physician retains complete control of needle
advancement while the trainee operates the loss-of-resistance syringe and concentrates solely on identifying
the tactile endpoint**; then progressive transfer of needle control under direct supervision.

**Interpretation.** **The transitional stage is the idea, and it is a good one.** Splitting one procedure into
two learning tasks, and keeping the hazardous half with the supervisor while the trainee learns the
perceptual half, is a specific and implementable change with an obvious safety rationale. **Anyone who has
taught epidural placement will recognise the problem it solves.**

**But the genre must be stated, because the title overclaims.** **The text calls itself "this expert
educational perspective" and describes "a progressive approach used by the authors".** It says *"We
believe…"*. **There is no panel, no Delphi, no voting threshold, no statement count, no grading, and no
literature search — none of the apparatus the words "Expert-Consensus" in the title imply.** **It is a
well-argued teaching perspective by its authors and should be read and cited as that.**

**This is the second instance in two days.** The [29 October entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-29/)
reported an Italian cardiac surgery working group document **titled "Expert Review of Evidence and Consensus
Guidelines" whose own methods described "a narrative, evidence-based synthesis."** **Two documents, two days,
both claiming a consensus in the title and describing something else in the text.** **The check is cheap and
is now standing: read the methods sentence against the title before filing anything as a consensus.**

**Archive context: epidural technique teaching has not appeared here, and nor has any document on acquiring a
tactile skill.** **It is the first entry of its kind, which is part of why the genre label matters** — if the
catalogue is going to hold educational documents, it should not record this one as a consensus.

[Read the perspective](https://doi.org/10.52965/001c.172702) · *Orthopedic Reviews*
