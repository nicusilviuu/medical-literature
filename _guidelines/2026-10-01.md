**Yesterday this watch closed the SFAR gap and wrote down why it had existed. Half of that explanation
was wrong, and today's sweep found it out within an hour.**

The note said Europe PMC "genuinely cannot find these documents" and generalised it to the French
societies. That is true of SFAR, whose RFEs are not deposited. It is **flatly false of SRLF**, which
publishes its guideline programme in *Annals of Intensive Care* — **open access, in PubMed Central, with
PDFs.** A single query returns **24 SRLF records**, most of them in PMC, going back four years. Nothing
was hidden; nobody had looked.

The reason yesterday's French sweep returned zero was the **window**, not the index: it covered 27–30
September 2026, and these documents are from March 2026, July 2025 and earlier. **A four-day trailing
window cannot find a guideline programme that publishes a few documents a year.** That is a different
failure from SFAR's, and it needed a different fix.

Three SRLF documents are reported below, oldest-first dates stated plainly, plus the one genuinely new
item in today's sweep.

## 1. SRLF / SFC / SFAR / SFCTCV — management of adult cardiogenic shock

**Published:** *Annals of Intensive Care*, **31 March 2026** — **six months old**, 3 citations, **open
access and in PubMed Central**. Co-published in *Archives of Cardiovascular Diseases* on **23 March 2026**
(10.1016/j.acvd.2026.02.001). **Four societies**: the French intensive care, cardiology, anaesthesia and
thoracic/cardiovascular surgery societies.
Aissaoui, Delmas, Merdji, Schurtz, Baudry, Beurton, Boissier, Bonello, Cholley, Combaret, Combes, David,
De Backer, Guinot et al.

> **The last specific international European recommendations regarding the management of cardiogenic shock
> (CS) regardless of the etiology were issued over 10 years ago.** We present herein recommendations for
> the management of CS in adults, developed using the GRADE system by an expert group from the French
> Intensive Care Society (SRLF) and the French Society of Cardiology (SFC), with the participation of the
> French Society of Anesthesia and Intensive Care (SFAR) and the French Society of Thoracic and
> Cardiovascular Surgery (SFCTCV).
>
> The recommendations covered **six fields of application: CS teams and expert centers, symptomatic
> medical management, etiological management, organ support, temporary circulatory support and
> de-escalation and early post-CS management.** Twenty-three PICO questions were identified, leading to
> **41 recommendations**… **Seven recommendations were scored with high level of evidence (Grade 1), 11
> with moderate level of evidence (Grade 2) and 17 with low level of evidence (Expert opinion). In 6
> cases, the experts were not able to give an answer.** All of the recommendations obtained strong
> agreement…
>
> The experts highlight that optimal management of CS requires **a structured, multidisciplinary shock
> team and regional referral network, applying standardized protocols for diagnosis and staging. Early
> etiological treatment — such as culprit-lesion revascularization or urgent valve intervention — is
> central to improve outcomes. Hemodynamic support should prioritize norepinephrine as first-line
> vasopressor and privilege selective inotrope use. Temporary mechanical circulatory support (Impella,
> VA-ECMO) should be reserved for carefully selected patients following discussion by the expert team.**

**Interpretation.** This is the most substantial document in today's entry and the one with the most
immediately usable content, because unlike most society summaries **the abstract actually states the
recommendations** rather than only listing the fields they cover.

**It completes a pair this archive opened five weeks ago.** On 30 August the watch reported the
[ESC ACVC/HFA/EAPCI criteria for diagnosing cardiogenic
shock](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-08-30/) — a document whose entire purpose was to define the
syndrome, and whose premise, as that entry put it, was that "cardiogenic shock" had named too many
different things to be studied properly. **Here is the other half: having been defined in August, it is
now told how to be treated.** That sequence — definition first, then management — is the pattern this
archive named on 29 September as the legitimate response of a field that cannot yet treat something well.
This is what it looks like when the sequence completes.

**Four specific things in the recommendations are worth pulling out.**

**Norepinephrine first-line, inotropes selectively.** This is a pressure-and-perfusion recommendation in
the week this archive has spent reporting the failure of pressure targets, and it points the other way
from a naive reading of those results: the recommendation is not "target a number" but "choose this drug
and use the other class sparingly." Worth holding against the [DO₂i and
noradrenaline/microcirculation findings in the perfusion-not-pressure
thread](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/).

**Mechanical support reserved for carefully selected patients after expert-team discussion.** That is a
restraint recommendation, and it lands squarely in the ECMO-candidacy thread this archive has been
following since 22 September. It also bears on the Impella data reported here on
[27 September](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-27/) — in-hospital cardiac arrest within
Impella-supported cardiogenic shock, from the J-PVAD registry. **A guideline saying "carefully selected"
and a registry showing what happens to the selected are the two halves of the same question.**

**Shock teams and regional networks come first among the six fields** — ahead of any drug or device. The
2015 predecessor said something similar, and that it is still the lead recommendation eleven years later
is either a durable insight or an admission that it has not been implemented.

**Seven of 41 recommendations at high evidence, 17 at expert opinion, and six questions the panel could
not answer at all.** The six refusals are the most informative number in the document: a panel of four
societies with 23 PICO questions conceded on a quarter of them.

**The 2015 comparison is available and instructive**, because the predecessor is the same title in the
same journal (10.1186/s13613-015-0052-1, now **78 citations**), retrieved in the same search. It covered
**15 fields of application**; this one covers **six**. The older document ranged across epidemiology,
poisoning, prehospital care, cardiac arrest and end-stage failure; the new one consolidates into teams,
symptomatic management, aetiology, organ support, circulatory support and de-escalation. **The newer
document is narrower and graded; the older one was broader and, from its abstract, ungraded.** Fewer
fields with explicit evidence levels is the better trade, and it is the same contraction SFAR's
in-hospital emergencies panel described making deliberately.

[Read the recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aicoj.2026.100038)

## 2. SRLF / SFNCM / GFRUP — nutritional support in the intensive care unit

**Published:** *Annals of Intensive Care*, **15 July 2025** — **fourteen months old**, and the most cited
document in today's entry (**19 citations**). Open access, in PubMed Central.
Reignier, Gaillard-Le Roux, Dequin, Bertoni Maluf, Bohe, Casaer, Delbove, Dupuis, Fontaine, Gamon,
Grange, Heming, Jezequel et al.

> The objective … was to develop guidelines for nutritional support in **critically ill adults and
> children (excluding neonates and burn patients) unable to maintain an adequate oral intake.** … Experts
> from the French Intensive Care Society (SRLF), **the French Society of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism
> (SFNCM)**, and the French-Speaking Group of Pediatric Emergency Physicians and Intensivists (GFRUP) used
> the GRADE methodology… **Twenty-four PICO questions were identified, resulting in 34 adult and 29
> pediatric recommendations.** Of the 34 recommendations for adults, **three were based on high-level
> evidence, 12 on moderate-level evidence, and 19 on expert opinion.** The corresponding numbers for the
> 29 pediatric recommendations were **one, five, and 23.** All recommendations achieved strong agreement…

**Interpretation.** Reported today because **yesterday's entry carried the SFAR perioperative and critical
care nutrition RFE**, and the two are not the same document despite sharing a topic and a co-author
society. **SFNCM co-signed both** — the same French clinical nutrition society, working with the
anaesthetists in 2026 and with the intensivists in 2025.

**The comparison is the reason to report this, and it sharpens a rule this archive wrote yesterday.**

| Document | Scope | Recommendations | High evidence | Expert opinion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [SFAR / SFNCM, 3 Sep 2026](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/) | **Perioperative + critical care** | 40 | **11** | 16 |
| SRLF / SFNCM, 15 Jul 2025 — adults | **Critical care only** | 34 | **3** | 19 |
| SRLF / SFNCM, 15 Jul 2025 — children | **Paediatric critical care** | 29 | **1** | **23** |

Yesterday's note said GRADE levels map where randomised evidence exists rather than clinical importance.
**This is the same observation inside a single topic, with the same nutrition society involved
throughout.** Nutritional support looks comparatively well-evidenced when a document covers the
perioperative period as well — **11 of 40 at high certainty** — and markedly less so when the scope is
narrowed to critical care alone: **3 of 34.** The likeliest explanation is that elective surgical
nutrition can be randomised on a schedule and critical illness cannot. **I have not verified that the
high-certainty SFAR recommendations are the perioperative ones** — the abstracts do not break the grading
down by field — so that is my inference and the full texts would settle it.

**The paediatric column is the finding that should not be buried. One recommendation of 29 rests on
high-level evidence and 23 are expert opinion** — the thinnest evidence base in any document this archive
has catalogued, below even the SFAR in-hospital emergencies RFE. Feeding critically ill children is
routine, daily, and almost entirely unevidenced. The panel reports this plainly rather than rounding it up.

Note also the **exclusions, which are doing real work**: neonates and burn patients are out. Those are two
of the populations where nutritional support is most aggressive and most debated, and a reader applying
these recommendations to a burns unit would be going outside the document.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13613-025-01509-0)

## 3. SRLF / GFRUP — renal replacement therapy in the intensive care unit

**Published:** *Annals of Intensive Care*, **16 July 2025** — **fourteen months old**, 8 citations, open
access and in PubMed Central. From a consensus conference held in **November 2024**.
Jourdain, Chatti, Housni, Jaquet, Jezequel, Kane, La Combe, Landais, Marzouk, de Montmollin, Mortamet,
Nay, Salmon-Gandonnière et al.

> **Although largely used, the place of extracorporeal renal replacement therapy (RRT) in acute kidney
> injury (AKI) in intensive care unit (ICU) patients has yet to be clarified.** …
>
> **Methods** A committee, **without any conflict of interest on the subject**, defined seven generic
> questions… An independent work group reviewed literature using predefined keywords. The quality of the
> data was assessed using the GRADE methodology. **Eighteen experts … proposed their own answers in a
> public session and answered questions from the jury (a panel of 14 critical-care medicine physicians and
> a nurse) and the public. The jury then met for 48 h to write out and vote on its recommendations.**
>
> **Results** The panel provided **45 statements** addressing seven questions… (1) **indications for RRT,
> when should it be initiated, and within what timeframe?** (2) advantages/disadvantages of the different
> **RRT modalities** and how to choose? (3) **which dose of dialysis**? (4) how to prescribe, adjust and
> monitor each technique? (5) **which vascular access** (insertion site, catheter type and length)? (6)
> **how to prevent circuit thrombosis?** (7) **criteria to consider weaning from RRT** and how?

**Interpretation.** **The methodology is the unusual part and deserves describing, because it is not how
most of the documents in this archive were made.** This is a *conférence de consensus* in the older
French sense: eighteen topic experts argue their answers **in a public session**, are cross-examined by a
jury that does not consist of topic experts — **14 intensivists and a nurse** — and then that jury, not
the experts, retires for **48 hours** to write and vote the recommendations. The committee setting the
questions was required to have **no conflict of interest on the subject.**

**That design deliberately separates the people who know the field from the people who write the
recommendations.** It trades some expertise for some independence, and it is close to the opposite of the
modified-Delphi-among-experts approach used by most documents reported here, including every ISHLT and
ERC document in this archive. **Which approach produces better recommendations is not something this
archive can answer**, but it is worth noticing that the question exists — guideline methodology is
usually reported as a formality and these two designs embed genuinely different assumptions about whose
judgement to trust.

**Clinically, the seven questions are exactly the practical ones**, and question 3 is live here: *Critical
Care* published **"lower limits of CRRT dose-intensity: what does the evidence support?"** on 28 September,
logged in [yesterday's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-30/). A 2024 consensus answering "which
dose of dialysis should be prescribed" and a 2026 review asking how low the dose can go are the same
argument a year apart. Question 6 — circuit thrombosis — connects to the citrate anticoagulation item
logged in [today's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-01/).

**One limit on what can be said here.** The abstract gives the seven questions and the count — **45
statements** — but **not one actual recommendation.** Unlike the cardiogenic shock document, this summary
is scope-only. **The full text is open access and in PMC**, so unlike the ERC and ISHLT documents this one
is genuinely retrievable; it is queued for a full read rather than reported as unreachable.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13613-025-01517-0)

## 4. ACC — nutrition for prevention and management of cardiovascular disease

**Published:** *Journal of the American College of Cardiology*, **30 September 2026** — **the only
genuinely new document in today's sweep.** 2026 ACC Scientific Statement. Not in PMC; abstract only.
Hu, Beleslin, Blaha, Kris-Etherton, Seligman, Williams

> Nutrition is a foundational determinant of cardiovascular health… **Contemporary evidence supports a
> shift in nutrition guidance from isolated nutrients toward whole foods and dietary patterns that
> emphasize overall dietary quality, food choice, and degree of processing.** …
>
> Evidence … **consistently supports replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fats, increasing
> consumption of dietary fiber and whole grains, reducing consumption of sodium and ultra-processed foods
> (particularly sugar-sweetened beverages and processed meats), and greater adherence to healthy dietary
> patterns**… **Mediterranean, DASH, vegetarian, and other plant-rich dietary patterns** demonstrate
> favorable effects on blood pressure, lipids, glycemic control, and body weight…
>
> Translation … requires integration of **dietary counseling, medical nutrition therapy, Food Is Medicine
> programs, public policy initiatives, and clinician education.**

**Interpretation.** **This is in the archive's secondary scope at best, and the honest thing to say is
that it shares a word with items 2 and the SFAR RFE rather than a subject.** The ACC statement is about
**diet** — what populations and outpatients should eat to lower cardiovascular risk over years. The SRLF
and SFAR documents are about **nutritional support** — how to feed a patient who cannot eat. Those are
different clinical problems, with different evidence bases and different failure modes, and a reader who
saw "three nutrition guidelines this week" would be misled.

It is reported because it is a tracked-society scientific statement published this week, and because the
contrast is itself informative. **Note what the ACC statement does not contain: a recommendation count,
a grading scheme, or any statement of evidence certainty.** Compare the French documents, which say
exactly how many recommendations rest on what quality of evidence and how many questions they could not
answer. **A scientific statement is a different instrument from a formalised recommendation and should not
be read as one** — which is also why its confident "consistently supports" language is not comparable to
a GRADE 1.

The substantive shift it describes — **from isolated nutrients to whole dietary patterns and degree of
processing** — is a real change in how the field reasons, and the inclusion of **food insecurity and "Food
Is Medicine" programmes** alongside dietary counselling puts social determinants inside a cardiology
statement rather than beside it. Neither point is actionable in an operating theatre or an ICU, which is
why this is item 4 and not item 1.

[Read the statement](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.08.025)

### Correction to yesterday's entry: the arithmetic resolves

Yesterday's entry flagged that the SFAR in-hospital emergencies RFE stated **36 recommendations** while
its GRADE breakdown summed to **32**, and said the summary did not reconcile it and that I would not
guess. **Having four more documents to compare, the pattern is clear and the gap is a counting
convention, not an error.**

| Document | Stated | Graded (G1+G2+expert) | Questions with no recommendation | Graded + unanswered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SFAR airway, 3 Sep 2026 | 27 | **27** | 1 | 28 |
| SFAR in-hospital emergencies, 3 Sep 2026 | 36 | 32 | 4 | **36** |
| SFAR nutrition, 3 Sep 2026 | 40 | 36 | 3 + 1 regulatory | **40** |
| SRLF nutrition (adults), 15 Jul 2025 | 34 | **34** | 0 | 34 |
| SRLF nutrition (children), 15 Jul 2025 | 29 | **29** | 0 | 29 |
| SRLF cardiogenic shock, 31 Mar 2026 | 41 | 35 | 6 | **41** |

**Every document reconciles.** Where there are unanswered questions, the stated total includes them; where
there are none, the graded counts close on their own. The one apparent exception, the SFAR airway
document, closes on the graded counts alone and reports its unanswered question separately.

**So yesterday's flag was a real observation and the wrong conclusion was available from it** — the
document was not inconsistent, and a reader of yesterday's page could reasonably have inferred sloppiness
that is not there. **This is an inference from a consistent pattern across six documents, not a convention
any of them states**, and the full texts would confirm it. Recorded here rather than silently, and a
correction notice has been added to yesterday's entry.

### What the SRLF seam still holds

A single Europe PMC query returned **24 SRLF records** over four years. Beyond the three reported above,
these are in scope and never covered — **all open access in PMC unless noted:**

- **Guidelines for the Initial Assessment of Respiratory Distress in the Emergency Department** — 20 Jan
  2026, 2,534-char abstract. **The newest SRLF guideline after cardiogenic shock.**
- **Oxygen therapy in acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, SRLF–SFMU guidelines** — 5 Sep 2024, **37
  citations**. The most cited SRLF guideline found.
- **Formal guidelines from an expert panel: intensive care unit medical staffing and organisation** — 20
  Jan 2025.
- **The place of new antibiotics for Gram-negative bacterial infections in intensive care** — 4 Jul 2023,
  15 citations, with a companion **rationale and evidence** paper (18 Jul 2023, **51 citations**).
  Secondary scope, and the only route this archive has yet found into francophone infectious-disease
  guidance.
- **Update of guidelines for management of community-acquired pneumonia in adults** — 25 Feb 2025, not in
  PMC.
- **Neuromuscular blockade and its monitoring in the intensive care unit** — 22 Oct 2025, 2,977 chars.
  Title suggests a multicentre study rather than a guideline; **check the pubType before writing it up.**
- From the society website and **not** found in the Europe PMC sweep: an **acute encephalopathy in the ICU
  expert consensus** (page dated 28 March 2025, **39 recommendations**, GRADE, covering clinical scores,
  EEG and neuromonitoring) and an **RFE on sickle cell disease**. The encephalopathy document needs its
  publication located.

**One methodological warning for whoever works this list.** The society's own
`/recommandations-referentiels-epp` page is **incomplete**: it showed six documents, while the sidebar of
an individual article surfaced three more (cardiogenic shock, nutrition, sickle cell) that the listing did
not. **Do not treat a society index page as a catalogue** — cross-check it against a Europe PMC query, and
cross-check the query against the index, because today each one found documents the other missed.
