## 1. ILCOR 2026: six task forces, 989 characters

**[2026 International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Consensus on Science With Treatment
Recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2026.111328)** · **ILCOR** · *Resuscitation* ·
**published 5 November 2026** · paywalled

The full abstract, quoted in its entirety because its brevity is the point:

> This is the annual summary of the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Consensus on Science With
> Treatment Recommendations; a comprehensive review was completed in 2025. This latest summary addresses the
> most recent published resuscitation evidence reviewed by International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation
> task forces, including Basic Life Support; Advanced Life Support; Pediatric Life Support; Neonatal Life
> Support; Education, Implementation, and Teams; and First Aid. Members from these 6 task forces assessed the
> certainty of the evidence using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation criteria,
> and their statements include consensus treatment recommendations. Highlights from task force discussions and
> insights into how decisions regarding treatment recommendations were made are provided in the Justification
> and Evidence-to-Decision Framework Highlights sections. The task forces also listed priority knowledge gaps
> for further research.

**989 characters for the annual consensus of the body that sets the evidence base for resuscitation
worldwide.** **It names its six task forces, says GRADE was used, and reports not one recommendation, not one
certainty rating, and not one knowledge gap.** **This archive has logged abstracts of 2,000 to 9,000
characters from national societies; the Chinese lung-biopsy consensus of 6 October deposited 9,056 characters
including all fifteen recommendations verbatim with evidence levels.** **ILCOR deposits a table of contents.**

**Under the 22 October format-decision rule, the honest classification has to be made carefully.** That rule
says a missing grade distribution has at least two causes — withholding, and a document class that does not
require one — and that the design section decides which. **Here the abstract states that GRADE was applied, so
certainty ratings exist and are simply not in the deposit. That is the withholding branch**, and it is the
second clear instance after the Brazilian bloodless-surgery guideline of 1 November. **The difference is that
this is ILCOR.**

**Interpretation, and it is limited by design.** **What can be said is structural and worth saying: this is
an annual update layered on a comprehensive review completed in 2025, which means the 2026 document is an
increment on the 2025 base rather than a replacement, and a reader must hold both.** **Six task forces
including Education, Implementation and Teams signals that the scope is systems as well as drugs.** **What is
actually new in adult advanced life support, paediatrics or neonatal care cannot be read from the deposit at
all, and I am not going to infer it.** The full document would settle it and is paywalled; the user can send
the PDF in a Claude Code session for a fuller read.

## 2. ANZICS on percutaneous tracheostomy: a consensus statement with no numbers

**[Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy in adult intensive care: An ANZICS consensus
statement](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057x261500117)** · **Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society
with the College of Intensive Care Medicine** · *Anaesthesia and Intensive Care* · **published
5 November 2026** · paywalled

From the abstract:

> Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) is a commonly performed procedure in intensive care units;
> However, the high-quality evidence base guiding many aspects of its practice remains limited, resulting in
> variation across clinicians and institutions. This statement was drafted by a panel of experts, later
> reviewed by an expert working group comprising representatives from the Australian and New Zealand Intensive
> Care Society and the College of Intensive Care Medicine, and endorsed by the boards of both organisations.
> The recommendations presented are based on the best available evidence and, where evidence is scarce, on
> structured expert consensus. … This document reflects current practice at the time of publication and is
> intended to be a living document, subject to regular review.

**The abstract contains no recommendation count, no grading scheme, no thresholds and no clinical content.**
**What it does contain is an unusually complete account of its own governance** — drafted by a panel, reviewed
by a joint ANZICS and College working group, endorsed by both boards — **and an unusually honest account of
its own evidence base: limited, with structured expert consensus filling the gaps.**

**Here the format-decision rule comes out the other way from ILCOR.** **This document does not claim a grading
system, and it states that where evidence is scarce it rests on expert consensus.** **So the absence of a
grade distribution is a property of the document class, not a withholding** — the category-difference branch,
as with the EACTS robotic cardiac surgery consensus of 1 November whose object was institutional
prerequisites. **Scoring either for GRADE would be a category error, and the two now make a pair worth
keeping: one claims grading and withholds the distribution, one claims none and says why.**

**Interpretation.** **The clinically significant sentence is the first one: practice varies across clinicians
and institutions because the evidence does not settle it.** **A consensus statement whose stated purpose is
to standardise a common procedure in the absence of good trials is doing the most useful thing available**,
and declaring itself a living document subject to regular review is the right posture for a document built on
expert opinion. **For an intensive care unit in Australia or New Zealand this is now the reference; for one
elsewhere it is a well-governed statement of what two colleges believe.**

**And it lands on a live thread with a dated sequel.** **Tracheostomy runs through at least six entries in
this archive**, and SCCM's own in-development list, read here on [2
November](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-11-02/),
schedules an **early-versus-late tracheostomy guideline for 2027–2028.** **A procedural consensus now and a
timing guideline in two years are complementary rather than competing**, and the pipeline reading from a week
ago is what makes that visible.

## 3. A fifth kind of Delphi threshold, and a hierarchical composite endpoint

**[Consensus on a core outcome set for ventilation trials in critically ill patients with acute brain
injury](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08659-0)** · **international Delphi panel, 31 countries** ·
*Intensive Care Medicine* · **published 5 November 2026** · paywalled

**A four-round international Delphi conducted to COS-STAD and COS-STAP standards, with candidate outcomes
from a scoping review and a multidisciplinary panel from 31 countries scoring on a 9-point GRADE scale.**
From the abstract:

> Inclusion required >70% rating as "critical" (score 7-9) and ≤15% as "not important" (1-3). … 78
> stakeholders participated in Round 1; 60 completed all rounds (23.1% attrition). Fifty-four outcomes
> achieved Delphi consensus. At Consensus Meeting 1, these were organized into 16 categories; the
> highest-scoring outcome per category was selected. Three outcomes were not ratified due to disease-specificity
> (NIH Stroke Scale) or lack of universal applicability (brain death, coma-free days). The final COS comprised
> 13 outcomes across 5 domains … A 3-level hierarchical composite endpoint was ratified at Consensus Meeting 2:
> (1) intensive care unit survival, (2) no reintubation within 72 hours, and (3) days alive without mechanical
> ventilation.

**Every count reconstructs: 78 minus 60 is 18, which is the stated 23.1% attrition; 16 categories minus 3
unratified outcomes is the final 13.**

**The threshold is a fifth entry in this archive's catalogue and it is structurally unlike the other four.**

| Document | Consensus threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Inguinal lymphadenectomy (1 Nov) | ≥70% agreement |
| Spinal muscular atrophy (2 Nov) | ≥75% agreement |
| ESICM very old patients (Feb 2025) | ≥90% strong, ≥80% moderate, 9-point Likert |
| **Core outcome set (today)** | **>70% "critical" AND ≤15% "not important"** |

**The other three are single cut-offs on agreement. This one is two-sided: an outcome must command a
supermajority calling it critical and must not command a substantial minority calling it unimportant.**
**That is a materially stricter rule, because it fails an outcome that polarises the panel even when most
members approve of it** — and polarisation is exactly what a single threshold hides. **Recorded as the
strictest Delphi rule this archive has catalogued.**

**Two further things worth keeping.** **The hierarchical composite endpoint is the substantive output and it
is ordered the right way**: intensive care survival, then freedom from reintubation at 72 hours, then days
alive without ventilation. **A hierarchical endpoint resolves ties by the outcome that matters most rather
than summing dissimilar events**, which is what an ordinary composite does and what this archive criticised
in OPTIPAM's death-or-kidney-injury-or-neurological composite in today's brief. **And three outcomes were
rejected for the right reasons after passing the Delphi** — the NIH Stroke Scale for disease-specificity,
brain death and coma-free days for lack of universal applicability. **A consensus process that overrules its
own vote on grounds of applicability is working.**

**Interpretation.** **This changes how future ventilation trials in brain-injured patients will be read rather
than how a patient is treated tomorrow**, and it is the sort of document that quietly determines what a
decade of evidence can be pooled into. **The reason it is in scope is the outcome set itself: reintubation
within 72 hours as the second tier is a direct judgement that extubation failure, not extubation, is the event
to count** — and this archive published a paediatric extubation-failure analysis from the same sweep window.
**COMET registration 2320.**

## 4, 5 and 6. Two resuscitation summaries and the eighth sepsis commentary

### The 2026 and 2025 resuscitation documents, read together

**[The novelties of adult advanced life support and post-resuscitation therapy according to the 2025 European
Resuscitation Council guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1556/650.2026.33739)** · *Orvosi Hetilap* ·
**published 8 November 2026** · Hungarian, with an English abstract · paywalled

**This is a national-language summary of the ERC's 2025 guidelines, and it is more informative about what
changed than today's ILCOR deposit is.** From the abstract:

> Although the Advanced Life Support guidelines do not represent a paradigm shift, they place a stronger
> emphasis on delivering high-quality, timely resuscitation. … The updated guidelines also address emerging
> topics, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation-induced consciousness, the specific considerations for
> cardiac arrest occurring in closely monitored patients, and the introduction of physiology-guided
> resuscitation. Similarly, the post-resuscitation care guidelines introduce no major changes … prevention of
> fever during the first 72 hours after return of spontaneous circulation … The indications for immediate
> coronary angiography following successful cardiopulmonary resuscitation have been further refined …
> Greater emphasis is placed on multimodal neuroprognosis and post-resuscitation rehabilitation.

**Three novelties are named and all three are new ground rather than revised thresholds: CPR-induced
consciousness, cardiac arrest in the already-monitored patient, and physiology-guided resuscitation.**
**The first is a problem created by better CPR; the third is a shift from protocol to feedback.** **And
post-resuscitation rehabilitation enters as the fourth link in the Chain of Survival**, which is a structural
change to how the field frames recovery.

**"Do not represent a paradigm shift" is the sentence to keep**, and it sits well beside the 30 October
divergence finding: **guidelines converge on process and diverge on numbers, and here a major revision
reports convergence on process with the numbers refined rather than reversed.** **Fever prevention for the
first 72 hours is stated as a target without a temperature**, which is itself the direction of travel after
the TTM trials — and today's brief published the TTM and TTM2 heterogeneity analysis finding no subgroup that
benefits from 33 °C.

**One route note. This is the second Hungarian-language guideline summary this archive has reached, and both
carried full English abstracts.** **A national-language summary in a general medical journal is often more
disclosive than the international society's own deposit**, which is the same lesson as the English
republication route recorded on 2 November.

### The eighth commentary on the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines

**Two documents, one readable and one not.**

**[New sepsis guidelines: Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2026 and German Sepsis Society 2025 — competing or
complementary?](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00101-026-01753-4)** · *Die Anaesthesiologie* · **published
2 November 2026** · **routed here from the 5 November brief**

**This is the first document this archive has found comparing SSC 2026 against a national adaptation**, and
the comparison is the value. **Named as new in SSC 2026: a graded sepsis terminology — definite, probable,
possible, unlikely — a mean arterial pressure target of 60 to 65 mmHg for patients aged 65 and over,
treatment initiation in the prehospital setting, and source control within six hours.** **Named as points of
agreement: early anti-infectives, balanced crystalloids, norepinephrine first-line, capillary refill time as
a perfusion parameter, and a strong recommendation for prolonged or continuous beta-lactam infusion.** The
authors' conclusion is that the two are complementary — the German document the high-resolution handbook for
Central Europe, the international one setting direction toward a continuum-based model.

**And today's literature brief put that last point of agreement to the test.** **A meta-analysis of prolonged
versus intermittent piperacillin-tazobactam infusion, published in this archive's
[10 November brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-11-10/), favours prolonged infusion for clinical cure on
585 patients at very low certainty, finds no clear 90-day mortality difference on 5,532 patients at moderate
certainty, and draws 99.3% of its weight from a single trial.** **Two guidelines agreeing on a strong
recommendation is not independent evidence when both rest on the same trial, and the brief and the watch have
now established that from both sides on the same day.**

**[Persistent hypoperfusion in the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines: an insufficiently defined treatment
trigger](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.medine.2026.502665)** · *Medicina Intensiva* · **published
4 November 2026** · **abstract-free; the content is not claimed**

**Typed as a Letter, with no abstract deposited and no PMCID.** **The title is a criticism and a specific one
— that "persistent hypoperfusion", which SSC uses as a trigger for escalating treatment, is not defined
tightly enough to act on.** **That is all that can be read, and it is added to the named gaps.**

**With these two, the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines have now attracted eight commentaries in this
archive** — the overinterpretation caution, the emergency-medicine harmonisation, the hospitalist highlights,
the reinforcement-learning discordance paper, the Icarus editorial, the German comparison, this letter, and
the hour-1 bundle compliance study in today's sweep. **A guideline drawing eight commentaries inside six
weeks is itself the finding, and the pattern across them is consistent: the disagreements are about
definitions and triggers rather than about drugs.**
