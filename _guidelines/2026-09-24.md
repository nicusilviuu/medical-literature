## 1. SAMBA — perioperative blood glucose management in ambulatory surgery

**Society · Published:** **Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia**. *Anesthesia & Analgesia*, **22 March
2024**, September 2024 issue — **two and a half years old.** **Cited 33 times.** Indexed as a
**Consensus Statement**
Rajan, **Duggan**, **Abdelmalak**, Butz, Rodriguez, Vann, Joshi

> This consensus statement is a **comprehensive update of the 2010 SAMBA Consensus Statement** on
> perioperative blood glucose management in patients with diabetes mellitus undergoing ambulatory
> surgery. Since the original… **several novel therapeutic interventions have been introduced**,
> including new hypoglycemic agents and **increasing prevalence of insulin pumps and continuous
> glucose monitors.**

Based on a review of the literature from **1980 to 2022**. Scope:

> preoperative evaluation of patients with DM presenting for ambulatory surgery, **management of
> preoperative oral hypoglycemic agents and home insulins**, intraoperative testing and treatment
> modalities, and blood glucose management in the **postanesthesia care unit and transition to home**
> after surgery.

And, unusually candidly, on its own evidence base and on the landscape:

> **High-quality evidence pertaining to perioperative blood glucose management in patients with DM
> undergoing ambulatory surgery remains sparse.** Recommendations are therefore based on recent
> guidelines and available literature, including general glucose management in patients with DM,
> **data from inpatient surgical populations**, drug pharmacology, and emerging treatment data.

> Since… 2010, several recent guidelines have been issued by the **ADA, AACE, the Endocrine Society,
> the Centre for Perioperative Care (CPOC), and the AAGBI** on DM care in hospitalized patients;
> **however, none are specific to ambulatory surgery.**

**Interpretation.** The document that should have anchored this archive's perioperative glycaemia
seam from the day it opened, and it is two and a half years old.

**Its self-assessment is the most valuable paragraph in it.** A consensus statement that states its
evidence is sparse, and that its recommendations are extrapolated from **inpatient surgical
populations and drug pharmacology**, is telling the reader precisely how much weight to place on it.
That is now the third document this month to do so — the
[videolaryngoscopy guidelines](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/) and the
[paraconduit herniation Delphi](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-23/) being the others — and it
remains rarer than it should be.

**The gap it identifies is real and still open.** Guidance from five major bodies on inpatient
diabetes care, **none specific to ambulatory surgery** — where the patient goes home the same day,
where nobody is checking a capillary glucose at 02:00, and where the consequences of a long fast in
someone on a sulfonylurea or an SGLT2 inhibitor land at home rather than on a ward. **The ambulatory
setting is the highest-volume and least-supervised part of perioperative practice**, and it has one
consensus statement based on sparse evidence.

**It completes a chain this archive has assembled backwards over five days.** The
[SPAQI SGLT2 consensus and the *Anaesthesia* GLP-1/GIP/SGLT2 consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/)
cover which drugs to stop; this covers glucose management around the operation itself; and
[yesterday's international position statement](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-23/) covers
inpatient technology. **Duggan and Abdelmalak author both this and the SPAQI device statement
below.**

*Access note: the abstract is a scope-and-method statement. **The recommendations themselves are not
in it** — no thresholds, no fasting rules, no insulin adjustments. The full text was not reachable.*

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0000000000006791) · PMID 38517760

## 2. SPAQI — patients who arrive wearing an insulin pump or a CGM

**Society · Published:** **Society for Perioperative Assessment and Quality Improvement**.
*Journal of Clinical Anesthesia*, **10 October 2024**, December 2024 issue — **two years old.**
**Cited 14 times.** Indexed as a **Consensus Statement**
**Oprea**, Kalra, **Duggan**, Russell, Urman, **Abdelmalak**, Patel, Pfeifer, Grant, Charitou,
Mendez, Sherr, Umpierrez, **Klonoff**

> In recent years, the integration of advanced diabetes technology into the care of individuals with
> diabetes has **grown exponentially.** Given their increasing prevalence, **insulin-requiring people
> with diabetes may present to preoperative clinics or the operating rooms wearing such devices.**

> While advantageous from a diabetes management perspective, **for those unfamiliar with devices this
> can add another layer of complexity**… particularly because of the **rapidly evolving technology.**
> Therefore, perioperative clinicians need to become familiar with diabetes technological advances,
> and device features and have an understanding of **how they can be used in the perioperative
> period.**

> This consensus statement aims to serve as an **educational material as well as to serve as a guide
> to perioperative clinicians caring for patients wearing diabetes devices (insulin pumps and
> continuous glucose monitors).**

**Interpretation.** This is the document [this morning's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-24/)
implicitly went looking for, and it has existed for two years.

**The brief reported a study showing the Dexcom G7 essentially uncorrelated with arterial glucose
during rapid excursions in critically ill patients (R² = 0.028), and yesterday's entry reported an
international position statement that explicitly declines the critically ill.** Between those two
sits the ordinary perioperative patient who **walks into pre-assessment already wearing the device** —
and that patient has had society guidance since October 2024.

**Note what it calls itself: "educational material as well as… a guide."** That is a weaker
self-description than "recommendations", and it is probably honest. The problem it addresses is not
primarily a question of evidence but of **unfamiliarity** — a clinician who has never seen a hybrid
closed-loop system cannot make a sensible decision about whether to suspend it for theatre,
regardless of what the evidence says. **A consensus statement written to teach rather than to direct
is an unusual and sensible response to that.**

**Oprea leads both this and the [SPAQI SGLT2 consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/)
found four days ago.** With Duggan and Abdelmalak appearing in both diabetes documents
today, and **Dhatariya authoring both the *Anaesthesia* GLP-1/GIP/SGLT2 consensus (found 20
September) and the inpatient CGM position statement (found 23 September)**, the authorship
concentration in this scope is now documented across five separate documents.

*Access note: the abstract states purpose and audience and **contains no recommendations at all** —
not even a topic list. Of the three documents here, this is the one whose full text would be most
immediately useful at the bedside, and it has not been read.*

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinane.2024.111627) · PMID 39388833

## 3. ITACTAIC — fast-track extubation after adult cardiac surgery

**Society · Published:** **Italian Association of Cardiac Anesthesiologists and Intensive Care
(ITACTAIC)**. *Minerva Anestesiologica*, **November 2024** — **twenty-two months old.** **Cited 12
times.** Indexed as both a **Systematic Review** and a **Consensus Statement**
Silvetti, Paternoster, Abelardo, Ajello, Aloisio, Baiocchi, Capuano, Caruso, Del Sarto,
**Guarracino**, **Landoni**, Marianello, Münch, Pieri, **Sanfilippo**, Sepolvere, Torracca and
colleagues

> Enhanced recovery after cardiac surgery in selected low-risk patients has the potential to improve
> outcomes and reduce the burden of healthcare costs. **Anesthesia-related challenges play a major
> role in the successful implementation of ERAS protocols, with particular emphasis placed on
> fast-track extubation.**

Method, and it is a real systematic review rather than an opinion round:

> After conducting a systematic review, **all randomised controlled trials published between 2013 and
> 2023** were meticulously selected and analysed during a consensus meeting **involving statement
> voting.** Out of the **2268 publications identified**, **60 RCTs were selected** and classified into
> six groups, each evaluating specific interventions associated with **extubation within 6 hours
> post-surgery**: **20 RCTs on loco-regional anaesthesia, 19 on elements of general anaesthesia, 12 on
> surgery-related aspects and techniques, three on ventilation, two on anaesthesia depth monitoring,
> and four on miscellaneous aspects.**

> The expert panel approved **16 statements, with 15 achieving high agreement and one moderate
> agreement.** A total of **eight interventions were considered associated with fast-track
> extubation:**

> **parasternal block, erector spinae plane block, alpha agonist in the operating room, opioids in the
> operating room, dexmedetomidine in the intensive care unit, minimally invasive surgical access,
> anaesthesia depth monitoring, adaptative support ventilation.**

> **The first consensus document ever published by a scientific society addressing practical
> recommendations for fast-track extubation post-cardiac surgery.**

**Interpretation.** The best-constructed of the three, and the one most squarely in this project's
primary scope.

**The method is what distinguishes it.** Sixty randomised trials, screened from 2,268 publications,
grouped by intervention type, then voted on — that is a systematic review with a consensus layer on
top, not a Delphi survey of opinion. It is a different and stronger thing than the
[paraconduit herniation consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-23/) reported yesterday,
which pooled the judgement of surgeons managing five cases a year because no trial base existed.
**Here a trial base existed and was used.**

**Two of the eight interventions are regional anaesthesia techniques** — the **parasternal block** and
the **erector spinae plane block** — which is a notable result for a cardiac-surgery document, and it
sits with this archive's running interest in regional techniques in cardiothoracic practice.

**And one of the eight is anaesthesia depth monitoring**, resting on only two of the sixty RCTs. That
lands in the middle of a thread running through this week. [This morning's JAMA
trial](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-24/) reported **two cases of unintentional awareness, both in
the TIVA arm**, in a pragmatic trial where depth monitoring was at clinician discretion;
[yesterday's BJA study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-09-23/) showed **BIS failing to register a
clear cortical change for ten to fifteen minutes**. **So depth monitoring appears here as a route to
earlier extubation, on two trials — while the monitor's own behaviour is being actively questioned.**
Those are not contradictory findings, but they belong on the same page: the case for the monitor is
currently stronger as a titration aid than as a detector.

**The scope limit is stated honestly in the first sentence — "selected low-risk patients."** Nothing
here applies to the patient who comes off bypass on three inotropes, and the document does not
pretend otherwise.

**ITACTAIC is the second national society in a week to appear in this archive only after a targeted
search**, after SEDAR on 19 and 21 September. **Added to the sweep**, along with a standing note that
national anaesthesia societies outside the English-speaking core are a systematic blind spot of an
acronym-based society list.

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.23736/s0375-9393.24.18267-3) · PMID 39545652
