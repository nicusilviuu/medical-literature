## Correction to yesterday's brief

**Yesterday's page reported, as a headline index finding, that "Bookshelf fell for the first time", and said
that until 26 October the only source this archive had watched lose records was MEDLINE.** **Both statements
are false.** The correction has been appended to
[the 26 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-26/) itself; it belongs here too, because this is the
archive's named failure mode and it has now fired three times in two days.

**What the archive's own notes already said, in two places, before that page was written:**

- **16 October.** The database was rolled back to 24 September. **Six sources lost records** — `MED` −53,519,
  `PMC` −13,009, `PPR` −12,435, `AGR` −1,049, `CTX` −5, and **`NBK` 5 → 0**. So Bookshelf had already gone to
  zero once, and five sources other than MEDLINE had been watched losing records.
- **19 October.** `PMC` −440 and **`NBK` −1** on an ordinary ingest day while MEDLINE gained 8,223, recorded at
  the time as the *"first observation of routine churn: per-source counts are not monotonic on a normal ingest
  day."* **That entry also set a rule: "a loss of a few hundred in one source on an ingest day is background.
  The reporting threshold for a decrement was 'any' and should now be higher."**

**So a `NBK` decrement of four was not a first, was not the first non-MEDLINE decrement, and under this
archive's own standing rule should not have been reported as a finding at all.**

**The aggravating detail is that yesterday's page recorded a new rule against precisely this.** It replaced an
earlier unverifiable ordinal ("the eleventh source observed losing records") with the sentence now being
corrected, and wrote: *"an ordinal claim must be checkable against the archive before it is written — this is
the archive's named failure mode and it fired again today."* **The replacement was itself an unchecked ordinal,
written in the same paragraph as the rule forbidding it.** Stating a rule is not the same as applying it, and
the only part of the original claim that survives is the one that was never an ordinal: **a −4 inside a +2,989
is invisible without the source decomposition.**

**Standing consequence, recorded today:** before any sentence containing *first*, *only*, *never*, or an
ordinal goes onto a page, **grep the notes for the counter-case**, not for the supporting case. Three failures
in two days is a procedure problem, not three accidents.

## 1. Thirty-seven consensus statements on emergency undocking, from a panel of fifty-two containing two anaesthetists

**Panel · Published:** international modified Delphi, *Journal of Robotic Surgery*, volume 20,
**26 October 2026** — one day old. Publication type: Consensus Statement.

**The gap is real and the paper states it plainly:**

> **Emergency undocking in robotic surgery is an uncommon but high-stakes event. Published protocols are
> predominantly single-institution and specialty-specific, and no multidisciplinary cross-specialty consensus
> exists.**

**This belongs in an anaesthesia archive even though it appeared in a surgical journal.** Emergency undocking
is what happens when a patient on a robotic list deteriorates — haemorrhage, arrest, a ventilation failure — and
the instruments and arms must come off before anyone can reach the patient. **The patient is typically in steep
Trendelenburg with a pneumoperitoneum, the airway is at the far end of the table behind the arms, and the
person who needs access first is the anaesthetist.**

**Method and output, counted:**

- **Four-round modified Delphi**, Round 1 open-text to generate themes, Rounds 2–4 binary statements
  (Yes / No / Unable to rate), **pre-specified threshold 80% agreement**, with "Unable to rate" excluded from
  the denominator.
- **52 experts from 10 countries** completed Round 1; **response rates 80.8%, 80.8% and 76.9%** for Rounds 2–4
  — that is 42, 42 and 40 of 52.
- **37 statements reached consensus: 2 definitions, 6 common-pathway items, 18 controlled-undocking items,
  11 uncontrolled-undocking items.** **25 were classified platform-agnostic and 12 platform-dependent.**
  **Three role-allocation items were deferred to a planned later round.**

**The panel composition, as reported:**

| Role | Share | Of 52 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead or experienced console surgeons | 53.8% | 28 |
| First assistants | 30.8% | 16 |
| Surgical care practitioners | 9.6% | 5 |
| **Anaesthetists** | **3.8%** | **2** |
| Industry representative | 1.9% | 1 |

**Two anaesthetists.** And the paper's own limitation sentence adds the other absence:

> **The panel was predominantly United Kingdom-based and surgical, and included no scrub or circulating
> practitioners.**

**Interpretation.** **The document's greatest strength is that it says this itself, in the abstract, rather
than leaving a reader to count percentages.** That is better practice than most consensus statements manage,
and it is the reason this entry can be precise rather than insinuating.

**But the composition bears directly on what the statements can be relied upon for.** **An emergency undock is
a two-team procedure**: the surgical team detaches instruments and arms in a defined order, and the anaesthetic
team maintains ventilation and venous access, manages the haemodynamic consequences of desufflation and
levelling the table, and — if the emergency is an arrest — needs the chest reachable for compressions. **The
three items deferred to a later round are the role-allocation items, which is to say the part that determines
who does what and when.** **So the consensus that exists covers the steps; the part that coordinates two teams
around a patient is explicitly not yet agreed.** **A framework for an emergency in which an anaesthetist is
one of the two principals, agreed by a panel in which anaesthetists are 3.8% and theatre nursing is 0%, with
the role allocation postponed — that is the shape of the document, and it is worth stating before anyone
adopts it locally.**

**Two further things are to its credit.** **The platform-agnostic/platform-dependent split (25 and 12) is an
unusually useful piece of structure**, because it tells an institution which statements survive changing robot
vendor and which must be re-derived — a distinction most device-adjacent consensus documents leave implicit.
**And the closing sentence declines to over-claim:** *"The resulting framework requires simulation and clinical
validation before implementation."*

**Archive context: emergency undocking has never appeared in this archive**, and nor has robotic-theatre crisis
management as a guideline topic. The nearest neighbours are both research rather than guidance: the uniportal
versus multiportal robotic thoracic surgery review in the [25 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-25/)
and the opioid-free anaesthesia for robotic prostatectomy item still held in the brief queue. **This is the
first consensus document on what to do when a robotic case goes wrong.**

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11701-026-04102-0) · *Journal of Robotic Surgery*

## 2. Four societies write a crosswalk because their guidelines do not agree

**Societies · Published:** **American Heart Association, American Academy of Pediatrics, American College of
Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine**, *Circulation*, **26 October 2026**
— one day old. A scientific statement.

**The stated reason for the document is the part that matters here:**

> Healthcare for girls and women is **uniquely fragmented across clinical specialties**, including pediatrics,
> adolescent medicine, primary care, obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, gynecology, cardiology,
> endocrinology, hematology, and neurology. **Furthermore, inconsistent alignment across society guidelines
> contributes to missed opportunities to optimize cardiovascular health.**

**And the product follows from it:** the statement *"crosswalks major society guidelines, synthesizes the
evidence, and provides consensus-based clinical guidance."*

**The construct.** A multisociety science advisory group developed the **Female Life Stage** construct, which
defines **four stages — perimenarche (S1), ovarian cycling (S2), perimenopause (S3), postmenopause (S4)** —
and treats **pregnancy as a modifying life event rather than a stage**, *"when cardiovascular risk may emerge
or be unmasked (eg, adverse pregnancy outcomes)."* Guidance is then given for each stage across three
interconnected domains: **hormonal, psychosocial and cardiovascular health**.

**Interpretation.** **Read this archive's last two days together and the pair is instructive.** Yesterday's
entry documented two 2026 guidelines — STS and APHRS — giving **inverted recommendation classes to
beta-blockers and amiodarone for the same operation**, neither acknowledging the other. **Today a four-society
panel writes a document whose explicit purpose is that society guidelines do not line up, and whose central
deliverable is a crosswalk between them.** **One is the disease and the other is a treatment for it**, and the
treatment is worth noting as a genre: a consensus document that does not add recommendations so much as
reconcile existing ones.

**Whether a crosswalk is the right instrument is a separate question, and the honest answer from the abstract
is that it cannot be judged from the abstract.** A crosswalk can do two very different things: map where
guidelines agree and flag where they conflict, or paper over conflicts by choosing one and not saying so.
**The abstract does not say which conflicts were found or how any were resolved, and it gives no count of
recommendations, no grading scheme and no distribution** — so there is nothing here for this archive's
recommendation-structure catalogue beyond the structure of the life-stage construct itself. **"Consensus-based
clinical guidance" is the only grading language in the abstract**, which is a statement about the method, not
about the strength of any particular piece of advice.

**The direct relevance to this archive is narrower than the document's scope but is real.** **Pregnancy as a
risk-unmasking event, and adverse pregnancy outcomes as a durable cardiovascular risk marker, is exactly the
history that changes a preoperative assessment** in a woman presenting years later for unrelated surgery — and
it is the kind of item that falls between specialties precisely because no single guideline owns it. **That is
the fragmentation the document is about, observed from the perioperative end.**

**The closing "call to action to improve clinical workflows, enhance clinician education, and address
scientific and implementation gaps"** is the part to be sceptical of: **a call to action is not a
recommendation, and a document that identifies misalignment between guidelines has not by itself removed it.**

[Read the statement](https://doi.org/10.1161/cir.0000000000001482) · *Circulation*
