## 1. ASGE on remimazolam — the society document this archive's thread was missing

**Society · Published:** **ASGE Standards of Practice Committee**, *Gastrointestinal Endoscopy*,
**7 October 2026** — **ten days old**, 0 citations, typed `Practice Guideline`.

> This clinical practice update provides **evidence-based recommendations on the use of remimazolam, a novel
> ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, for procedural sedation during GI endoscopy.** Remimazolam was approved by
> the Food and Drug Administration in **July 2020** … **It offers rapid onset, short duration of action, and
> organ-independent metabolism, making it an alternative to traditional sedatives such as midazolam and
> propofol.** This update **synthesizes data from randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and real-world
> studies, addressing efficacy, safety, dosing, and patient selection.** It aims to assist clinicians in
> optimizing sedation while minimizing risks, **particularly in high-risk populations.** **Previous guidelines,
> such as the 2018 American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines on sedation and anesthesia in GI
> endoscopy, focused on midazolam and propofol but did not include remimazolam.**

**Interpretation.** **This archive opened a remimazolam thread on 10 October, and it was three research papers
with no society document anywhere.** The thread held a meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis against
sevoflurane, a randomised trial on postoperative nausea, and the one that mattered most — **a double-blind
crossover trial showing that the bispectral index reads differently under remimazolam than under propofol at
matched sedation depth**, written up [on 11 October](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-11/) and logged as the
monitoring thread's second failure mode, "the calibration changes". **The missing piece was whether any
society had yet told anyone what to do with the drug. Now one has.**

**The gap it fills is explicit and worth noting as a pattern: a guideline that names what it previously
omitted.** The 2018 ASGE sedation guidance covered midazolam and propofol; remimazolam was approved in July
2020 and has been in use ever since without society guidance in this setting. **Six years is a long interval
between a drug entering practice and a specialty saying how to use it**, and the update's framing — "did not
include remimazolam" — is unusually direct about the hole. **"Organ-independent metabolism" is the property
driving adoption**: remimazolam is hydrolysed by tissue esterases rather than cleared hepatically or renally,
which is exactly why "high-risk populations" appears in the aims.

**And here is the limitation, which is also the thread's open question.** The abstract is a **statement of
scope, not of content**: it names the domains addressed — efficacy, safety, dosing, patient selection — and
gives **no recommendations, no counts, no grades.** So this archive cannot tell you what ASGE actually
recommends. **More pointedly: it says nothing about depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring.** If the bispectral index
is miscalibrated under remimazolam at equal clinical depth, then a unit that adopts the drug while keeping its
processed-EEG targets is titrating to a number that has changed meaning. **Whether the update addresses that is
not readable here, and an absence from an abstract is not an absence from a document** — so this is logged as
the specific thing to check in the full text, not as a criticism. **It is the highest-value unanswered question
in the remimazolam thread and the full text is behind the publisher.**

[Read the update](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gie.2026.08.019)

## 2. Sixteen questions, two recommendations: EAN and ESCMID on infectious encephalitis

**Society · Published:** **European Academy of Neurology with the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and
Infectious Diseases**, *European Journal of Neurology*, **1 October 2026** — **sixteen days old**,
0 citations, typed `Practice Guideline`. **Updates the 2010 European guidelines.** PICO framing, systematic
search per question, **GRADE**, with good practice statements where only indirect evidence existed.

> **Since the last European guidelines were published in 2010, novel causes have emerged, diagnostic processes
> have evolved and treatments assessed** … **Results Sixteen questions were considered, with two
> recommendations reached: for initiation of intravenous aciclovir as soon as possible upon suspicion of
> encephalitis, and against replacing pathogen-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of
> cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with multiplex CSF PCR panels** for diagnosis of adult patients with suspected
> infectious encephalitis. **For adults with proven HSV encephalitis, we were unable to recommend for or
> against the use of corticosteroids. A further 13 good practice statements** regarding when to clinically
> suspect infectious encephalitis, microbiological investigations, additional investigations, treatment and
> ongoing care and rehabilitation were agreed.
>
> **Conclusions Given the clinical severity and public health importance of this condition, the paucity of
> high-quality evidence is striking.** Transnational, large-scale research in infective encephalitis is
> encouraged.

**Interpretation.** **Two graded recommendations out of sixteen prioritised questions is the lowest ratio this
archive has recorded, and the panel says so in its own conclusion.** For comparison, the AAP paediatric urinary
tract infection guideline reported eight key action statements, the Spanish *Helicobacter pylori* consensus
twenty-five recommendations, and the Chinese intravenous-to-oral switch guideline twenty-four. **Here: two,
plus one explicit inability to decide, plus thirteen statements of good practice.** For a condition that kills
and disables, sixteen years after the last European edition. **"The paucity of high-quality evidence is
striking" is the kind of sentence a guideline panel writes when it has done the search properly.**

**The first recommendation is the one every clinician already follows and it is still the right thing to put
first: intravenous aciclovir as soon as encephalitis is suspected**, not when it is confirmed. Herpes simplex
encephalitis is treatable and time-critical, and a recommendation to treat on suspicion is a recommendation to
accept over-treatment as the price of not missing it.

**The second is the interesting one, because it recommends against a newer technology.** **Multiplex CSF PCR
panels should not replace pathogen-specific PCR.** A panel is faster, broader and easier to order; the
guideline says it should not displace targeted testing. **That is a diagnostic-accuracy-versus-clinical-use
judgement of exactly the kind this archive's monitoring thread keeps meeting from the other direction** — a
broader test is not automatically a better test, and the failure modes of a panel (lower sensitivity for
individual targets, detections of uncertain significance, a negative panel read as a negative workup) are
clinical rather than technical. **The abstract does not give the reasoning or the certainty grade**, which is
what one would want here, and it is behind the publisher.

**The corticosteroid non-recommendation deserves its own sentence, because an explicit "unable to recommend
for or against" is more useful than silence.** In proven HSV encephalitis, steroids have been argued about for
decades on the basis that cerebral oedema and inflammation contribute to the damage. **The panel looked and
found it could not say.** Recording the question as open, rather than omitting it, tells a clinician that the
uncertainty is real and not an oversight.

**One deposit note: this document is deposited twice** — once typed `Other` with a PMCID, open access and
**no abstract**, and once typed `Practice Guideline` with the full abstract and no PMCID. **The open-access
copy is the one with no readable abstract.** Sixth distinct variant of the dual-deposit pattern this archive
tracks, and the second in two days.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1111/ene.70647)

## 3. Cord milking in non-vigorous newborns, and a deposit cut off at the word "Recommendation"

**Society · Published:** **Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada**, *Journal of Obstetrics and
Gynaecology Canada*, **6 October 2026** — **eleven days old**, 0 citations, typed `Practice Guideline`, with a
**French co-publication under its own DOI**. Literature searched **April 2020 to May 2026**. **GRADE** for
quality and strength.

> **Objective** To assess the benefits and risks of **intact umbilical cord milking in non-vigorous infants
> from 35 weeks onwards.** … **Outcomes** The primary outcomes assessed included **neonatal mortality and
> morbidity, including hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, the need for resuscitation, and cardiorespiratory
> support.** **Benefits, harms, and costs In term and late preterm infants who remain non-vigorous despite
> stimulation, intact umbilical cord milking may reduce the incidence of moderate to severe hypoxic ischemic
> encephalopathy and the need for cardiorespiratory support. Available evidence does not suggest a significant
> increase in neonatal adverse outcomes, including jaundice requiring phototherapy.** … **This document
> represents an abstraction of the evidence rather than a methodological review.** …
>
> **Social media abstract If an infant at a gestational age of ≥35 weeks, after 15 seconds of deferred
> (delayed) cord clamping and tactile stimulation while being kept warm still remains non-vigorous, then
> intact umbilical cord milking may be considered to decrease hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy and the need for
> cardiorespiratory support.**

**Interpretation, and the deposit problem first because it nearly cost the item.** **The abstract ends with the
heading "Recommendation" and then stops.** The structured field that holds the actual recommendation is
truncated out of the deposit. **What saved it is the "Social media abstract" field directly above, which
carries the operative content in full** — the gestational threshold, the fifteen-second window, the
prerequisites (deferred clamping, tactile stimulation, kept warm) and the two outcomes. **New rule for this
archive: when a guideline deposit truncates at "Recommendation", read the social-media abstract; journals now
publish a plain-language summary and it survives where the structured field does not.**

**On the substance, this is a resuscitation recommendation issued at a precise decision point, and the
precision is the useful part.** Not "consider cord milking in depressed newborns" but: **≥35⁰ weeks, after
fifteen seconds of deferred clamping and stimulation, still non-vigorous.** That is a protocol step with a
clock on it, placed inside the first minute of life, and it is actionable in a way most guideline language is
not. **The claimed benefits — less moderate-to-severe hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, less need for
cardiorespiratory support — are the outcomes that matter**, and the harms assessment specifically clears the
mechanism anyone would worry about: **transfusing extra blood volume did not significantly increase jaundice
requiring phototherapy.**

**The strength of the claim is "may reduce" and "may be considered", which is the honest register for this
evidence base**, and the document is explicit that it is **"an abstraction of the evidence rather than a
methodological review"** — a technical update, not a systematic review, while still grading with GRADE.
**That disclosure is a document-class marker worth recording: the technical update occupies the space between
a narrative review and a guideline, and says so.**

**It also sits directly against the newborn-resuscitation item reported two days ago.** The
[NeoBeat post-mortem](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-15/) was a trial of whether displaying a measured heart
rate from birth got non-vigorous newborns ventilated within sixty seconds — destroyed when the staff being
filmed were given an anonymous way to delete the recordings. **Both documents are about the same sixty seconds
and the same infant.** One asks whether better information speeds the intervention; the other adds an
intervention at the fifteen-second mark. **Between them they frame the problem of the non-vigorous newborn as
one of sequence and clock rather than of drug or device**, which is a useful way to hold it.

**Limitation stated plainly: the recommendation's GRADE strength and certainty are in Appendix A and the
truncated field, neither of which is readable here**, so I can report what is recommended and not how strongly.
**The French co-publication has no abstract at all** — the same two-DOI, two-language, one-empty pattern as
the Spanish *H. pylori* consensus, which is the seventh variant of the deposit problem.

[Read the technical update](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jogc.2026.103523) ·
[French co-publication](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jogc.2026.103524)
