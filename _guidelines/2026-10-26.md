## 1. APHRS — the first comprehensive Asia-Pacific atrial fibrillation guidelines, in four parts, and the one with the cardiac-surgery chapter has no abstract

**Society · Published:** **Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society**, *Journal of Arrhythmia*, volume 42,
**21 October 2026** — five days old. Four parts, four DOIs:

| Part | Topic | DOI | Abstract in the index |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | Epidemiology, detection/screening, integrated care | `10.1002/joa3.70495` | 706 characters |
| II | Stroke prevention | `10.1002/joa3.70491` | 1,275 characters |
| **III** | **Rate and rhythm control** | `10.1002/joa3.70492` | **none, in either record** |
| IV | Lifestyle risk factors and comorbidity | `10.1002/joa3.70490` | 973 characters |

**These are described by the society as "the first comprehensive APHRS guidelines dedicated to the management of
atrial fibrillation".** Parts I, II and IV are summarised by abstracts short enough to be scope notes rather than
summaries. **Part III — the one that matters most here — has no abstract at all.**

**Each part appears twice in the index, in the dual-deposit pattern this archive has logged repeatedly: one
record with the publisher's metadata and no PMCID, and a sibling with a PMCID and the open-access flag set.**
For Part III both copies are abstract-free. **Under the method this archive used until last week, Part III
would have gone to the needs-a-PDF list and its contents would never have been read.** Instead the recovery route
first used on 20 October and formalised as a two-field check on 25 October — `pmcid` present *and*
`isOpenAccess` set, then fetch `/<PMCID>/fullTextXML` — returned **862 kB of XML for PMC13687593**,
containing the entire guideline.

**This is the third use of that route, and the first where there was no abstract whatsoever to work from.** The
SRLF atrial fibrillation guideline on 20 October and the two AAST post-intensive-care consensus guidelines on
25 October each had something; Part III had nothing, and the route still returned the whole document. **The
method correction of 25 October — an abstract-free record is not an unreadable document — is now established
rather than provisional.**

### What Part III contains

**111 graded recommendations**, extracted from the recommendation tables:

| Class | Level A | Level B | Level C | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | 19 | 16 | 15 | **50** |
| **IIa** | 4 | 22 | 12 | **38** |
| **IIb** | 5 | 7 | 3 | **15** |
| **III** | 2 | 4 | 2 | **8** |
| **Total** | **30** | **49** | **32** | **111** |

**Two of its chapters are in this archive's primary scope rather than its secondary one.** Section 3.6 covers
**cardiac surgery and concomitant surgical ablation**, including the stand-alone Cox-Maze IV procedure and
thoracoscopic and convergent hybrid approaches. Section 4.6 is **"Prevention and Treatment of AF After Cardiac
Surgery"**. Section 4.1 is **"Management of Unstable Patients"**, which splits explicitly into atrial
fibrillation as the cause of instability and **atrial fibrillation as a secondary phenomenon in critically ill
patients**.

**The surgical ablation recommendations:**

> **Concomitant surgical ablation is recommended in patients with AF undergoing mitral valve surgery** to
> improve maintenance of sinus rhythm and long-term outcomes. — **Class I, Level A**
>
> **Concomitant surgical ablation should be considered in patients with AF undergoing nonmitral cardiac
> surgery** to improve maintenance of sinus rhythm and long-term outcomes. — **Class IIa, Level B**
>
> **Stand-alone surgical or hybrid ablation should be considered in symptomatic patients with persistent or
> longstanding persistent AF who have failed or are unsuitable for catheter ablation, when performed in
> experienced centers.** — **Class IIa, Level B**

The text puts numbers to the stand-alone option: Cox-Maze IV gives **"approximately 94% freedom from atrial
arrhythmia at 1 year and 77%–89% at 5–10 years"** with perioperative mortality **"low (~0%–0.7%)"**. It also
records the cost: surgical ablation **"has been associated with a higher rate of permanent pacemaker
implantation compared with no ablation … especially in patients who underwent biatrial lesion set compared to
left-sided lesion set."**

### The disagreement with STS 2026

**The postoperative atrial fibrillation chapter puts APHRS directly against a guideline this archive already
holds.** The **STS 2026 Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of New-Onset
Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation After Cardiac Surgery** were reported here in the
[22 August brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-08-22/). Both sides of the comparison were checked today, and
they are exactly swapped on the two central prophylactic drugs:

| Recommendation | **STS 2026** | **APHRS 2026 Part III** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Perioperative beta-blockers** | **Class IIa** | **Class I, Level A** |
| **Perioperative amiodarone** | **Class I** (oral) | **Class IIa, Level A** |
| Posterior left pericardiotomy | Class IIa | **Class IIa, Level A** |
| Cardioversion for haemodynamically unstable POAF | Class I | **Class I, Level C** |

**APHRS: "Perioperative β-blocker therapy is recommended for the prevention of POAF in the absence of
contraindications" — Class I, Level A. "Perioperative amiodarone should be considered in patients at increased
risk of POAF, particularly when β-blockers alone are unlikely to provide adequate prophylaxis" — Class IIa,
Level A.** So beta-blockers are first-line and amiodarone is the escalation.

**STS, verbatim from its abstract: "Two Class I recommendations addressed perioperative oral amiodarone and
rhythm cardioversion for hemodynamically unstable POAF. Two Class IIa recommendations supported posterior
pericardiotomy and perioperative β-blockers."** So amiodarone is the Class I agent and beta-blockers sit a
class below.

**Two guidelines, six months apart, on the same question, citing largely the same trials — BLOS, PAPABEAR,
PALACS, the landiolol randomised trials — and the ranking of the two drugs is inverted.** The APHRS text
quotes BLOS at 39% versus 31% for metoprolol and PAPABEAR at 29.5% versus 16.1% for amiodarone; the raw
reductions are not obviously in conflict, so the inversion is a difference in how the same evidence was
weighted, not in what was read.

**One part of the divergence has a clean explanation and one does not.** APHRS adds a recommendation STS could
not make: **"Perioperative intravenous landiolol or esmolol, an ultra–short-acting β1-selective blocker, should
be considered to reduce POAF" — Class IIa, Level B**, supported by a randomised trial it reports as reducing
POAF from 34.3% to 10% after CABG and a meta-analysis at relative risk 0.40. **Landiolol is available in Japan
and parts of Europe and not in the United States, so a US society cannot recommend it whatever the evidence
says, and APHRS says explicitly that it is integrating "data and clinical considerations specific to the
Asia-Pacific region".** That is a regional-availability effect on a recommendation, and it is legitimate.
**It does not explain the beta-blocker and amiodarone swap**, because metoprolol and amiodarone are available
in both regions and the trials are the same ones.

### Two things today's check settled in the archive's own record

**The 22 August entry carried the STS recommendation classes from secondary sources** — a society news release
and trade coverage — and said so. **Today's reading of the STS primary abstract confirms both of the classes
that entry reported: amiodarone Class I, beta-blockers Class IIa.** The secondary sources were right, and the
primary record adds the distribution the entry did not have: **15 recommendations — 8 preventive, 3
intraoperative, 4 postoperative treatments — comprising 2 Class I, 2 Class IIa, 8 Class IIb and 3 Class III.**
**Eight of fifteen at Class IIb is a guideline that is mostly uncertainty by its own count**, which is the
distribution this archive's recommendation-structure catalogue exists to record.

**The 22 August entry also recorded the STS publication date as "unclear and conflicting across sources
(~April or July 2026)". The index gives 18 April 2026.** That is now settled.

**One caveat carried forward honestly: the anticoagulation comparison is not verified on both sides.** The
22 August entry recorded STS anticoagulation as **Class IIb, individualised, discouraging routine use**, from
secondary sources; **the STS abstract does not name that recommendation's class**, so it is not confirmed.
APHRS's position is **Class IIa, Level B**: *"Anticoagulation should be considered in patients with POAF
lasting ≥ 24–48 h, recurrent episodes, or elevated thromboembolic risk, taking into account bleeding risk and
surgical factors."* **If the secondary-source reading of STS is right, that is a third disagreement; it is
reported here as unconfirmed rather than as a finding.**

### The critically-ill section, against the French document from six days earlier

**Part III gives three graded recommendations for atrial fibrillation in critically ill patients:**

> **Electrical cardioversion is recommended in AF patients with acute hemodynamic instability or worsening
> hemodynamic status attributable to AF** — **Class I, Level C**
>
> **Intravenous short-acting beta-blockers (e.g., landiolol) when available, may be considered in patients with
> AF who have hemodynamic instability or depressed LVEF** — **Class IIb, Level B**
>
> **Intravenous amiodarone may be considered in patients with AF who have hemodynamic instability or severely
> depressed LVEF** — **Class IIb, Level B**

**Set this beside the [20 October entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-20/), which reported the French
Intensive Care Society's formalized expert recommendations on atrial fibrillation in the adult ICU: eighteen
recommendations, of which only two could be graded, sixteen issued as expert opinions because the evidence
could not be classified within GRADE at all, and six further questions abandoned.** The French document's
stated premise, quoted in that entry, was that **"most existing recommendations for atrial fibrillation are
derived from cardiology settings."**

**APHRS is precisely such a cardiology-setting document, and the comparison is sharper than it first looks.**
APHRS does grade its ICU recommendations where the French panel could not — but **its one Class I recommendation
for this population is Level C, and the other two are Class IIb.** Level C is expert consensus or small
non-randomised data. **So the cardiology guideline's strongest ICU recommendation rests on the same grade of
evidence that caused the intensivists to decline to grade at all.** The two documents are not actually in
disagreement about the evidence; they disagree about whether to attach a class label to it. **The French panel
reported the weakness in its grade distribution. APHRS reported it in the level, one column over, where it is
easier to miss.**

**Interpretation.** **The four-part guideline is a substantial document and the cardiac-surgery and
critically-ill chapters are the parts this archive should carry forward.** Three findings are worth keeping.
**First, the beta-blocker and amiodarone inversion against STS is real, verified on both sides, and not
explained by drug availability** — a surgeon reading STS and an electrophysiologist reading APHRS will
prophylax the same operation differently, and neither document acknowledges the other. **Second, the
availability effect is worth separating out rather than treating as disagreement**, because the landiolol
recommendation is a case where a regional guideline can legitimately go further than a US one. **Third, the
document that contains all of this had no abstract, and three weeks ago this archive would have recorded it as
unreadable.**

## 2. A Swedish national consensus on follow-up after ICU stay, with a publication date of 1 January 2027

**Society · Published:** commissioned by Sweden's **National System for Knowledge-Driven Healthcare Management
(NPO)**, published in *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica*, volume 71 issue 1, article e70361. **Publication
date needs a paragraph of its own, below.** Publication type: Consensus Statement.

**The problem stated:**

> A substantial proportion of intensive care unit (ICU) survivors experience long-term physical, psychological
> and cognitive impairments after critical illness. **However, methods for identifying patients in need of
> post-ICU support vary widely. As a result, many patients are left without access to established
> rehabilitation pathways.**

**Method.** A multidisciplinary task force; a systematic search of four databases in 2024 for **pre-existing
systematic reviews** rather than primary studies; recommendations developed from that literature. **The
consultation is the unusual part and is reported in numbers:** workshops with **critical care nurses (n = 30)**
and **professionals responsible for follow-up clinics (n = 74)**, plus **patient representatives (n = 4)**, then
external peer review by professional organisations, the national intensive care registry and government
agencies.

**What it recommends, quoted closely:**

> **All adult patients with an ICU length of stay ≥ 48 h are recommended for follow-up**, with an **initial
> contact within 1 week of ICU discharge** to assess clinical status, identify early problems and ensure
> continuity of care. Patients and their families are recommended to be offered an **outpatient follow-up visit
> at 2–3 months after discharge**, led by ICU clinicians and, when possible, delivered by a multidisciplinary
> team. **Validated screening tools and objective tests are recommended** to assess post-traumatic stress,
> anxiety, depression, cognitive impairment, physical function and health-related quality of life as
> appropriate. Patients with identified problems should be **referred to appropriate healthcare providers or
> specialist services**.

**Interpretation.** **This is a selection-and-timing document, and it says so:** *"a framework for patient
selection, timing and assessment to support a more structured and equitable management of post-intensive care
syndrome."* **It does not claim that follow-up improves outcomes** — it claims that the absence of a rule for
who gets followed up produces inequity, which is a different and more defensible claim. **The 48-hour
threshold and the one-week/2–3-month schedule are the operative content**, and they are specific enough to
audit against, which is the test of whether a framework document is usable.

**It arrives one day after the [25 October entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-25/) reported the two
AAST Critical Care Committee consensus guidelines on post-intensive care syndrome** — the same clinical
territory, from a trauma-surgery committee in the United States, recovered the same way. **Two post-intensive
care consensus documents from two continents in two days, and the division of labour between them is clean:
the AAST documents enumerate what to expect and screen for, this one specifies who enters the pathway and
when.** Read together they are closer to a complete answer than either is alone.

**On the date, which this archive has to get right.** The index record carries **`firstPublicationDate`
2027-01-01**, and that is not a placeholder of the kind logged before — it is the **January 2027 print issue
date**, from `printPublicationDate` and `dateOfPublication` "2027 Jan". **`electronicPublicationDate` is null,
so the index holds no online publication date at all.** What it does hold is `dateOfCreation` 2026-10-25 and
`firstIndexDate` 2026-10-26. **So the honest statement is: online ahead of the January 2027 print issue,
entered the index today, actual online date not recorded in the index.** Reporting it as "published 1 January
2027" would be absurd and reporting it as published today would be unsupported. **This is the mirror image of
the 1 January 2026 placeholder the archive logged on abstract-free sibling records: there the date was a
stand-in for nothing, here it is a real date in the future.**

[Read the recommendation](https://doi.org/10.1111/aas.70361) · *Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica*

## 3. Twenty Delphi statements on polymyxin B haemoadsorption, written for a therapy not yet approved where its authors practise

**Panel · Published:** modified Delphi consensus, **ten critical care and nephrology experts**, *Critical Care*,
**29 July 2026** — three months old, and included because the archive holds nothing on extracorporeal endotoxin
removal and because of what its closing sentence does.

**The gap it addresses:**

> **Despite long-standing clinical use and emerging evidence, no structured framework exists** guiding
> clinicians in identifying septic shock patients for endotoxemia assessment, interpretation of the Endotoxin
> Activity Assay (EAA), or practical implementation of polymyxin B hemoadsorption (PMX-HA) therapy.

**Method and output.** Pre-meeting survey, a one-day in-person conference, iterative voting; **consensus
predefined at ≥ 80% agreement; 20 statements agreed across three domains** — when to assess for endotoxaemia,
how to read the assay, and how to implement treatment.

**The specifics are unusually concrete for a Delphi document:**

> **EAA thresholds of ≥ 0.60 and < 0.90** to support PMX-HA consideration; **initiation as early as possible
> once eligibility is confirmed, with ≤ 36 h from septic shock onset proposed as a pragmatic implementation
> benchmark**; and standardized treatment protocols consisting of **two sessions, 24 h apart, targeting 120 min
> each**.

**Interpretation.** **Three things are worth separating.** **First, the thresholds are a window, not a cut-off
— ≥ 0.60 and < 0.90 — which means the panel is excluding the most endotoxaemic patients from treatment
consideration**, and anyone implementing this needs to know that is deliberate rather than a typographical
oddity. **Second, the ≤ 36-hour benchmark is labelled "pragmatic implementation" rather than evidence-derived,
which is the right label and is easy to lose once a number is in a protocol.** **Third, and this is the
sentence that places the whole document:** *"It is intended to support institutions currently using PMX-HA and
to assist U.S. clinicians and health systems in preparing for potential adoption pending regulatory
approval."*

**A consensus framework written in part for a therapy not yet approved in the country whose clinicians it is
preparing.** That is a legitimate thing to do — implementation readiness is real work — but it is a different
genre from a guideline that tells you whether to use a treatment, and the document does not make a claim about
efficacy anywhere in its abstract. **The research priorities it lists say the same thing: "treatment effect
heterogeneity, EAA kinetics, clinical implementation, and healthcare system integration" — the first of those
is the question of whether it works in whom, and it is listed as a priority rather than answered.** Read it as
a protocol for institutions that have already decided, not as evidence for deciding.

[Read the recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06182-8) · *Critical Care*
