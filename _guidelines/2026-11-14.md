## 1. Five organisations: do not withhold RhD-positive blood from a woman who is bleeding to death

**[Posttransfusion Management of RhD-Negative Females of Childbearing Potential Who Receive RhD-Positive
Low-Titer Group O Whole Blood and RBCs During Trauma Resuscitation](https://doi.org/10.1097/XCS.0000000000002099)** ·
*Journal of the American College of Surgeons* · **published 13 November 2026** · indexed as both a Practice
Guideline and a Consensus Statement · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Society · Published:** a joint position statement and resource document of the **American College of
Surgeons Committee on Trauma**, the **Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition**, the **National Association
of Emergency Medical Services Physicians**, the **Trauma Hemostasis and Oxygenation Research Network** and
the **Allo Hope Foundation**. A multidisciplinary panel spanning trauma surgery, transfusion medicine,
emergency medicine, critical care, obstetrics, paediatrics and prehospital care.

> Increasing use of low-titer group O whole blood and RBC in trauma resuscitation has increased the
> likelihood that RhD-negative females of childbearing potential (FCPs) will receive RhD-positive blood
> products. Concurrently, **national trauma data demonstrate that FCPs with traumatic hemorrhage are
> approximately 40% less likely to receive low-titer group O whole blood than comparable male patients,
> highlighting a trauma systems and equity concern.** … Reported D-alloimmunization rates after trauma
> transfusion range from approximately 8% to 43%. Modeling studies estimate a perinatal death risk from
> anti-D-mediated hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) of approximately 0.04% and a combined
> severe HDFN and perinatal death risk of approximately 0.24% after RhD-positive transfusion. Estimated risk
> of any HDFN-complicated future pregnancy ranges from approximately 0.6% to 6.5%. **The panel concluded that
> RhD-positive blood products should not be withheld when RhD-negative products are unavailable, and
> transfusion is clinically indicated.** Recommended management includes selective Rh immunoglobulin
> prophylaxis for low-volume exposures, structured antibody surveillance, patient counseling,
> multidisciplinary follow-up, and institutional protocols.

**Interpretation.** **The equity datum is the reason the document exists, and it should be read first: women
of childbearing potential with traumatic haemorrhage are about 40% less likely to receive whole blood than
comparable men.** That is not a statement about alloimmunisation risk; it is a statement that clinicians have
been resolving the uncertainty by withholding, and that the withholding is measurable in national data.
**A position statement that begins from an observed inequity rather than from new trial evidence is doing
something this archive has not seen this quarter.**

**The uncertainty in the numbers is enormous and the recommendation is nonetheless firm. That combination is
correct here, and it is worth saying why.** **Alloimmunisation after trauma transfusion is reported between
8% and 43% — a five-fold range. The risk of any future pregnancy complicated by haemolytic disease is
0.6% to 6.5% — an eleven-fold range.** Ordinarily this archive treats a range that wide as a reason to
withhold a recommendation. **The asymmetry of the decision is what rescues it: the comparator is immediate
death from haemorrhage, and the modelled perinatal death risk is approximately 0.04%.** **Even at the top of
every range, the future reproductive risk is orders of magnitude below the immediate mortality risk of not
transfusing — so the recommendation is robust to the uncertainty, which is a different and much stronger
property than the uncertainty being small.** **RULE: a wide-interval estimate can support a firm
recommendation when the decision is asymmetric; what matters is whether the conclusion survives the whole
range, not whether the range is narrow.**

**Two things to carry into practice.** **Selective Rh immunoglobulin prophylaxis for low-volume exposures**
is the operative recommendation and the word doing the work is *selective* — the document does not recommend
immunoglobulin for everyone. **And the infrastructure items — structured antibody surveillance, counselling,
multidisciplinary follow-up, institutional protocols — are what convert a resuscitation-room decision into
something a patient can be followed up on.** **Those are the parts that fail silently if nobody owns them**,
and the document's framing as a "resource document" alongside a position statement suggests the panel knows
it.

[Read the position statement](https://doi.org/10.1097/XCS.0000000000002099)

## 2. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons published a guideline in April that this archive never reported

**[The Society of Thoracic Surgeons 2026 Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of
New-Onset Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation After Cardiac
Surgery](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.athoracsur.2026.04.002)** · *The Annals of Thoracic Surgery* 122 ·
**published online 18 April 2026, print August 2026** · indexed as a Practice Guideline · paywalled;
abstract quoted below

**This was found by checking the Society of Thoracic Surgeons directly while working through the owed
in-development list. It is seven months old, it is from a tracked society, it is squarely in scope, and it
is the second such gap in two days** — after yesterday's ESAIC and ESPA paediatric neuromuscular block
guideline.

> …the group used the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome framework … Following standardized STS
> methodology, evidence from randomized controlled trials and observational studies was reviewed, and
> recommendations were graded by Class of Recommendation and Level of Evidence. … The group developed 15
> recommendations: 8 addressing preventive strategies, 3 intraoperative techniques, and 4 postoperative
> treatments. **Two Class I recommendations addressed perioperative oral amiodarone and rhythm cardioversion
> for hemodynamically unstable POAF. Two Class IIa recommendations supported posterior pericardiotomy and
> perioperative β-blockers. Eight Class IIb recommendations reflected areas of uncertainty and limited data.
> Three Class III recommendations addressed therapies without demonstrated benefit.** … Levels of Evidence
> vary across interventions, and high-quality data remain limited for POAF after cardiac surgery.
> Pharmacologic studies often lack granularity in dose and timing…

**The grade distribution is the finding, and the guideline publishes it in full.** **Of fifteen
recommendations, two are Class I, two are Class IIa, three are Class III and eight — a clear majority — are
Class IIb, which the authors gloss themselves as "areas of uncertainty and limited data".** **So for the
commonest complication of cardiac surgery, a tracked society's considered position is that two interventions
should be done, two are reasonable, three should not be done, and more than half the field is a judgement
call.** **This archive's standing rule is that a published grade distribution is worth more than a confident
summary, and this is the clearest example yet: the document's own arithmetic tells the reader how much of it
is settled.**

**What is Class I is narrow and practical: perioperative oral amiodarone for prevention, and rhythm
cardioversion when postoperative atrial fibrillation makes a patient haemodynamically unstable.** **What is
Class IIa is interesting for a cardiothoracic reader — posterior pericardiotomy, a surgical manoeuvre,
sits at the same grade as perioperative beta-blockers**, which means the strongest non-pharmacological
evidence in the document is for draining the pericardium rather than for any drug beyond amiodarone.

**Read it against what this archive carried two days ago.** **On 12 November the brief reported a Bayesian
systematic review of new-onset atrial fibrillation in general critical illness: 54 studies, 367,896 patients,
a pooled incidence of 10.6%, a post-discharge thromboembolism risk ratio of 1.94, and anticoagulation rates
of 32.9% in hospital with a credible interval spanning fourfold.** **So cardiac surgery now has a
fifteen-recommendation guideline for this condition and the general intensive care unit has none, while the
practice variation in the general unit is fourfold.** **The two documents together make the case for the
guideline the general literature does not have.**

**The honest limits.** The abstract names no systematic-review registration and the clinical questions were
formulated **in 2025**, so the evidence base closes before the trials this archive has reported since.
**And "Levels of Evidence vary across interventions" with "high-quality data remain limited" is a conclusion
that concedes the eight Class IIb recommendations rather than defending them** — which is the right posture
and also a warning against quoting any single recommendation as though the document were uniform.

[Read the guideline](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.athoracsur.2026.04.002)

## 3. The in-development list, finished — and what each society's page actually shows

**This completes the dated action carried since 2 November. Seven societies, four checked yesterday, four
attempted today. Two of today's four produced usable results, one produced a negative result that is itself
informative, and one could not be reached.**

### ESC — a published schedule, nine documents planned

**The ESC does something none of the other tracked societies do: it publishes a forward schedule with
chairpersons.** Read from the page itself today:

| Planned for 2027 | Planned for 2028 |
| --- | --- |
| Acute coronary syndromes | **Myocardial revascularization** |
| Focused update of the 2023 cardiomyopathies guidelines | Adult congenital heart disease |
| Obesity and cardiovascular disease | Cardiovascular disease prevention |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | **Universal definition of cardiogenic shock** (no chairpersons listed) |
| Syncope and cardiovascular autonomic disorders | |

**Three of these matter to this archive.**

**Myocardial revascularization is scheduled for 2028 — and three days ago this watch reported an American
College of Cardiology document whose entire argument is that the revascularisation evidence base is
outdated**, because the landmark trials predate physiologic lesion assessment, intracoronary imaging and
modern arterial grafting. **So the complaint and the revision are now both on the record, two years
apart.** A reader deciding today how much weight to give current revascularisation guidance has, in effect,
been told by one body that the evidence has moved and by another that the document will be rewritten in
2028.

**A universal definition of cardiogenic shock is scheduled for 2028 and has no chairpersons listed yet** —
the only entry on the page in that state, which the page itself shows. **This archive already holds French
expert recommendations on cardiogenic shock from March 2026 (SRLF with the Société Française de Cardiologie,
SFAR and SFCTCV), so a national management document exists and an international definition is two years
away.** **That is this fortnight's dominant theme on a two-year horizon: the management guidance arrives
first and the definition follows.**

**Pulmonary embolism in 2027** is the third, and it is a 2019 guideline being revised.

**One methodological note that matters more than the list.** **A web search summary offered to me today
placed a "focused update of the 2019 dyslipidaemia guidelines" in the 2027 column. The page itself places
the 2027 focused update on the 2023 cardiomyopathies guidelines, and shows the 2019 dyslipidaemias update
under 2025 as already published.** **The summary had conflated two entries.** **This is the second time in
three days that a secondary source has misdescribed a primary one** — after the ESRA newsletter's
characterisation of the ASRA fifth edition, which this archive labelled unverified. **Read the page, not the
summary about the page.**

### EACTS — 35 documents listed, none from 2026

**The EACTS clinical practice guidelines page shows no in-development section at all.** It lists 35
documents; the first page of 20 runs from **9 October 2025** back to February 2022, and **the most recent
item on it is dated 9 October 2025.** **Nothing from 2026 appears.**

**That is a verification of something this archive inferred on 1 November and could not then confirm.** On
that day the October index audit found an **EACTS Expert Consensus Document on Robotic Cardiac Surgery
minimum standards, published 1 October 2026** (`10.1093/ejcts/ezag187`), and the entry recorded it as "a
tracked society, missed by the society route and found only by the index". **Six weeks later the society's
own page still does not list it.** **The inference is now a confirmed observation.**

### ASA — not reached

**The page this archive attempted returned HTTP 404.** **The URL was constructed rather than found, so this
is a failure of my approach and not evidence about the ASA.** **Recorded as not done, and owed.**

### What the two days together establish, in both directions

**This is the useful conclusion and it now has named cases on both sides.**

- **The index finds what a society page omits:** the EACTS robotic cardiac surgery consensus, published
  1 October 2026, found by the index audit and still absent from the EACTS page today.
- **A society page finds what the daily index sweep misses:** the ESAIC and ESPA paediatric neuromuscular
  block guideline of 4 March 2026, with its erratum, a letter and a reply, found yesterday; and the STS
  postoperative atrial fibrillation guideline of 18 April 2026, found today.

**Neither route dominates and neither is redundant.** **Three tracked-society documents in two days were
each invisible to one route and visible to the other.** **The operational consequence: the society route
cannot be run as a formality that confirms the index, and the index cannot be trusted to cover societies.
Both have to be read, and the pages have to be read for their PUBLISHED lists and not only for
announcements** — which is how both of this week's gaps were found.

## 4. Who may do what in an anaesthetic room, scored on a four-point scale

**[What Should Anaesthesia Technologists Do? Identifying Entrustable Professional Activities for Anaesthesia
Technologists](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11402268/v1)** · **preprint, not peer reviewed** ·
**posted 9 November 2026** · open

**Reported because scope of practice in the anaesthetic room is guidance in substance whatever its format,
and flagged clearly as a preprint.** A two-round modified Delphi with practising anaesthesia technologists
and anaesthesiologists from eight countries.

> Twenty panellists (19 anaesthesia technologists and one anaesthesiologist) completed Round 1, and 14
> completed Round 2. Round 1 produced 64 task statements, which were consolidated into 20 candidate EPAs.
> **Eighteen of the 20 activities met the post consensus criterion of at least 75% of panellists rating the
> activity 3 or 4 on the four-point scale; conducting handover and documentation and assessing discharge
> criteria from the post-anaesthesia care unit did not (71.4% each).** Five activities had a normalised
> importance score of 95% or higher: assisting with airway management (100%); assisting in the management of
> anaesthesia complications during the operation, assisting in the management of airway complications during
> the operation, and preparing and administering intravenous fluids, blood and blood products (98.2% each);
> and preparing the operating theatre for different types of anaesthetic procedure (96.4%).

**For the threshold catalogue this is an eighth distinct scheme: at least 75% of panellists rating 3 or 4 on
a FOUR-point scale.** **The catalogue now holds four different scale lengths — four, five, seven and nine
points — and the same nominal 75% means something different on each.** **On a four-point scale with no
neutral option, "3 or 4" is simply the top half, so this threshold asks whether three quarters of the panel
are on the positive side of a forced choice.** That is a weaker instrument than 75% on the top three of
seven, and not comparable to it.

**Three things about this panel the abstract reports and a reader should not skip.**

**It lost 30% of its panel between rounds — twenty completed round one and fourteen completed round two.**
**So the 71.4% that failed two activities is ten of fourteen**, and every percentage in round two rests on a
denominator of fourteen. **A threshold of 75% on fourteen panellists means eleven people.**

**Nineteen of the twenty panellists were anaesthesia technologists and one was an anaesthesiologist.** **This
is a document about what technologists may be entrusted to do, in a role that exists under
anaesthesiologist supervision, and the supervising profession contributed one voice in twenty.** **The
authors say the list needs "a broader, multidisciplinary panel" before use, which is the correct
conclusion** — and the composition is worth naming precisely because the activities that scored highest are
**assisting with airway management at 100%** and assisting with anaesthesia and airway complications at
98.2%, which are the activities where the supervising clinician's view matters most.

**And one high-scoring activity was removed by judgement rather than by threshold** — continuing education
and knowledge update, excluded "on conceptual grounds" as a professional responsibility rather than an
entrustable activity. **That is defensible and it is also a reminder that a Delphi's output is curated after
the scoring, so the final list is never simply what the panel voted for.**

**The authors' own conclusion is the right one and unusually plain: "The items are not yet fully specified
EPAs," and further rounds, complete descriptions and workplace testing are needed "before the list is used
for curriculum design or entrustment decisions."** **Reported here as an early signal of a document that
will matter when it is finished, not as guidance to act on.**
