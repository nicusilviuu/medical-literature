## One new document today, and this morning's index finding says why

**The literature brief published three hours ago established that MEDLINE contributed zero records to the
22 October index date, and that the whole database had not moved in thirty-one minutes.** **Both sweeps were
run anyway and both say the same thing.**

**The guideline-publication-type sweep returned 3 hits**, of which one was the STS document reported yesterday,
one was an orthopaedic interpretation out of scope, and **one was new: a Swiss haematology consensus on
desmopressin.** **The title-keyword sweep returned 10 hits, every one of which was in yesterday's set.**

**So today reverses the last two days: sweep A found the unique item and sweep B missed it entirely.** On 20
and 21 October it was the other way round — the keyword sweep carried items the publication-type sweep could
not see. **The reason is mechanical and worth recording: the new document is titled "Desmopressin (DDAVP):
indications and practicalities", which contains no clinical keyword from the in-scope list.** **Only its
publication type — Europe PMC assigns it four, including Practice Guideline and Consensus Statement — makes it
findable.** **Three days, three different patterns of which sweep found what. Neither is a superset of the
other in either direction, and running both is not redundancy.**

**With one new document, the rest of today's entry comes from the readable backlog, and the lead item there
was flagged yesterday as a test of a live hypothesis.** That hypothesis has been answered, and not as
predicted.

## 1. SFAR and SFPC reclassified the document instead of labelling 29 recommendations as expert opinion

**Society · Published:** **the French Society of Anaesthesia and Intensive Care (SFAR) with the French Society
of Clinical Pharmacy (SFPC)**, in *Anaesthesia, Critical Care & Pain Medicine*. **First published online
26 November 2025, in a 2026 issue** — **eleven months old**. **Nineteen French experts.** **PICO questions
across four fields, a twenty-year bibliographic search following PRISMA, GRADE methodology, voting by the
GRADE grid method.** **Twenty-nine recommendations.**

> **Potential conflicts of interest were formally declared at the outset of the recommendations development
> process, which was conducted independently of any industry funding.** … **4 fields were defined: (1) Work
> environment and processes; (2) Human and organizational factors; (3) Post-hoc risk management; (4) The
> problem of drug shortages.** …
>
> **Due to the very small number of studies that could provide the necessary power to answer the most important
> judgment criterion (i.e., medication errors), it was decided, prior to drafting the recommendations, to adopt
> the format of Professional Practice Guidelines (PPG) rather than Formalized Expert Recommendations.** The
> recommendations were then voted on by all the experts using the GRADE grid method.
>
> **For all questions, recommendations could be formulated, either for the entire field of the question or
> partially.** … **resulted in 29 recommendations** … **After a round of voting and incorporating a few
> adjustments, a strong agreement was reached on all the recommendations.**

**Interpretation.** **Yesterday this watch wrote that this document was "now the obvious next test of the
three-document pattern, because it is the one French document to date that reports unanimity without reporting
a grade distribution." That was a prediction, and it was wrong in the way that matters.**

**It does not withhold a grade distribution. It explains why there is not one, and it did so before writing a
word of the recommendations.** The panel searched twenty years of literature, found that almost no study had
the statistical power to answer its primary criterion — **whether an intervention reduces medication errors** —
and **formally changed the class of document from a Formalized Expert Recommendation to a Professional Practice
Guideline.** **The other three French-speaking documents applied GRADE to each question and labelled the
ungradeable ones expert opinions. This one concluded in advance that the whole exercise was ungradeable and
relabelled the exercise.**

**So the pattern is not three documents with a fourth exception. It is four documents using two mechanisms for
the same honesty.**

| Document | Recommendations | Graded | Expert opinion | Abandoned | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SFAR / SFPC medication errors (26 Nov 2025)** | **29** | **— by design** | **— by design** | **0** | **format changed in advance: PPG, not RFE** |
| SFAR / SOFMER withdrawing life-sustaining treatment ([19 Oct](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-19/)) | 9 | 3 | 6 | 2 | labelled per recommendation |
| SRLF atrial fibrillation in the adult ICU ([20 Oct](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-20/)) | 18 | 2 | 16 | 6 | labelled per recommendation |
| GFRUP supraventricular tachycardia in the PICU ([21 Oct](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-21/)) | 21 | 5 | 16 | not stated | labelled per recommendation |
| **Four documents** | **77** | — | — | — | — |

**The reclassification has one consequence the other three do not, and it cuts the other way.** **Zero questions
were abandoned here: "for all questions, recommendations could be formulated."** The other three abandoned two,
six, and an unstated number. **That is not because the evidence was better — the abstract says it was worse.
It is because a Professional Practice Guideline does not require gradeable evidence to answer a question, so
nothing had to be left open.** **Both designs are honest and they trade off differently: label each
recommendation and some questions go unanswered; relabel the document and all of them get answers of a weaker
kind.** **A reader needs to know which trade was made, and in both cases the abstract says so.**

**Two things here are better practice than anything else this watch has reported this month, and both are in
the first three sentences.** **Conflicts of interest were formally declared at the outset and the process was
conducted independently of industry funding — stated in the abstract.** **Of roughly thirty guideline abstracts
reported here since late September, this is the first to put either fact where a reader meets it.** For a
document about medication safety, where the interests of manufacturers of labelling systems, syringe pumps and
prefilled syringes are obvious, **declaring industry independence up front is the relevant disclosure and it is
made.**

**On substance, the four fields are chosen in a way that says the panel understood its own subject.** **Work
environment and processes; human and organisational factors; post-hoc risk management; and drug shortages.**
**Three of the four are not about drugs at all** — they are about the system in which a drug is given. **And
the fourth, drug shortages, is the one almost no medication-safety guideline includes**, despite substitution
of an unfamiliar presentation during a shortage being an obvious and well-documented error mechanism. **A
guideline that treats supply failure as a patient-safety field rather than a procurement problem is thinking
about the right thing.**

**A date correction, on the record.** **My own backlog recorded this document as "published 24 June 2026",
taken from a web-search summary on 20 October.** **The index gives a first publication date of 26 November 2025
in a 2026 issue.** **The web-search date was wrong and I carried it in the backlog for two days without
checking it against the primary source** — which is the same class of error as the recalled counts this watch
has caught on each of the last three days, with the difference that this one came from outside and I let it
through. **The document is eleven months old, not four, and is reported here as such.**

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accpm.2025.101718) · *Anaesthesia, Critical Care & Pain Medicine*

## 2. A desmopressin consensus that says the baseline measurement does not predict the response

**Society · Published:** **the Haemostasis Working Group of the Swiss Society of Haematology, with the Swiss
Haemophilia Network**, in *Swiss Medical Weekly*, **30 September 2026** — **twenty-two days old**, the only new
document in today's sweeps. **Europe PMC types it as Practice Guideline, Review, Consensus Statement and
Journal Article.** **Scope-checked in on perioperative haemostasis: the societies are haematological and not
on the tracked list, but desmopressin for bleeding is given in operating theatres and intensive care units,
and the document is a national consensus on when to give it.**

> **Objective** To provide practical guidance for the use of desmopressin in patients with **non-severe
> haemophilia A, von Willebrand disease, bleeding disorders of unknown cause and mild platelet function
> disorders.** …
>
> **Results and conclusions** Desmopressin **may increase and normalise factor VIII and von Willebrand factor
> levels** in patients with non-severe haemophilia A and von Willebrand disease and **can prevent or treat
> bleeding in patients with bleeding disorders of unknown cause and mild platelet function disorders. A
> desmopressin trial is recommended to verify tolerability and identify responders, as the response to the
> drug is not predictable based on baseline levels of factor VIII and von Willebrand factor. Previously
> observed side effects and current contraindications must be carefully evaluated prior to every
> administration.**

**Interpretation.** **The central recommendation is a measurement recommendation, and it is one of the cleanest
examples this archive has met of a surrogate being named as inadequate and replaced with a direct test.**
**"The response to the drug is not predictable based on baseline levels of factor VIII and von Willebrand
factor"** — so the document does not say measure the factor level and infer; **it says give a test dose and
measure what actually happens.** **A desmopressin trial to identify responders is the direct measurement of
the thing you care about, and the baseline assay is the surrogate that fails.**

**Set against the three conditions this archive applies to a monitored variable, it meets the first two
emphatically and says nothing about the third.** **The measurement is closer to the pathology than the one it
replaces — and uniquely among the documents in that thread, the guidance states explicitly why the surrogate
does not work (condition 1).** **A protocol converts the result into an action: responders get the drug,
non-responders do not (condition 2).** **There is no outcome in the abstract measured by anything other than
the test itself (condition 3, absent).** **That is the same shape as closed-loop oxygen** — but the reasoning
is better, because here the surrogate's failure is an established pharmacological fact rather than an
assumption.

**The practical clinical content is in the last sentence and it is the kind of line that gets skipped.**
**"Previously observed side effects and current contraindications must be carefully evaluated prior to every
administration."** **Before *every* administration, not once per patient.** Desmopressin causes
hyponatraemia through free-water retention, and tachyphylaxis means repeated doses work less well while the
sodium risk accumulates. **A consensus that puts "every administration" in its conclusion is targeting the
repeat dose on day two, which is where the harm actually happens and where a perioperative team is least
likely to re-check.**

**And the scope is where this document connects to the next item, which is the useful finding of today's
reading.** **Its named indications are non-severe haemophilia A, von Willebrand disease, bleeding disorders of
unknown cause, and mild platelet function disorders.** **Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is not among them.**

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.57187/5030) · *Swiss Medical Weekly*

## 3. An anaesthesia guideline recommends desmopressin in Ehlers-Danlos, which the haematology consensus does not cover

**Society · Published:** **AANA**, in the *AANA Journal*, **1 October 2026** — **twenty-one days old**, carried
from the backlog. **Three-round modified Delphi with 15 experts in anaesthesia and EDS. Seven preliminary
guidelines, consensus on all seven by the third round.** **The first consensus-based clinical practice
guidelines for anaesthetic care in this population.**

> **No standardized anesthesia guidelines currently exist for this population, despite facing increased
> perioperative risks.** … **Key recommendations include comprehensive airway assessment for all EDS subtypes;
> bleeding risk evaluation and consideration of desmopressin in classical, classical-like, kyphoscoliotic, and
> vascular EDS; multimodal analgesia and regional anesthesia strategies for hypermobile EDS; perioperative
> planning that incorporates common comorbidities; and cautious use of regional or neuraxial anesthesia in
> vascular EDS, requiring individual risk-benefit analysis due to hematoma risk.** … **While limited by sample
> size and gaps in existing evidence**, these recommendations provide a framework …

**Interpretation.** **Read beside item 1, these two documents sit three weeks apart and do not quite meet.**
**The AANA guideline recommends considering desmopressin in four named EDS subtypes. The Swiss haematology
consensus, which exists to define when desmopressin should be used, lists four indications and EDS is not one
of them.**

**The honest version of that observation needs a qualification, and it is the interesting part.** **An
individual EDS patient with a bleeding tendency and no identified factor deficiency might well fall under
"bleeding disorders of unknown cause" or "mild platelet function disorders", both of which the Swiss document
covers.** **So this is not a contradiction — it is a gap in whose judgement applies.** **The anaesthesia
guideline recommends the drug by syndrome; the haematology consensus licenses it by haemostatic phenotype, and
insists on a test dose because the response cannot be predicted from baseline levels.** **The two are
reconciled by doing what the Swiss document says: establish whether this patient responds, rather than
inferring it from the diagnosis on the chart.** **That is a genuinely useful practical conclusion and neither
document states it, because neither knows about the other.**

**It also matters that EDS bleeding is mechanistically different from the conditions desmopressin was developed
for.** Desmopressin releases stored von Willebrand factor and raises factor VIII; **EDS bleeding is a
connective-tissue and vessel-wall problem, with abnormal collagen giving fragile vessels and poor platelet
adhesion to subendothelium.** **Raising von Willebrand factor in a patient whose collagen is the defect is a
plausible intervention and not an obvious one**, which is exactly the case where a test dose, rather than a
syndrome-level recommendation, is the right instrument.

**On structure, this is the smallest document in this month's catalogue and it reports its own limits.**
**Seven guidelines, fifteen experts, consensus on all seven by round three** — **a 100% consensus rate, which
with seven statements and fifteen raters is a statement about the panel's cohesion rather than about the
evidence.** **The authors say so: "limited by sample size and gaps in existing evidence".** **And the one
recommendation that will change practice most is the most cautious one: cautious use of regional or neuraxial
anaesthesia in vascular EDS, with individual risk-benefit analysis, because of haematoma risk.** **Telling
anaesthetists to think twice before a neuraxial block in the most fragile EDS subtype is worth seven
statements on its own**, and it is the kind of recommendation that needs no trial to justify.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.70278/aanaj/.0000001099) · *AANA Journal*
