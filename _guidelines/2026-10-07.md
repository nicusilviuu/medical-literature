## 1. Eight areas, fifteen recommendations, and two questions the panel would not answer

**Society · Published:** **SFMU / SFAR / CNGOF** — the French societies of emergency medicine, of
anaesthesia and intensive care, and the French College of Gynaecologists and Obstetricians — *Anaesthesia,
Critical Care & Pain Medicine*, **5 August 2022.** Four years old, **24 experts**, GRADE, two rounds of
voting, 5 citations. **The first obstetric-emergency guidance in this archive**, and the last of the SFMU
seam-audit documents in primary scope.
Bagou, Sentilhes, Mercier, Berveiller, Blanc, Cesareo, Dewandre, Douai, Gloaguen, Gonzalez, Le Conte,
Le Gouez, Madar, Maisonneuve, Morau, Rackelboom, Rossignol and colleagues

> **Objective** To provide recommendations on the management of **urgent obstetrical emergencies outside the
> maternity ward.** … The authors followed the GRADE … method to assess the level of evidence in the
> literature. **The potential drawbacks of strong recommendations in the presence of low-level evidence were
> highlighted. Some recommendations with an insufficient level of evidence were not graded.**
>
> **Methods** **Eight areas were defined: imminent delivery, postpartum haemorrhage (prevention and
> management), threat of premature delivery, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, trauma, imaging,
> cardiopulmonary arrest, and emergency obstetric training.**
>
> **Results** **Fifteen recommendations** on the management of obstetrical emergencies were issued by the
> SFMU/SFAR/CNGOF panel of experts, and **4 recommendations from formalised expert recommendations (RFE)
> established by the same societies were taken up to answer 4 PICO questions** dealing with the pre-hospital
> context. After two rounds of voting and several amendments, **strong agreement was reached for all the
> recommendations. For two questions (cardiopulmonary arrest and inter-hospital transfer), no recommendation
> could be made.**

**Interpretation. Read the areas list against the results and one line does not survive the comparison:
cardiopulmonary arrest is one of the eight areas the panel defined, and it is one of the two questions the
panel could not answer.** A French guideline on obstetric emergencies outside the maternity ward **defines
maternal cardiac arrest as in scope and then issues no recommendation on it.**

**That is worth stating carefully, because it is not a criticism.** Maternal cardiac arrest is rare, the
evidence is case series and registry data, and perimortem caesarean delivery has never been and will never be
randomised. **A panel that declines to grade a recommendation it cannot support is doing the thing this
archive keeps asking panels to do.** The useful reading is the inverse: **a reader looking for guidance on
maternal cardiac arrest in the prehospital setting should know that the three relevant French societies
convened 24 experts, defined it as one of eight areas, and came away with nothing gradeable.** That absence
is information.

**On the counting convention, this is the clearest statement yet — and it runs the other way from the others.**
This archive inferred across six documents on 1 October, and demonstrated inside a single abstract on
5 October, that **a stated French recommendation total includes questions the panel could not answer.** Here
the panel does the opposite: **it reports 15 recommendations of its own, 4 imported from an earlier RFE, and
2 unanswered questions as three separate figures**, and names which questions failed. **So the convention is
not uniform across French documents, and this one is the model.** A total of "19" would have been defensible
and would have hidden the two failures; a total of "21" would have followed the convention seen elsewhere and
hidden them differently. **Reporting 15 + 4 + 2 hides nothing.**

**And the methods paragraph names, in its own words, the hazard this archive recorded yesterday.** *"The
potential drawbacks of strong recommendations in the presence of low-level evidence were highlighted."*
**Yesterday's [Chinese lung-biopsy consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-06/) carried five strong
recommendations on level 4 evidence without comment**; this French panel flags the same configuration as a
known problem in its methods and additionally **leaves some recommendations ungraded rather than forcing them
onto a scale.** Two documents, the same structural issue, one disclosure.

**One area connects directly to this week's literature.** The eighth defined area is **emergency obstetric
training** — and [yesterday's brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-06/) carried **the only
patient-outcome meta-analysis of obstetric emergency team training**, in which the single positive signal
reversed between six cohort studies and the one randomised trial. **So the French panel wrote a
training recommendation in 2022, and the outcome evidence for training was synthesised in 2021 and did not
support it at moderate certainty.** The guidance and the evidence are now both in this archive, and they do
not agree.

## 2. Twenty-six questions, twelve with no includable study, and a companion document that says so

**Society · Published:** **Brain Trauma Foundation**, *Neurosurgery*, **16 February 2026** — eight months
old, **two companion papers published the same day** plus an executive summary in *Journal of Trauma*
(9 January 2026). `Practice Guideline`. **Over half the panellists were serving military or veterans.**
Bell, Selph, Ghajar, Aarabi, Lumba-Brown, Mangat, Wright, Dengler, Stein, Pannell, Ecklund, Shackelford,
Pappas, Totten, Adelson, Armonda, Benjamin and colleagues

> **Background** Penetrating traumatic brain injury (pTBI) affects civilian and military populations… **No
> up-to-date and evidence-based guidelines exist** to assist modern medical and surgical management…
>
> **Methods** … they addressed **twenty-six Key Questions (KQs).** We searched Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, and
> Cochrane CENTRAL **from inception to August 31, 2022**…
>
> **Results** **125 studies provided evidence and another 80 studies provided contextual data** … **In
> general there was a paucity of literature and most of the identified evidence was judged to be high risk of
> bias due to study design. We did not identify any studies meeting inclusion criteria for 12 KQs. The
> highest quality evidence, rated moderate in strength, was identified for four KQs** that covered:
> **cerebral angiography vs computed tomography angiography**, the relationship between **bihemispheric injury
> in adult pTBI and mortality**, the ability of the **Surviving Penetrating Injury to the Brain (SPIN) score
> to predict mortality**, and the relationship between **infection and cerebrospinal fluid fistula.**
> **Evidence for most KQs came from case series.**
>
> **Conclusions** … **Few moderately strong conclusions on the benefit of specific management strategies for
> penetrating brain injury could be made.**

**Interpretation. Look at what the four moderate-strength questions have in common: not one of them is a
treatment question.** Imaging modality choice, an anatomical predictor of mortality, a prognostic score, and
an association between infection and CSF fistula — **all four are diagnostic or prognostic.** The guideline
says so in its own conclusion: *few moderately strong conclusions on the benefit of specific management
strategies.*

**That is the same shape as yesterday's finding, and now it has happened twice in two days in unrelated
fields.** The Chinese lung-biopsy consensus had **exactly one level 1 recommendation and it was a
contraindication list.** Here the only moderate-strength evidence is about predicting and detecting, none
about treating. **Stated as a rule: evidence accumulates fastest on the questions that can be answered by
observing patients, and slowest on the questions that require randomising them** — so a guideline's
strongest statements will tend to be about prognosis, diagnosis and who not to treat, and its treatment
recommendations will rest on consensus. **A reader who assumes the strongest evidence sits behind the
treatment advice has it backwards.**

**Two things about the method deserve flagging.** **The literature search closed on 31 August 2022 and the
guideline published in February 2026** — a three-and-a-half-year gap between evidence cutoff and
publication, in a field the guideline itself says is changing because of armed conflict. And **no includable
study for 12 of 26 questions** is close to half the document's scope returning nothing.

**What the Brain Trauma Foundation did about the vacuum is the most interesting part, and it is a separate
paper published the same day.**

> **Methods** To supplement the evidence-based recommendations … **we developed protocols for care to help
> bridge limitations of published evidence with care decisions required at the bedside.** Our working group of
> **over 30 diverse expert panelists** identified care, care pathways and key decisions … **A rigorous,
> blinded Delphi consensus process was then applied. Items achieving at least an 80% consensus vote were
> incorporated into the treatment algorithms.**
>
> **Results** … a **Master Care Pathway** relevant to all patients … **'Toolkits'** … for **surgical
> management, protruding foreign bodies, severe injury, skull base injury and vascular injury** … **In
> addition, a futility assessment is provided to assist with delineating the small proportion of patients for
> whom initial non-aggressive care might be considered with the recognition that avoidance of nihilism is
> critical to achieving best outcomes in pTBI victims.**

**This is a two-document structure worth copying: the guideline reports what the evidence supports and says
plainly where there is none, and a companion paper fills the gaps by blinded Delphi at an 80% threshold and
is labelled as consensus rather than evidence.** The alternative — which this archive has seen repeatedly —
is one document that mixes the two and issues strong recommendations on case-series evidence without
distinguishing them. **Separating them lets a clinician see which of the two they are following.**

**And the futility sentence is the most unusual line in either paper.** A guideline that provides a futility
assessment **and in the same clause warns against nihilism** is doing something harder than giving a
threshold: penetrating brain injury has a long history of self-fulfilling prognostication, where an early
judgement of hopelessness produces the outcome it predicted. **Naming that risk inside the tool meant to
support the judgement is the right place to put the warning.**

## 3. Two documents that make the recommendation depend on what the hospital has — and the answer to a question this archive has been carrying

**Society · Published:** **Colombian consensus committee**, *Brain & Spine*, **12 April 2026** — six months
old, **open access** (PMC13101607), 17 national experts, second edition of a protocol first published in
2020. And the **Explosive Weapons Trauma Care Collective (EXTRACCT)**, *World Journal of Surgery*, **31 August
2026** — five weeks old. **Both led by the same first author, Andrés M. Rubiano.**

> **BOOTStraP, second edition.** … **A multidisciplinary panel of 17 national experts** in prehospital care,
> emergency medicine, neurosurgery, and intensive care conducted a structured consensus process using
> **Delphi principles and Nominal Group Technique. Eleven predefined clinical questions** were addressed. …
> requiring **≥70% agreement at subgroup level and ≥90% plenary consensus.** … The second BOOTStraP edition
> produced **9 integrated management algorithms** covering prehospital transport, emergency care,
> neurosurgical decision-making, and critical care. **Interventions are stratified and color-coded by
> resource requirements, enabling feasible actions in low-, intermediate-, and high-resource settings.**

> **EXTRACCT.** … **No protocols or guidelines comprehensively address the full spectrum of TBI from blast and
> penetrating mechanisms across variable resource levels.** … a targeted narrative literature review …
> supplemented by military, penetrating and trauma system clinical practice guidelines (**Joint Trauma
> System, Brain Trauma Foundation, TCCC**) … following the **AGREE II** framework. … Key recommendations
> include: **early physiological stabilization with avoidance of hypotension and hypoxia; structured
> neurological assessment using postresuscitation GCS and pupillometry; noncontrast head CT as the primary
> imaging modality, with CT angiography for penetrating TBI**…; **hyperosmolar therapy as first-line treatment
> for intracranial hypertension**; … **noninvasive multimodal neuromonitoring when invasive ICP monitoring is
> unavailable**; surgical thresholds for decompressive craniectomy and craniotomy; and standardized transport
> protocols…

**Interpretation. The structural novelty is that the recommendation changes with the resource level rather
than being written for a well-equipped centre and then caveated.** BOOTStraP colour-codes interventions by
what they require; EXTRACCT writes the non-invasive neuromonitoring recommendation as the primary path **when
invasive ICP monitoring is unavailable**, rather than as a compromise. **Most guidance in this archive is
written from the top resource tier down. These two are written across tiers**, which is a different document
design and arguably a more honest one — the majority of the world's traumatic brain injury happens where the
monitor is not available.

**Now the backlog question, answered.** This archive has carried an item asking **whether anything succeeded
the ANARLF 2017 French document on intracranial pressure and severe traumatic brain injury management**, with
the standing instruction that **if no successor exists, the absence is the finding.** Searched today across
2024–2026:

- **The Brain Trauma Foundation has published no fifth edition for severe TBI.** Its 2024–2026 output is the
  penetrating-TBI second edition above, plus that document's algorithms, forewords and executive summary.
  **Severe-TBI guidance remains the fourth edition, from 2016.**
- **The American College of Surgeons** updated its **Trauma Quality Improvement Program TBI Best Practice
  Guidelines in 2024**, with a **de novo early rehabilitation chapter** summarised separately in May 2026 —
  a quality-improvement framework rather than a replacement for the BTF document.
- **The French route produced the SFNC 2025 neurosurgical document** already reported here on
  [3 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-03/), which covers the acute neurosurgical phase.
- **And the two documents above** cover the same clinical ground stratified by resources.

**So: there is no successor, and that is the finding.** **A decade on from the 2016 fourth edition, the
canonical severe-TBI guideline has not been revised, and the field has filled the space with documents of
three different shapes** — a quality-improvement framework, national and conflict-specific resource-stratified
protocols, and a neurosurgical-phase guideline. **None of them is a replacement, and a clinician asking "what
is the current guidance on ICP management in severe TBI?" is still being pointed at a 2016 document.**

**One limitation to record about the resource-stratified pair, because it is visible from the author lists.**
**Rubiano is first author on both**, and BOOTStraP's panel is Colombian while EXTRACCT's collaboration is
conflict-focused. **That a single group is producing the resource-stratified neurotrauma guidance is why it
exists at all — and it means these are not independent corroborations of each other.** Worth knowing before
citing both as though two bodies had converged.
