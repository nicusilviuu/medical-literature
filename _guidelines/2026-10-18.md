## 1. The EAU restructures its urological infections guideline around a new classification of UTI

**Society · Published:** **European Association of Urology**, *European Urology*, **15 July 2026** —
**ninety-five days old**, 1 citation, typed `Practice Guideline`. Medline, EMBASE and the Cochrane Library;
recommendation strength set by **the balance of desirable and undesirable consequences, the quality of the
evidence, and the nature and variability of patient values and preferences.**

> **Key recommendations emphasise the importance of thorough medical history and physical examination** … The
> guidelines **stress the role of antimicrobial stewardship to combat the rising threat of antimicrobial
> resistance, providing recommendations for antibiotic selection, dosing, and duration** based on the latest
> evidence. **Key updates in the 2026 Urological Infections Guidelines summary include: comprehensive
> restructuring of the guideline framework in alignment with the new classification system for UTIs; the
> addition of a new chapter on the diagnosis and management of Herpes simplex virus; the addition of a new
> chapter on the diagnosis and treatment of fungal UTIs; and an update of the evidence and recommendations on
> periprocedural antibiotic prophylaxis for prostate biopsy.**

**Interpretation.** **The first of the four key updates is the one that matters and it is easy to read past:
the entire guideline framework has been restructured to align with a new classification system for urinary
tract infection.** That is not a change in treatment. It is a change in how the condition is divided up — and
every recommendation, audit standard and stewardship metric built on the previous classification now refers to
categories that have moved.

**And it is the second guideline in three days to do this.** The
[American Academy of Pediatrics](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-16/), reported here on Thursday,
shipped its paediatric UTI guideline with a **companion technical report revising the definition of UTI** —
adding pyuria as a diagnostic criterion with thresholds by age and by whether culture results are available,
and modifying the positive-culture definition by age and collection method. **Two societies, two continents,
two age ranges, and both have changed what counts as a urinary tract infection in the same fortnight.** The
AAP revised the *definition* for diagnosis; the EAU restructured the *classification* for management. **Anyone
comparing UTI rates, antibiotic-days-per-UTI or stewardship performance across 2025 and 2026 is now comparing
across two separate redefinitions**, and neither guideline is at fault for that — but nobody downstream will
know unless they are told.

**The two new chapters are the substantive clinical additions and both say something about where the field has
moved.** A chapter on **herpes simplex virus** in a urological infections guideline is an acknowledgement that
genital HSV presents to urology and has been sitting between specialties. A chapter on **fungal UTI** is a
response to a real epidemiological shift — candiduria in catheterised, diabetic and immunosuppressed patients,
where the hard question is not treatment but whether to treat at all. **Neither is readable in detail here**,
and for a summary document that lists its own key updates, the absence of any recommendation text in the
abstract is the limitation.

**The periprocedural prophylaxis update for prostate biopsy is the item with the clearest stewardship
consequence**, and it is the one this archive would most want to see: transrectal biopsy prophylaxis has been a
major driver of fluoroquinolone use and of resistant post-biopsy sepsis, and the move to transperineal
approaches has changed the calculation. **"An update of the evidence and recommendations" is all the abstract
says.**

**One structural note on the document class.** This is a **summary of the guidelines**, not the guidelines —
the EAU publishes a short journal version alongside a long online text. **A summary that lists its key updates
without stating them is a pointer, and this archive is reporting the pointer.** That is worth saying plainly
rather than presenting it as a reading of the guideline.

[Read the summary](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eururo.2026.04.011)

## 2. French guidelines on primary CNS vasculitis — the non-infectious half of yesterday's problem

**Society · Published:** **a French multidisciplinary panel** — internal medicine, neurology,
neuroradiology, neurosurgery, neuropathology and rheumatology — *La Revue de Médecine Interne*, **6 August
2026** — **seventy-three days old**, 0 citations, typed `Practice Guideline`. **Based on expert consensus and
registry data.**

> **Primary central nervous system vasculitis (PCNSV) is a rare and heterogeneous disease whose diagnosis
> relies on a combination of clinical, radiological, and laboratory findings, and which requires a
> multidisciplinary hospital-based approach** … **Diagnosis is challenging due to non-specific clinical and
> radiological features.** The diagnostic process includes **(1) confirming vascular involvement (via MRI and
> angiography), and demonstrating the inflammatory process (via lumbar puncture, contrast-enhanced MRI and
> vascular wall sequences), (2) excluding more common differential diagnoses (especially intracranial
> atherosclerosis in patients with vascular stenosis), (3) and as often as possible discussing a brain or
> meningeal biopsy. Histological evidence obtained from a biopsy remains the gold standard** …
>
> **Induction therapy consists of high-dose corticosteroids combined with an immunosuppressant, most often
> intravenous cyclophosphamide** … **The initial corticosteroid dose is maintained for 2–3 weeks, then
> gradually tapered over 12–18 months.** Once remission is achieved, **maintenance therapy** is introduced,
> **typically using oral or subcutaneous immunosuppressants such as azathioprine, methotrexate, or
> mycophenolate mofetil, for 18–24 months.** **Preventive measures address treatment-related complications,
> including osteoporosis, infections, and metabolic disturbances. Relapses occur in a significant proportion
> of patients.**

**Interpretation, and a scope judgement stated rather than assumed.** **Primary CNS vasculitis is not
anaesthesiology, intensive care, resuscitation or cardiothoracic surgery, and it is not cardiology or
infectious disease** — it sits outside this archive's stated scope, and it has been carried on the backlog for
eight days as "readable and outstanding" without anyone checking that. **The reason to publish it rather than
quietly drop it is specific and it is not that it was already on the list.** PCNSV presents as stroke-like
events, seizures and encephalopathy; it is managed in neurological intensive care; and **it is a principal
differential diagnosis for the condition the European Academy of Neurology and ESCMID published guidelines on
[yesterday](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-17/).** A patient with suspected infectious encephalitis who
turns out not to have an infection is frequently this patient. **The two documents are the two halves of one
diagnostic problem, and that is the justification; the process fix is below.**

**The clinically striking thing is the diagnostic sequence, because of what it puts second.** Step two is
**"excluding more common differential diagnoses (especially intracranial atherosclerosis in patients with
vascular stenosis)"** — and that is the trap the whole document is built around. Vessel narrowing on
angiography looks the same whether the wall is inflamed or atherosclerotic, and the consequence of getting it
wrong runs in both directions: **immunosuppress an atherosclerotic patient, or give antiplatelets and statins
to a patient whose arteries are inflamed.** The guideline's answer is **vessel-wall imaging sequences and, "as
often as possible", biopsy** — with histology named as the gold standard, especially in small-vessel disease
where angiography is normal.

**That is a guideline recommending an invasive test in a rare disease, which is a hard thing to recommend and
a harder thing to audit.** "As often as possible" is doing careful work: it is not "always", and it concedes
that brain or meningeal biopsy will not happen in every case. **Set against the EAN encephalitis guideline's
two recommendations from sixteen questions, the pattern in both documents is the same** — a severe, rare
condition where the evidence does not support strong statements, and the panel writes a protocol instead of a
recommendation set. **This one is explicitly "based on expert consensus and registry data", which is the
honest label.**

**The treatment schedule is the most immediately usable content and it is unusually specific for a consensus
document**: high-dose steroids held for **2–3 weeks** then tapered over **12–18 months**, intravenous
cyclophosphamide for induction, then azathioprine, methotrexate or mycophenolate for **18–24 months**. **Two
years of immunosuppression in a disease diagnosed without a biomarker**, which is why the document spends its
last section on osteoporosis, infection and metabolic prophylaxis. **Relapse in "a significant proportion" is
as precise as the abstract gets, and a proportion is what one would want.**

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.revmed.2026.07.007)

## Retired rather than carried: the ESPEN "partially revised" diff

**This archive has been trying since early September to find out which recommendations changed in the ESPEN
practical ICU nutrition guideline.** The phrase "partially revised" was flagged on 10 October as a phrase to
stop on, and the item has appeared on the backlog in every entry since. **Today it is closed as unobtainable
by this archive's means, with what is and is not established recorded.**

**The record has no PubMed Central identifier, is not in Europe PMC's full text, and is not open access.**
There is no route from here to the document. **The 626-character deposit is provenance only** — and it is the
case that produced the archive's rule that an abstract naming no method and no non-year digit is a provenance
note rather than a summary.

**What the abstract does establish, which is more than nothing and was not noticed until today.** It says the
2019 guideline **"has been shortened and partially revised"**, that the result is **"a practical guideline …
shortened and illustrated by flow charts"**, and — the sentence that matters — **"All the items discussed in
the previous guidelines are included as well as special conditions."** **So the coverage did not shrink.**
Nothing was dropped; the document was reformatted and some recommendations were revised. **That answers half
the standing question: the scope is unchanged.**

**What cannot be established is the half that mattered: which recommendations were revised, and in which
direction.** For a guideline whose own abstract advertises partial revision, that is the only thing a reader
needs, and it is not in the deposit and not reachable. **Carrying the item for another six weeks would not
change that.** **It is retired with the gap stated, and the general rule it leaves behind: a document that
announces a partial revision without a change log is, for an archive working from deposits, a document that
cannot be read — and the right response is to say so once rather than to list it indefinitely.**
