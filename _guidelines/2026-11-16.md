**No new or updated guidance appeared from SFAR, SPILF, SRLF, ESAIC, ESC, ESPEN, ESICM, SCCM, IACTS, EACTS,
STS or ASA.** **What the day produced instead is an instrument that works better, and the first things it
found.**

## 1. The filter, rebuilt and validated

**Yesterday's 16 October test returned 60 in-scope guidance records of which 47 were irrelevant — wild boar
injuries, periprosthetic joint infection, a haemodialysis diet audit — because the scope terms were matched
against the whole record rather than the title and journal.** The action logged yesterday was to narrow it.
**Narrowing a filter is easy and silently losing true positives is the risk, so the change was validated
before it was adopted.**

| Test | Old filter | New filter | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Current window, 9–16 November | 25 records | **11** | **−56%** |
| The 16 October test set | 60 records | **16** | **−73%** |
| **EACTS documents retained in the 16 October set** | 5 of 5 | **5 of 5** | **no loss** |

**The five EACTS expert consensus documents are the test that matters, because they are the known true
positives that made yesterday's investigation necessary** — and four of them have titles that name no scope
term at all. "EACTS Expert Consensus Document on aortic valve repair and valve-sparing aortic root
replacement procedures" contains no word the old filter keyed on. **They survive the narrowing because the
new filter also keys on JOURNAL, and *European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery* is on the list.**
**That is the design point: a title-only filter would have lost them, and a full-text filter drowns them.**

**Everything the new filter retained on 16 October is plausibly in scope** — guidelines on renal replacement
therapy and stress ulcers in critically ill patients (both already held, both reported in October), neonatal
therapeutic hypothermia, the five EACTS documents, an oncologic critical care Delphi, vancomycin dosing in
intensive care, propofol waste management, chest wall trauma guideline adherence, intensive care nursing
research priorities, and the PUMA extubation editorial below. **Nothing in the retained set is obviously
wrong, and nothing in the dropped 44 is obviously missed.**

**One methodological near-miss, recorded because it nearly produced a false claim.** **The first validation
run was issued with `resultType=idlist`, which returns identifiers and no titles — so the script reported
"EACTS records retained: 0" and would have condemned the new filter.** The run was repeated with `lite` and
returned 5 of 5. **RULE: a recall test cannot be run on a result type that does not carry the field being
tested. Check what the response contains before believing what the script says about it.** **This is the
third tool-level error this archive has caught in two weeks, after the `core` paging misdiagnosis of
5 November and its withdrawal on 13 November.**

## 2. The tenth threshold scheme, and the first that is not a Delphi

**[Phenotype-based appropriateness of IGM-enriched polyclonal immunoglobulin use in critical illness: report
of an expert consensus](https://doi.org/10.1186/s44158-026-00459-8)** · *Journal of Anesthesia, Analgesia
and Critical Care* · **published 26 September 2026 — routed here from yesterday's brief** · paywalled in the
deposit read; a second open-access deposit carries no abstract

**An Italian multidisciplinary panel — intensive care, infectious diseases, transplant medicine,
oncology — applying a modified RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method to seven clinical phenotypes.**

> …applied a modified RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method. The process integrated a structured literature
> review … with anonymous 9-point Likert scale ratings. **Indications were classified according to the
> median score as appropriate (7-9), uncertain (4-6), or inappropriate (1-3), and interpanel disagreement
> was assessed using the Interpercentile Range Adjusted for Symmetry.**

**This is methodologically new to the catalogue and the difference is not cosmetic.** **Every one of the nine
schemes recorded here so far counts panellists or averages their scores: at least 70% agreeing, or 75%, or
90% strong and 80% moderate, or a mean above 80%, or two-sided rules that count disagreement as well.**
**This one classifies by the MEDIAN rating and measures disagreement with a separate statistic designed for
the purpose.** **A median is robust to a few extreme raters in a way a mean is not, and reporting
disagreement separately rather than folding it into the threshold is the better instrument** — which is why
the RAND/UCLA method exists. **Tenth scheme, first non-Delphi, and the first to separate the question "what
does the panel think?" from "do they agree?"**

**On substance, the panel's own answers are notable for where they stop.** IgM-enriched immunoglobulin was
rated **appropriate** for severe community-acquired pneumonia with hyperinflammation **plus shock**
(median 8), for IgM below 60 mg/dL (8), for prior immunosuppression (8), for overwhelming septic shock with
severe hyperinflammation (8) and for toxin-mediated syndromes (9) — **and merely uncertain for
hyperinflammation WITHOUT shock (median 6), for prophylaxis in high-risk transplant recipients (5), and for
post-cardiac-surgery hyperinflammation (6).**

**The pattern is consistent and clinically legible: the panel endorses the therapy where there is shock or a
measured immunoglobulin deficiency, and declines to endorse it where there is inflammation alone.**
**That is the phenotype argument doing real work rather than decorating a recommendation** — and the
document says the quiet part, that decades of immunomodulatory adjuncts have produced nothing with
consistent efficacy, so the rationale here is low circulating immunoglobulin rather than inflammation as
such. **The consensus closes by asking for adequately powered trials to define timing, dosing and
responsive subgroups, and notes that adverse events are "likely to be underreported".**

## 3. Sugammadex given against the product information, in four of five dialysis patients

**[Sugammadex Use In Severe Renal Dysfunction Despite Regulatory Recommendations: A Secondary Analysis Of
The Prospective Multicentre DELPO Cohort](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accpm.2026.101993)** · *Anaesthesia
Critical Care & Pain Medicine* · **published 15 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Secondary analysis of the prospective DELPO cohort: adults at 43 Spanish hospitals receiving general
anaesthesia, neuromuscular blockade and pharmacological reversal.** 1,331 patients, of whom **35 were in
GFR category G4 or G5 or receiving dialysis — 2.6% of the cohort — including 20 on dialysis.**

> Sugammadex was used in 1,278 of 1,331 patients overall (96.0%), in 1248 of 1,296 patients in GFR
> categories G1-G3 (96.3%), and in **30 of 35 patients in GFR categories G4-G5 or receiving dialysis (85.7%;
> 95% CI, 70.6-93.7%). Among the 20 patients receiving dialysis, sugammadex was administered to 16 (80.0%;
> 95% CI, 58.4-91.9%)** … despite current European product information not recommending its use in this
> population.

**The arithmetic is internally consistent: 1,296 plus 35 is 1,331, and 1,278 minus 1,248 is exactly the 30.**

**This is a guidance document in the negative — a measurement of how far practice has moved away from a
regulatory recommendation — and it belongs in this watch for that reason.** **European product information
does not recommend sugammadex when the estimated glomerular filtration rate is below 30 or the patient is
dialysed, because the drug and the sugammadex-rocuronium complex are cleared by the kidney. Spanish
anaesthetists used it in 86% of such patients anyway, and in 80% of patients on dialysis.**

**Two things bound what can be concluded, and both matter.**

**The denominator is 35 patients and the confidence interval says so: 70.6% to 93.7%.** The dialysis
subgroup is 20 people. **A practice pattern described from 35 patients in one country is a signal that the
regulatory restriction is being disregarded, not a measurement of how often.**

**And the denominator is conditioned on having received pharmacological reversal at all.** **The statistic is
"among patients who were reversed, what was used" — so a patient in whom reversal was avoided entirely, or
who was left to recover spontaneously, is not in it.** **That is the question a reader actually wants
answered — what do anaesthetists do when facing a dialysis patient who needs reversal? — and this design
cannot answer it.**

**My reading.** **The finding is real and the direction is unsurprising: neostigmine has its own problems,
sugammadex works, and the renal caution rests on pharmacokinetics rather than on reported harm.** **What the
paper usefully establishes is that the gap between the label and practice is not a few outliers — it is the
overwhelming majority of cases in a 43-hospital prospective cohort.** **Either the restriction needs
revisiting with evidence, or the practice does; a 2.6% subgroup in one cohort is where that conversation
starts, not where it ends.**

## 4. A protocol followed a quarter of the time, and following it did not help

**[Vancomycin dosing and monitoring in the intensive care unit: Guideline adherence and
efficacy](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aucc.2026.101691)** · *Australian Critical Care* · **published
7 October 2026 — surfaced by the new filter, never reported here** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Single-centre retrospective audit of a newly implemented vancomycin prescribing protocol in a mixed
medical-surgical intensive care unit, 148 patients, May to July 2024.**

> Protocol compliance was 82% for the loading dose (101/123), 49% for maintenance dosing (49/101), and 51%
> for timing of monitoring (46/91). **Compliance across all protocol domains occurred in 27% of instances
> (17/64). Therapeutic drug levels were achieved in only 20% of prescribing episodes.** … **Protocol
> compliance was not associated with therapeutic levels.**

**The last sentence is the finding and it is the most uncomfortable result in today's entry.** **Adherence
was low — full adherence in 27% of cases — and adherence did not help: therapeutic concentrations were
reached in 20% of episodes, and compliance was not associated with reaching them.**

**That combination forecloses the easy conclusion.** **An audit showing poor adherence usually ends with a
recommendation to improve adherence. This one cannot, because the patients whose prescribers followed the
protocol did no better.** **So either the protocol's dosing is wrong for this population, or fixed-dose
protocols cannot hit a vancomycin target in intensive care at all** — and the paper's own risk factors point
at the second: **augmented renal clearance (odds ratio 15.1 for subtherapeutic levels), central nervous
system infection (9.5, where the target is higher), and impaired renal function (3.6 for supratherapeutic).**
**Those are the three states in which a weight-based protocol is guaranteed to miss, and they are common in
intensive care.**

**Two cautions on the numbers, and the first is structural.** **The four compliance domains have four
different denominators — 123, 101, 91 and 64 — and the abstract does not explain why they shrink.** **This
archive has flagged denominator mismatches three times in a month in trial reports; here the same issue
appears as a cascade, where each successive domain is assessed in fewer patients than the last.** **A
compliance rate of 27% "across all domains" computed on 64 of 148 patients is a statement about the 64.**

**And the odds ratios are enormous with intervals to match: 2.7 to 41.3, 4.0 to 80.4, 1.1 to 13.2 — log
widths of 2.5 to 3.0, which is a 12- to 20-fold span.** From 148 patients and a minority of events, these
are directional findings and not effect sizes. **The one at 1.1 to 13.2 barely excludes unity.**

**My reading.** **The practical message is not "follow the protocol better" — the data refuse that — it is
that vancomycin in intensive care needs concentration-guided dosing rather than protocol-guided dosing**,
which is what the augmented-clearance and renal-impairment findings say in two directions. **A single
centre, three months, 148 patients; but the internal contradiction between adherence and effect is the kind
of result that does not need a large sample to be worth acting on.**

## 5. Eight years of change credited to one guideline

**[Clinical and Economic Impact of Non-Opioid Analgesia Guidelines in Pediatric Adenotonsillectomy: A
Multi-Institutional Database Study](https://doi.org/10.1002/ohn.70510)** · *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck
Surgery* · **published 10 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Retrospective cohort in a federated electronic health record database. Children under 18 having
adenotonsillectomy, stratified by surgical era: January 2014 to December 2017 against January 2021 to
December 2025, 1:1 propensity matched at 66,844 per cohort — 133,688 patients.**

> …the pre-2019 cohort demonstrated significantly higher risk ratios (RR) of dehydration (1.27; 95% CI
> 1.17-1.38), IV fluid needs (1.27; 95% CI 1.17-1.37), ED visits (1.21; 95% CI 1.16-1.27), and
> nausea/vomiting (1.24; 95% CI 1.12-1.39) compared to the post-2019 cohort (P < .0001). Postoperative
> hemorrhage rates were statistically stable (P = .145). Post-2019 opioid prescriptions decreased
> significantly (RR 1.47; 95% CI 1.44-1.50; P < .0001), notably with a **25-fold reduction in codeine
> prescriptions**. **The NNT to prevent one ED visit was 101.7, resulting in an estimated cost avoidance of
> $3.2 million within the studied cohort.**

**The design problem is in the title.** **"Impact of ... Guidelines" is a causal claim, and the comparison is
2014–2017 against 2021–2025 with propensity matching on patient characteristics.** **Propensity matching
balances the covariates it is given; it cannot balance eight years.** **Between those two windows sit a
pandemic, changed admission and discharge thresholds, changed fluid and antiemetic practice, changed
emergency department utilisation, and a national opioid policy environment that moved independently of any
otolaryngology guideline.** **The authors' own wording is appropriately hedged — outcomes fell "concomitant
with" reduced prescribing, "suggesting" consistency — and the title and the cost figure are not.**

**The mechanism they propose is coherent and is the paper's best argument.** **Less opioid means less nausea
and vomiting (risk ratio 1.24 in the expected direction), which means better oral intake, which means less
dehydration (1.27), fewer intravenous fluids (1.27) and fewer emergency visits (1.21).** **That chain hangs
together, each link is measured, and haemorrhage — which opioid-sparing should not affect — did not change
(P = .145), which is the right negative control and the authors deserve credit for reporting it.**

**The cost figure should be read with its arithmetic exposed.** **A number needed to treat of 101.7 across
66,844 patients implies about 657 emergency visits prevented; $3.2 million across 657 visits is roughly
$4,870 per visit.** **That is the assumption carrying the headline, and whether a paediatric
post-tonsillectomy emergency attendance costs $4,870 is a question about American billing rather than about
analgesia.** **Reported so the reader can judge it, since the abstract does not state it.**

**The one unambiguous result is the exposure itself: opioid prescriptions down with a risk ratio of 1.47,
interval 1.44 to 1.50, and a 25-fold reduction in codeine.** **Codeine in children after
adenotonsillectomy carries a specific and well-documented danger in ultra-rapid metabolisers, and a 25-fold
fall is the clearest evidence here that guidance changed behaviour.** **That much the design does support.**

*Anaesthesia* · **published 7 October 2026 — surfaced by the new filter** · **no abstract is deposited**

**Reported as identified and not summarised.** **This archive holds the PUMA airway guidance already, from
entries in August and September; this is an editorial commenting on the extubation guidelines, and its title
poses the question that every expanding guideline faces.** **Zero characters are deposited, so nothing about
its argument can be quoted** — and this archive's rule is to say that rather than infer a position from a
title, however suggestive. **Named as a gap with the route attempted: the index has no abstract, and the
journal is paywalled.**

**It is worth recording for one reason beyond its content: it is the kind of document the old filter would
have found only by accident and the new filter found because it keys on the journal.** *Anaesthesia* is on
the journal list; "tracheal extubation" is not a phrase the scope terms contain.
