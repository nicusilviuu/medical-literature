A thin Saturday for new guidelines — the sweep returned **23 records, four in or near scope** — so this entry
is built mostly from the backlog, and it closes **two gaps this watch had named itself.**

On 30 September it recorded that **the archive contained no stroke guidelines at all.** On 2 October it
recorded that **it contained no neurotrauma guideline**, and called the French one the highest-value
unblocked item. Both are addressed below. The one genuinely new document, published yesterday, turns out to
belong in the same entry.

**One correction to my own backlog note before starting.** The traumatic brain injury guideline was filed
under the SPILF backlog on 2 October because the SPILF seam audit surfaced it. **It is not an SPILF
document.** It is led by the **French Society of Neurosurgery (SFNC)**, with SPILF as one of nine
participating societies. Attribution corrected here and in the notes.

## 1. SFNC and eight partner societies — the acute neurosurgical phase of traumatic brain injury

**Published:** *Neuro-Chirurgie*, **23 May 2025** — **sixteen months old**, 5 citations. **The first
neurotrauma guideline reported in this archive.**
Manet, de Courson, Capel, Joubert, Chivoret, Faillot, Balanca, Bani-Sadr, Cardinale, Coca, Cotton, Esnault,
Gallet, Gazzola et al.

> **Objective** To develop a multidisciplinary French framework addressing neurosurgical management in the
> initial phase of traumatic brain injury (TBI) in adults and children.
>
> **Design** A panel of **29 experts** was formed at the request of the French Society of Neurosurgery
> (SFNC), with the participation of the French Society of Pediatric Neurosurgery (SFNCP), French Society of
> Private-Practice Neurosurgeons (SFNCL), **French-Speaking Neurocritical Care and Neuro-Anesthesiology
> Society (ANARLF)**, **French Society of Anesthesia, Critical Care and Perioperative Medicine (SFAR)**,
> French-Speaking Pediatric Emergency and Intensive Care Group (GFRUP), French Society of Neuroradiology
> (SFNR), French-Speaking Infectious Diseases Society (SPILF), and the French Society of Physical Medicine
> and Rehabilitation (SOFMER).
>
> **Methods** Questions were formulated using the PICO format, grouped into **7 categories: 1. Factors of
> poor prognosis, 2. Extradural hematoma, 3. Acute subdural hematoma, 4. Skull-base fracture and dural tear,
> 5. Penetrating traumatic brain injury, 6. Post-traumatic cerebrospinal fluid disorder, and 7. Pediatric
> specificities.**
>
> **Results** … application of the GRADE® method resulted in the formulation of **45 recommendations. Strong
> consensus was reached for all recommendations at the first round of rating.**

**Interpretation.** **Strong consensus on all 45 recommendations at the first round** is the detail that
distinguishes this from every other formalised French document catalogued here. The SFAR and SRLF documents
all required **two or more voting rounds with amendments**; the procedural sedation RFE needed two rounds and
three amendments, the nutrition guidelines four rounds. **A 29-expert panel drawn from nine societies
agreeing on 45 recommendations without a second round is either a remarkably settled field or a sign that
the contested questions were not asked.**

The categories suggest the former, and it is worth saying why: **extradural haematoma, acute subdural
haematoma, skull-base fracture, penetrating injury and post-traumatic CSF disorder are surgical-decision
questions with decades of observational data and reasonably clear thresholds.** This is not a document about
intracranial pressure targets, osmotherapy, or decompressive craniectomy timing — the genuinely disputed
areas of neurocritical care. **It is about when to operate, and that is a narrower and better-settled
question than how to manage the brain afterwards.**

**What the abstract does not give is a GRADE breakdown**, and that is a real limitation given how
informative those breakdowns have been in this archive. The SFAR and SRLF documents all state how many
recommendations rest on high, low or expert-opinion evidence; **this one states only that GRADE was applied
and that consensus was unanimous.** Unanimity on 45 recommendations tells you about the panel, not about the
literature. **Without the breakdown, nothing can be said here about how well-evidenced these 45
recommendations are**, and the full text is the only route to it.

**A new society for the sweep list, found the same way the others were.** **ANARLF** — the French-speaking
Neurocritical Care and Neuro-Anaesthesiology Society — is squarely in this project's primary scope and this
watch had never heard of it. It is now on the list. **Every structural gap this watch has found has been
found by reading participating-society lists off documents rather than by thinking harder about which
societies exist**, and this is the ninth time.

**Honest note on what this gap actually was.** TBI had appeared here twice before, both times incidentally:
as a single recommendation inside [the ESICM fluids
guideline](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-02/) — isotonic saline rather than albumin, very low
certainty — and as [a Chinese expert consensus on TBI in mass casualty incidents, logged and not
pursued](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-08/). **So the gap was partly visible a month ago and was
not acted on.** That second item is also an instance of the Chinese-society access gap named yesterday.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuchi.2025.101686)

## 2. ESO — blood pressure in acute ischaemic stroke and intracerebral haemorrhage

**Published:** *European Stroke Journal*, **1 May 2026** — five months old, 3 citations, **open access in
PubMed Central with a PDF.** GRADE, ESO standard operating procedure, with its own systematic reviews and
meta-analyses of RCTs.
Sandset, Palaiodimou, Jahr, Ho, Fischer, Katsanos, Krishnan, Maïer, Mistry, Sacco, Schönenberger, Steiner,
Tsivgoulis

> …this update provides revised, evidence-based recommendations across **8 key clinical questions**…
>
> **We advise against routine pre-hospital BP lowering in suspected stroke (moderate-certainty evidence).**
> In AIS patients undergoing reperfusion therapy, we recommend maintaining **BP < 185/110 mmHg before the
> bolus of intravenous thrombolysis and < 180/105 mmHg during and for 24 h after** intravenous thrombolysis
> (low-certainty evidence) and/or mechanical thrombectomy (moderate-certainty evidence). **We recommend
> against intensively lowering systolic BP < 140 mmHg in the first 24 h after successful mechanical
> thrombectomy (high-certainty evidence).** Routine use of **vasopressors to raise BP** in AIS patients with
> neurological deterioration not treated with reperfusion **is discouraged (low-certainty evidence).** In
> acute ICH, the net clinical benefit of intensive BP lowering **remains uncertain**; however, **expert
> consensus supports early systolic BP reduction to < 140 mmHg in patients with small-to-moderate
> haematomas** to limit haematoma expansion.
>
> …**the quality of evidence remains moderate to very low**, limited by a lack of high-quality RCTs,
> methodological issues, inconsistent results and study heterogeneity. **Consequently, most recommendations
> are weak and supported by expert consensus.**

**Interpretation.** **One recommendation in this document carries high-certainty evidence, and it is a
recommendation not to do something: do not intensively lower systolic pressure below 140 mmHg in the first
24 hours after successful thrombectomy.**

That is worth setting against everything this archive has accumulated on arterial pressure. **Across the
pressure-targeting literature reported here — a 15-RCT Bayesian meta-analysis, the HPI trial, the PRESSURE
trial in 1,900 children, and [yesterday's 151,036-patient threshold
study](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-03/) — the single high-certainty graded recommendation anywhere is
negative.** Not "target this number" but "do not drive it down."

**And the document contains its own internal tension, stated plainly rather than hidden.** In ischaemic
stroke after thrombectomy, intensive lowering below 140 is recommended **against** with high certainty. In
intracerebral haemorrhage, early reduction to **below 140** is **supported by expert consensus.** The same
threshold, opposite directions, in two conditions that present identically until imaging. **That is not
inconsistency — it is the difference between a reperfused brain that needs flow and a bleeding one that
needs pressure control** — but it means the number 140 means opposite things depending on a CT scan, and a
guideline that makes that explicit is doing its readers a service.

**The self-assessment is unusually blunt for a society document: "most recommendations are weak and supported
by expert consensus."** Eight clinical questions, one high-certainty answer. Compare the French documents
catalogued this week, where the proportion of expert opinion ran from 40% to 88% — **ESO is reporting the
same reality in prose rather than in a table.**

**Half the stroke gap remains open.** The **2026 AHA/ASA guideline for early management of acute ischaemic
stroke** (10.1161/str.0000000000000513, with two July 2026 corrections) is still unreported. This one was
taken first because it is in PMC with a PDF and therefore actually readable.

[Read the guideline](https://doi.org/10.1093/esj/aakag004)

## 3. An ERAS protocol for cerebral bypass in Moyamoya disease

**Published:** *Neurosurgery Practice*, **2 October 2026** — **published yesterday; the only genuinely new
document in today's sweep.** Multidisciplinary group spanning cerebrovascular neurosurgery, neurointensive
care, vascular neurology, **neurophysiological monitoring and anaesthesiology.**
Hallan, Padmanaban, Jareczek, Gravener, Hong, Shah, Feidt, Coleman, Zacko, Hazard, Church

> Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a progressive cerebrovascular disorder treated primarily with surgical
> revascularization… **Despite established surgical techniques, standardized perioperative care protocols
> remain limited**… ERAS protocols have demonstrated improved outcomes across surgical disciplines **but have
> not been systematically applied to cerebral bypass surgery for MMD.**
>
> **Methods** … literature review of PubMed … The GRADE system was used to evaluate evidence quality and
> recommendation strength.
>
> **Results Thirty-two studies met inclusion criteria.** Seven primary categories were identified: **patient
> selection, preoperative optimization, anesthesia and analgesia, intraoperative neuromonitoring, surgical
> strategy, postoperative management, and follow-up and recovery protocols. Strong recommendations were
> developed for 34 specific interventions** across the perioperative continuum.
>
> **Conclusion** … **the first comprehensive ERAS protocol for direct cerebral bypass surgery** in MMD.

**Interpretation.** **Two of the seven categories are anaesthesia and intraoperative neuromonitoring**, and
an anaesthesiologist and a neurophysiological monitoring specialist sat on the development group. That is why
this is in a guidelines watch for anaesthesia and critical care rather than a neurosurgical footnote: **ERAS
protocols are perioperative medicine, and this one allocates two-sevenths of its structure to the
anaesthetist's territory.**

**The number to be sceptical about is 34 strong recommendations from 32 included studies.** That is **more
than one strong recommendation per study** in a rare disease, from a PubMed-only literature review. ERAS
protocols in better-studied surgery are typically built from hundreds of trials; **a protocol that produces
34 strong recommendations from 32 studies is either extrapolating heavily from other surgical disciplines —
which is legitimate and is how ERAS has always spread — or applying "strong" more loosely than the GRADE
framework intends.** The abstract reports no evidence-level breakdown, so which cannot be determined from it,
and the distinction matters because **a strong recommendation on low-certainty evidence is exactly what GRADE
asks authors to justify explicitly.**

**Set against the French TBI document above, the contrast is instructive.** Both are neurosurgical, both used
GRADE, both report unanimity or strength across their whole output, and **neither gives a breakdown.** The
SFAR and SRLF documents reported in this archive over the past four days all did. **A reader cannot tell,
from any of these three neurosurgical abstracts, how much evidence sits behind the recommendations** — which
is a pattern worth watching rather than a criticism of any one of them.

[Read the protocol](https://doi.org/10.1227/neuprac.0000000000000286)

## 4. Intraoperative neuromonitoring in spine deformity surgery

**Published:** *Spine Deformity*, **28 September 2026** — five days old. Modified Delphi, **16
multidisciplinary experts** including **paediatric and adult orthopaedic and neurosurgical spine surgeons,
anaesthesiologists, neurologists and neurophysiologists.** **Reported now after being deferred three times**
with its abstract already in hand.
Anari, Park, Brown, Elfilali, Kourpas, Bruins, Franco, Mendiratta, Pinyavat, Umina, Strantzas, Sethi, Lewis
et al.

> To develop expert consensus-based guidelines for the execution of IONM in **routine adolescent idiopathic
> scoliosis (AIS) and high-risk spinal deformity cases**, with the goal to reduce variability in monitoring
> practices…
>
> **Three consecutive rounds**, each including an online survey and a virtual meeting… followed by a fourth
> online survey to finalize consensus. **An 80% agreement threshold** … Items that did not reach consensus
> were refined based on feedback and redistributed in subsequent rounds. … **All 16 (100%) experts completed
> the final survey.**
>
> **The expert panel reached consensus on 27 items in the AIS cohort and 25 items in the high-risk cohort;
> final consensus items were organized into IONM Timeline Checklists** for the AIS and high-risk cohorts.

**Interpretation.** **The deliverable is a checklist organised along the operative timeline, not a list of
recommendations**, and that is the most useful thing about it. Monitoring failures in spinal deformity
surgery are rarely failures of knowledge — they are failures of sequence: baselines not established before
positioning, a signal change noticed without an agreed response, anaesthetic depth altered at the wrong
moment. **A timeline checklist addresses the thing that actually goes wrong**, which is the same reasoning
behind [the SFAR airway document's dedicated human-factors
field](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-30/).

**It connects to anaesthetic technique directly**, which is why it belongs here. Motor-evoked potential
monitoring **requires avoiding halogenated agents and neuromuscular blockade** — so IONM is one of the
clinical reasons intravenous anaesthesia is mandated, a link set out in [the 2 October
brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-02/). **Two of the sixteen panellists are anaesthesiologists**, and
the categories of a monitoring protocol and of an anaesthetic plan are not separable here.

**Two things to note about the method, and the second is small but real.** An **80% threshold across three
rounds with iterative refinement of non-consensus items** is a more demanding process than the ≥70%
single-threshold Delphi reported in [yesterday's entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-02/) — but
**it is still a measure of panel agreement, not of evidence**, and like the two documents above it reports no
evidence grading at all. **And the participation percentages do not quite compute:** 13 of 16 experts is
**81.25%**, and the paper reports **81.6%** twice; the other four figures (87.5%, 87.5%, 75.0%, 68.8%) are
exact. Almost certainly a typo for 81.3%, noted because the arithmetic check has caught substantive
discrepancies before and reporting only the ones that turn out to matter would be selective.

**Indexed as plain `Journal Article`** — no `Practice Guideline` or `Consensus Statement` tag, despite being
titled "Best Practice Guidelines." The pubType-unreliability note from 30 September stands.

[Read the guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43390-026-01559-9)

### Routed to the literature brief, not reported here

***CJEM*, 2 October — "Brain injury guidelines for the management of traumatic brain injury: a systematic
review and meta-analysis"** (10.1007/s43678-026-01218-y). Despite the title, **this is a research article —
a diagnostic-accuracy meta-analysis of the Brain Injury Guidelines (BIG) decision rule, not a guideline** —
so it goes to the brief under the same rule that sent the *Journal of Anesthesia* peri-thrombectomy review
there on 30 September.

It is flagged prominently because the numbers deserve a full write-up rather than a tail note. From 4,733
studies screened and 47 included, 23 suitable for meta-analysis, **pooled sensitivity for BIG1 to rule out
neurological deterioration, neurosurgical intervention and death was 98.3%, 98.7% and 98.2% — with pooled
specificity of 12.7%, 14.4% and 12.8%.** The specificity confidence intervals are enormous: **1.5–58.7% for
the first.** A rule being used to avoid neurosurgical consultation and repeat imaging, with 98% sensitivity
and 13% specificity, is a rule that almost never misses and almost never reassures. **First in the brief
queue.**
