## The structural finding: four consensus documents, four methods, no strengths

Before the documents themselves, the comparison that this archive's recommendation-structure catalogue exists
for. All four landed in the same two-day index window.

| Document | Method as stated | Statements | Strength or certainty attached |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ESICM, human-centred partnership** | 20 PICO questions, rapid reviews, two-round eDelphi, ratification meeting | **53 drafted → 43 endorsed** | **none** |
| **Carcinoid crisis** | modified Delphi, four voting rounds, defined consensus rule | **41** (12 pre / 27 intra / 2 post) | **none** |
| **Italian aortic stenosis** | *"a narrative, evidence-based synthesis"* | **no count given** | **none** |
| **Singapore sustainability** | *"iterative consensus building through discussion and consultation"* | **no count given** | **none** |

**Two of the four report how many statements they produced and what threshold counted as consensus. Two
report neither. None of the four attaches a grade, class, level or certainty to any individual statement.**

**This archive has a rule for reading that, and it matters here.** On 22 October it recorded: *a missing grade
distribution has at least two causes — withholding, and a document class that does not require one; read the
design section for a format decision before scoring the disclosure axis.* That rule came from the SFAR/SFPC
medication-error document, which was **formally reclassified from Formalized Expert Recommendations to
Professional Practice Guidelines before drafting**, because twenty years of literature contained almost nothing
powered to answer its primary question.

**For the ESICM statement there appears to be exactly such a format decision, and it is on the record.**
ESICM's 2025 ethics section report, surfaced by today's society search, is reported to state that **a consensus
statement was chosen over a guideline because of the limited randomised trials available on the topic.**
**That is a web-search-derived lead and not a fact** — this archive's standing rule, reinforced three days
running last week — **but the published abstract is consistent with it**: it describes the methodology as
*"evidence-informed"* rather than evidence-based, uses **rapid reviews** rather than full systematic reviews,
and attaches no grades. **So ESICM is the third documented instance of a society choosing a weaker document
class in advance rather than issuing graded recommendations the evidence could not carry.**

**For the other three, nothing in the abstracts says why.** That is the distinction worth keeping: **an
ungraded consensus statement with a stated reason is a different object from an ungraded consensus statement
with no stated reason**, and only one of today's four is the former.

**Set against this week's other end of the range:** the [20 October entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-20/)
reported the SRLF's atrial fibrillation recommendations with its grade distribution and its abandonments in
the abstract — eighteen recommendations, two gradeable, sixteen expert opinions, six questions dropped — and
the [26 October entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/) reported 111 class-and-level pairs from
APHRS Part III. **Today's four sit at the opposite end, and three of them without explanation.**

## 1. ESICM — the society's journal has published it and the society's website has not noticed

**Society · Published:** **European Society of Intensive Care Medicine**, *Intensive Care Medicine*,
**26 October 2026** — two days old. **The first ESICM consensus statement to appear in this archive.**

**How it was found, which is the point.** **It did not come from the society route.** It surfaced in **this
morning's literature-brief sweep** of the Europe PMC index, was recognised as guideline-tracking material
rather than research, and was deliberately held back from the brief for this entry. **Then today's society
search found ESICM's own guidelines page, which still lists the document as "development in progress".**

**So the society's website says the statement is unpublished while the society's journal carries the finished
article with a full abstract.** **That is the sharpest demonstration this archive has recorded of why the
index route keeps beating the society route: a society site can be behind its own journal.** The search result
is not useless — **the six domains named in ESICM's 2025 planning record match the six domains in the
published abstract exactly**, which corroborates the document's identity while being wrong about its status.

**The method, in numbers:**

- **An international multidisciplinary panel of 46 members**, including patient and family representatives.
- **Rapid reviews addressing 20 PICO questions across six domains**: person-centred care; communication and
  transparency; emotional well-being; equity, diversity and inclusion; ICU environment; technology and
  innovation.
- **53 draft statements**, evaluated through a two-round eDelphi.
- **201 international stakeholders in round 1, 149 in round 2** — a **25.9% drop-out between rounds**.
- **43 statements endorsed** at the final consensus meeting. **Ten of the 53 did not survive, 18.9%.**

**The key recommendations, quoted:**

> flexible family visitation, structured interdisciplinary communication, empathy-based staff training,
> psychological support for patients **and ICU professionals**, culturally responsive care, **environmental
> interventions to improve sleep and reduce delirium**, and the integration of digital supported technologies.

**Interpretation.** **Two things in that list are not soft, and they are the two worth extracting.**
**"Environmental interventions to improve sleep and reduce delirium" is a clinical intervention with a hard
outcome**, and it connects directly to the circadian-oriented sedation study logged in the brief queue
yesterday and to this archive's standing delirium thread. **"Psychological support for patients and ICU
professionals" puts staff inside the scope of a clinical consensus statement**, which is a genuine extension
of what such a document usually covers.

**The rest is a framework, and the document says so** — *"a comprehensive framework for clinicians with
evidence-informed directions"*, with outcomes that **"may improve"**. **That hedge is correct and should be
read as the honest form of a statement built on rapid reviews of a literature with few trials, not as
weakness.**

**What is missing is what the table above records: no statement carries a strength.** **With 43 statements and
no grades, a reader cannot tell which of them rest on evidence and which on the panel** — and the drop-out of
ten drafts tells you only that ten were less agreed than the rest, not how well-supported the surviving 43
are. **The 25.9% attrition between eDelphi rounds is also unexplained, and in a two-round Delphi it matters:
the second round's consensus is the consensus of whoever came back.**

[Read the statement](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00134-026-08626-9) · *Intensive Care Medicine*

## 2. Forty-one statements on carcinoid crisis, and the two that change practice are both reversals

**Panel · Published:** international modified Delphi, *BJS Open*, **1 September 2026** — nearly two months
old, included because **carcinoid crisis has never appeared in this archive** and because the document is
squarely an anaesthesia one despite the journal.

**The problem is a genuine intraoperative emergency**: a patient with a midgut or bronchopulmonary
neuroendocrine tumour can decompensate catastrophically during surgical handling, and practice has long
revolved around prophylactic octreotide.

**The method is unusually well specified, and that deserves recording:**

- **44 experts, multidisciplinary and international, plus 3 patient partners.**
- **Four rounds of voting**; statements rated on a 1–7 Likert scale with anonymous iterative feedback.
- **Consensus defined precisely: median score between 5 and 7, AND no more than 13 panellists rating outside
  the score category containing the median.** **A defined dispersion criterion as well as a central one is
  better than most Delphi documents manage** — a median alone can hide a split panel.
- **41 statements reached final consensus: 12 preoperative, 27 intraoperative, 2 postoperative.**

**The distribution is itself the finding about scope. Twenty-seven of 41 statements — 65.9% — are
intraoperative, and two are postoperative.** **This is a consensus document whose centre of gravity is the
anaesthetic**, covering monitoring, prophylaxis and treatment of crisis, and a reader should not be put off by
its appearing in a surgical journal.

**And the practice-shifting content, quoted:**

> The principal practice-shifting recommendations were that **somatostatin analogues are not necessary for
> prophylaxis before surgery or on the day of operation**, and **treatment in the event of carcinoid crisis
> should start with vasopressors, with short-acting somatostatin analogues used as an adjunct.**

**Interpretation.** **Both headline recommendations are reversals, and that is rarer and more useful than a
new recommendation.** Routine preoperative and day-of-surgery somatostatin analogue prophylaxis is
long-standing practice in many centres; this panel says it is unnecessary. **And the treatment order is
inverted: vasopressors first, somatostatin analogue as adjunct** — which is the opposite of treating carcinoid
crisis as primarily a hormonal event to be suppressed, and reframes it as a vasoplegic one to be supported.
**For an anaesthetist that changes what is drawn up before the case and what is reached for during it.**

**The caution is the one the table above records.** **Forty-one statements, no strength attached to any of
them**, and in a document whose principal content is the removal of a long-standing prophylaxis, **a reader
needs to know whether "not necessary" rests on trials, on registry data, or on the panel's collective
judgement.** The abstract does not say. **A reversal recommendation carries a higher evidentiary burden than
an addition, because the practice being removed was presumably adopted for a reason**, and this document does
not show the reader where the burden was met.

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjsopen/zrag159) · *BJS Open*

## 3. A national surgical working group reading down a Class I recommendation its own international society co-wrote

**Group · Published:** **Italian Cardiac Surgery Working Group**, *The Annals of Thoracic Surgery*,
**26 October 2026**. Titled an *"Expert Review of Evidence and Consensus Guidelines"*.

**The starting point is a guideline, and the recommendation at issue is specific:**

> The **2025 European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery Guidelines**
> introduced a **Class I recommendation for transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in
> selected patients aged 70 years or older** with tricuspid aortic stenosis and suitable anatomy, while
> **surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) remains recommended in younger low-risk patients.**

**What this document does with it:**

> Evidence suggests that **treatment effects are time-dependent: early non-inferiority of TAVI may attenuate
> with longer follow-up**, particularly in patients with substantial life expectancy. **Randomized durability
> data remain limited, whereas propensity-matched real-world registries have suggested possible higher late
> mortality after TAVI in younger, lower-risk populations.** Reintervention after a transcatheter-first
> strategy may be associated with increasing technical complexity and procedural risk over time.

**Conclusion:** transfemoral TAVI *"is often appropriate in patients with limited life expectancy or
recognized high surgical risk when anatomically feasible"*; **in patients with long expected survival,
"SAVR should retain a central role as an important reference strategy."**

**Interpretation, and there are three things here.**

**First, the structural observation.** **EACTS — the European cardiothoracic surgical society — co-authored
the Class I recommendation, and a national cardiac surgery working group is now publishing a narrower reading
of it.** That is not a scandal; national working groups exist partly to translate international guidance into
local practice, and a reference-strategy framing is a legitimate translation. **But it is the second time in
three days that this archive has recorded a guideline being argued with rather than implemented** — the
[26 October entry](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-26/) documented STS and APHRS inverting each other's
recommendation classes for postoperative atrial fibrillation. **Yesterday's entry then carried a four-society
document whose stated reason for existing was that society guidelines do not align.** **Three consecutive
entries on the same structural problem, from three different angles.**

**Second, the evidence inversion, which is defensible and should be stated as such.** **The case for restraint
rests on propensity-matched registries over randomised trials** — ordinarily the weaker evidence qualifying
the stronger. **Here it is the right way round, and the document explains why: the question is valve
durability and reintervention over decades, and no randomised trial has that follow-up.** *"Randomized
durability data remain limited"* is the operative sentence. **When the question is longer than the trials,
registries are not weaker evidence; they are the only evidence on the relevant timescale.** What a reader must
then hold onto is that **propensity matching cannot balance the reasons a 68-year-old was offered a
transcatheter valve rather than an operation**, and "possible higher late mortality" is correctly hedged.

**Third, and this is a genre note for the catalogue: the title claims more than the methods deliver.**
**It is called "Expert Review of Evidence and Consensus Guidelines", and its own methods section describes
"a narrative, evidence-based synthesis."** **No panel size, no voting, no consensus threshold, no statement
count, no grading** — none of the apparatus the word "consensus" implies. **A narrative review by a working
group is a worthwhile document and this one appears to be a careful one; it is not a consensus guideline, and
the title should not be read as if it were.**

**Archive context: aortic stenosis has not appeared in this archive's guidelines entries at all**, and TAVI
only as research — a durability review in the 10 September brief, a TAVI-versus-PCI timing trial on
1 September, and an age-based-recommendations critique in the 3 September entry. **This is the first time the
lifetime-management question has been reported here.**

[Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.athoracsur.2026.09.062) · *The Annals of Thoracic Surgery*

## 4. A position statement that says, in the abstract, that its evidence is not randomised

**Society · Published:** **Singapore Society of Anaesthesiologists**, *Singapore Medical Journal*,
**27 October 2026**.

**The premise is a number:** *"Healthcare accounts for 7% of Singapore's carbon emissions."* The statement
provides Singapore-specific recommendations for sustainable anaesthesia and intensive care practice, built by
a society workgroup reviewing **international sustainability guidelines, peer-reviewed literature and
evidence**, with **"iterative consensus building … through discussion and consultation"**.

**The sentence worth the entry is this one:**

> **Evidence was derived from life cycle analyses, laboratory or environmental studies, and quality improvement
> initiatives rather than conventional randomised controlled trials**, but can nevertheless provide a robust
> scientific basis for recommendations underpinned by the triple bottom line framework.

**Interpretation.** **Declaring the character of your evidence base in the abstract is good practice and this
archive should record it as such.** Most documents of this kind leave a reader to discover that the
recommendations rest on life-cycle analyses; this one leads with it. **And the claim that follows is argued
rather than asserted** — life-cycle analysis is the appropriate method for a question about emissions, and it
would be a category error to demand a randomised trial of a procurement decision. **The honest objection is
not to the evidence type but to the second half of the sentence: "can nevertheless provide a robust scientific
basis" is a general claim about a method, not an assessment of the particular studies used, and no certainty
is attached to any recommendation.**

**The scoping is careful.** The statement *"aims to support discussion, planning and implementation of
initiatives without superseding clinical judgement or local policies"*, and closes by calling on
anaesthetists *"to exercise leadership and stewardship to reduce emissions without compromising care."*
**That is a call to action rather than a recommendation set, and this archive flagged the same genre yesterday
in the four-society cardiovascular document.** **A call to action is not a recommendation, and a reader cannot
count what this statement recommends because no count is given.**

**Archive context: sustainability in anaesthesia and intensive care has not been reported here as a document
in its own right.** It appears once as a domain inside a larger guideline — one of the six domains of the
21 September entry's document — and otherwise not at all. **This is the first standalone sustainability
position statement in the archive, and the first document from a Singaporean society.**

[Read the position statement](https://doi.org/10.4103/singaporemedj.smj-2026-426) · *Singapore Medical Journal*
