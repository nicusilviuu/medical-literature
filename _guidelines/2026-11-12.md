## 1. PrECiSIOn — what a postoperative pulmonary complication is, and how severe each part of it is

**[Consensus on the definition, components, and grading of composite postoperative pulmonary complication
outcomes (PrECiSIOn)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-2600(26)00296-1)** · *The Lancet Respiratory Medicine* ·
**published 11 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Society · Published:** PrECiSIOn investigators — an international, mixed-method Delphi consensus.
**44 panellists and five patients or patient representatives.**

> Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are common after surgery and contribute substantially to
> morbidity, mortality, and health-care burden, and are frequently used as endpoints in clinical studies.
> However, absence of standardised definitions and heterogeneity in component selection limit comparability
> across studies and might bias estimates of treatment effects. … candidate PPC components were identified
> through a focused literature review, structured into domains, and evaluated across iterative Delphi rounds
> using a seven-point Likert scale, with consensus predefined as ≥75% agreement. … Consensus was achieved on
> a standardised PPC construct comprising components grouped into four domains: symptoms, diagnoses,
> therapies, and outcomes. Panellists supported the use of routinely collected clinical data and a
> standardised in-hospital monitoring approach but **did not reach consensus on the optimal follow-up period
> or need for post-discharge surveillance.** Severity-based rank ordering enabled hierarchical organisation
> of PPC components according to their clinical importance, supporting the development of ordinal composite
> outcomes that account for differences in severity between individual complications.

**Interpretation.** **This is the third document in three days to attack the same problem from a different
direction**, and the convergence is the story. On 10 November this archive recorded a core outcome set for
ventilation trials in acute brain injury that chose a **hierarchical** endpoint; today's literature brief
carries a *Critical Care Medicine* methods paper stating that the composite endpoints used in delirium
trials **"have not been rigorously evaluated"**, published in the same week as a trial using one of them. **In
all three the complaint is identical: a composite that sums dissimilar events treats them as exchangeable at
a rate nobody chose.** PrECiSIOn's answer is the same as the brain injury group's — **rank the components by
severity and build an ordinal composite** rather than a count.

**Two things to hold onto.** **The honest gap is stated: no consensus on the follow-up period or on
post-discharge surveillance.** That matters more than it sounds, because a PPC rate is a function of how
long you look, so two trials can adopt the whole PrECiSIOn construct and still not be comparable. **And the
patient involvement is real but small** — five patients and representatives against 44 panellists, with
their input gathered by interview and survey rather than by scoring.

**For the threshold catalogue: ≥75% agreement on a seven-point Likert scale.** That is the same numeric
threshold as the SMA-PV consensus and the same as the SCCM/ESICM definition below — **but see item 5, where
the same 75% is applied in both directions.**

[Read the consensus](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-2600(26)00296-1)

## 2. ESC — the fortnight after the cardiac care unit

**[Early ambulatory re-evaluation and follow-up of patients admitted in the acute cardiac care
unit](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjacc/zuag133)** · *European Heart Journal: Acute Cardiovascular Care* ·
**published 10 November 2026** · paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Society · Published:** **Association for Acute CardioVascular Care of the ESC** — a Clinical Consensus
Statement, the first document from a tracked society in this watch for several days.

> Early, structured ambulatory follow-up after hospitalization for acute cardiac events remains a key unmet
> need in cardiovascular care. … provides pragmatic, condition-specific guidance on post discharge management
> and (early) ambulatory re-evaluation for patients admitted to an acute cardiac care unit. **It complements
> existing disease-oriented guidelines by focusing on the vulnerable transition from hospital to home rather
> than on acute in-hospital treatment.** … general principles regarding timing, content, and organization of
> the first post-discharge contacts, including risk stratification, medication reconciliation, symptom
> assessment, evaluation of psychosocial status and comorbidities, patient and family education, and
> integration of cardiac rehabilitation. … how should the follow-up team look like, when should the first and
> subsequent visits be planned, what should be done at each contact, and how can multidisciplinary teams and
> hybrid (in-person plus remote) models be implemented according to local resources. **Supplementary material
> highlights major evidence gaps, including the optimal timing and intensity of follow-up**, the composition
> of ambulatory teams, the role of telemonitoring and digital health, and the needs of vulnerable
> populations.

**Interpretation.** **The document's own framing is the interesting part: it says it is filling a gap between
disease-oriented guidelines rather than competing with them.** Acute coronary syndrome, heart failure,
ventricular and atrial arrhythmias, conduction disorders, myocarditis and pericarditis each have guidelines
that stop at discharge; this one covers what happens next, across all of them.

**Two structural features worth recording.** **It is resource-scaled** — hybrid in-person plus remote models
"according to local resources" — which is now the fourth document in six weeks to make the recommendation
depend on the resource level rather than state one standard. **And it answers its own central question with
an evidence gap:** the optimal timing and intensity of follow-up, which is the thing a reader most wants a
number for, is listed in the supplementary material as unknown. **A consensus statement that names the
missing number instead of inventing one is behaving correctly, and the practical consequence is that "early"
is left to the clinician.**

[Read the consensus statement](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjacc/zuag133)

## 3. EXTRACCT adds abdominal blast injury — with a thinner method statement than its own TBI guideline

**[EXTRACCT Blast Injury Clinical Practice Guideline: Abdominal Blast
Injuries](https://doi.org/10.1002/wjs.70669)** · *World Journal of Surgery* · **published 10 November 2026** ·
paywalled; abstract quoted below

**Society · Published:** **Explosive Weapons Trauma Care Collective** — the same collaboration whose
**traumatic brain injury** guideline this archive reported on 7 October (`10.1002/wjs.70522`, 31 August
2026). **The series is publishing organ system by organ system.**

> This guideline … outlines best practices for managing abdominal blast injuries in **resource-limited
> environments**, especially after blast injuries from explosive ordnance including improvised explosive
> devices. … **A semi-structured literature review was conducted** to identify evidence-based practices for
> abdominal trauma care in low-resource environments. … key assessment points and management strategies …
> with interventions adapted to the severity of tissue and organ damage. It includes surgical techniques,
> imaging approaches, and adjunct therapies designed to support surgeons operating in low-resource settings
> … **Abdominal trauma following blast incidents can be managed effectively in austere settings by
> prioritizing resuscitation, hemorrhage control and bowel management.**

**Interpretation.** **The clinical content is sensible and the structural novelty is the one this archive
credited in October: the recommendation changes with the resource level rather than assuming one.**

**What has changed is the method statement, and it is worth flagging precisely because the series is the
control for itself.** **The August traumatic brain injury guideline described a targeted narrative review
supplemented by named military and trauma-system guidelines, developed "following the AGREE II framework".
Today's abdominal guideline describes "a semi-structured literature review" and names no appraisal
framework, no grading scheme and no consensus process.** This archive's standing rule is that a missing
grade distribution has at least two causes — withholding, or a genuine category difference — and **here
neither branch applies cleanly, because a sibling document from the same collaboration did state a
framework.** **So the honest reading is that the two documents in one series report their methods to
different standards, and a reader cannot tell from the abstracts whether the underlying processes differed
or only the reporting did.** It is a guideline worth having in an austere setting and a weaker document on
paper than its predecessor.

[Read the guideline](https://doi.org/10.1002/wjs.70669)

## 4. The ASRA fifth edition: a January 2025 guideline carrying a November 2026 index date

**[Regional anesthesia in the patient receiving antithrombotic or thrombolytic therapy: ASRA Evidence-Based
Guidelines (fifth edition)](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2024-105766)** · *Regional Anesthesia & Pain
Medicine* · **actually published 31 January 2025** · **index date 5 November 2026** · paywalled, and
**no abstract is deposited at all**

**This is the single most useful thing the watch turned up today, and it is not a new guideline.**

The sweep returned the fifth edition of the ASRA anticoagulation guideline — one of the most-used documents
in regional anaesthesia — with `firstPublicationDate` **5 November 2026**, print publication November 2026,
volume 51 issue 11. **Taken at face value, it is this week's major guideline. It is not: it was published on
31 January 2025, and the index itself proves it.**

| Evidence | Date | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The guideline's own index record | **2026-11-05** | What the sweep reported |
| Its DOI, `rapm-2024-105766` | **2024** | Submitted in 2024 |
| **A published *Response to* the fifth edition, by the same six authors** | **2025-06-08** | **The guideline existed by June 2025** |
| ASRA's own guideline page | **2025-01-31** | The real publication date |

**The internal proof is the row that matters.** `10.1136/rapm-2025-106881` is a Response to the fifth
edition "by Kopp *et al*", published 8 June 2025, authored by Kopp, Vandermeulen, McBane, Perlas, Leffert and
Horlocker — **the same six names as the guideline.** You cannot reply to a document that has not been
published, so **the guideline's true date is bounded above by 8 June 2025 using nothing but two records in
the same index.** ASRA's own page then fixes it at **31 January 2025**. **The gap between the index date and
the real date is about 21 months.**

**RULE, and a new verification route: a Response, Comment or Correction record dated EARLIER than its
target's own first-publication date bounds that target's real date, from inside the index.** This archive
already knew that index dates are restamped and that publication dates are not; what it did not have was a
way to catch a wrong date on a single record without leaving the index. **The title-stem sibling search —
built to recover missing abstracts — turns out to do this job too.** Today it stopped a 2025 guideline from
being published here as this week's news.

**A second, smaller instance of the same disorder appeared in the same sweep:** an *Intensive Care Medicine*
editorial on the refractory septic shock definition carries `firstPublicationDate` **1 May 2026**, while the
**Correction to that editorial** carries **1 April 2026** — a correction a month before the thing it
corrects. Print-versus-online dating again, and harmless here, but it is the same failure mode.

**On what the guideline says, I am reporting a secondary source and labelling it as one.** No abstract is
deposited for the record — zero characters — so the archive cannot quote the guideline's own summary. A
European Society of Regional Anaesthesia newsletter characterises the fifth edition as **moving from
"prophylactic"/"therapeutic" dosing language to "low dose"/"high dose", adding laboratory-monitoring
recommendations including drug-specific assays, updating P2Y12 inhibitor guidance, revising vitamin K
antagonist and direct oral anticoagulant reversal, and changing the timing of anticoagulant
discontinuation.** **That is a promotional secondary source, not the guideline, and none of it has been
verified against the document.** **A reader who needs the intervals must read the guideline itself.**

[Read the guideline](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2024-105766) ·
[the authors' 2025 reply](https://doi.org/10.1136/rapm-2025-106881)

**A companion case from the same journal and the same print issue:** the ASRA **gastric point-of-care
ultrasound** expert practice recommendations surfaced today as an **infographic**
(`10.1136/rapm-2025-106926`, 5 November 2026, no abstract). The parent narrative review
(`10.1136/rapm-2024-106346`) was published **6 July 2026**. **A companion infographic is a different
document from its parent, so this is not a duplicate — but it is not new guidance either.**

## 5. Two consensus definitions of refractory septic shock, five weeks apart — neither ever recorded here

**Both explicitly dated. Neither is new. Both are reported because this archive has never carried either,
and because definitions are the dominant theme of the fortnight.**

### SCCM and ESICM, 24 March 2026

**[Clinical Criteria for the Definition of Refractory Septic Shock: A Joint Delphi
Consensus](https://doi.org/10.1097/CCM.0000000000007124)** · *Critical Care Medicine*, co-published in
*Intensive Care Medicine* (`10.1007/s00134-026-08344-2`) · **both 24 March 2026** · paywalled

**Two tracked societies, 57 invitations and 56 participants, a five-round Delphi, 34 statements proposed, 5
rejected after round 2, and consensus reached on 13 of the 29 that remained.** The criteria:

> Markers of organ dysfunction (75%, 2 rounds), tissue perfusion (91.1%, 2 rounds) including lactate (94.6%,
> 2 rounds) and capillary refill time (76.8%, 2 rounds), assessment of fluid-responsiveness after initial
> resuscitation (92.9%, 5 rounds), and use of vasoactive drugs at norepinephrine equivalents greater than
> 0.5 µg/kg/min (75.0%, 3 rounds), were selected as clinical criteria of refractory septic shock. The use of
> critical care ultrasound (CCUS) (92.9%, 3 rounds) was the single diagnostic modality that reached a
> consensus-based agreement.
>
> Refractory septic shock is characterised by persistently elevated lactate concentrations and or prolonged
> capillary refill time in patients with septic shock who are fluid unresponsive, require a norepinephrine
> base equivalent dose greater than 0.5 micrograms per kilogram per minute, and undergo CCUS assessment when
> mixed shock is suspected.

**For the threshold catalogue, and this is a new kind:** consensus was **"at least 75% of panellists in
agreement OR DISAGREEMENT on the three highest or lowest levels of a 7-point Likert scale."** **A two-sided
threshold of this shape lets a statement reach consensus by being rejected** — which is why 5 of 34
statements could be thrown out after round 2 as a consensus result rather than an inconclusive one.
**Numerically 75%, like PrECiSIOn above; operationally a different instrument.** Sixth distinct threshold
scheme catalogued here, and still none of them comparable.

**Two observations on the content.** **Only 13 of 29 statements reached consensus — fewer than half, and
the panel's first agreed statement was that a comprehensive set of criteria is needed.** And **fluid
responsiveness took five rounds to reach 92.9%**, the slowest item to converge despite one of the highest
final figures — so the number alone does not tell you how contested an item was. **The round count is part
of the result and most reports drop it.**

**The co-publication deposits are near-identical but not byte-identical** (2,283 characters in *Critical Care
Medicine* against 2,275 in *Intensive Care Medicine*; the differences are a comma, a hyphen and
"µg/kg/min" spelled out). **That is the complement of the AHA/ACC case proved here on 11 November, where two
co-publications carried byte-identical deposits.** **Rule confirmed from both ends: compare co-publications
by hash, not by eye — and a near-match is as informative as an exact one.**

### A Spanish consensus five weeks later, 29 April 2026

**[Consensus statement on the definition, diagnostic criteria, and treatment of refractory septic shock using
a Delphi methodology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.medine.2026.502448)** · *Medicina Intensiva* ·
**29 April 2026** · **30 experts, consensus on 23 of 28 statements**

> …allowing RSS to be defined as a clinical entity characterized by **persistent hypotension and signs of
> global hypoperfusion for more than one hour, despite optimized initial treatment.** Consensus was reached
> on the use of **hydrocortisone**, the inclusion of elevated lactate as a marker of hypoperfusion, the
> utility of advanced hemodynamic monitoring and echocardiography, and general recommendations on the use of
> combined vasopressors were evaluated.

**Interpretation — read the two together, because they do not agree.** **The SCCM/ESICM definition is
pharmacological and physiological: a norepinephrine-equivalent dose above 0.5 µg/kg/min, fluid
unresponsiveness, lactate or capillary refill time, ultrasound when mixed shock is suspected. The Spanish
definition is temporal: persistent hypotension and global hypoperfusion for more than one hour despite
optimised treatment — no vasopressor dose threshold at all.** **A patient on 0.3 µg/kg/min who has been
hypotensive and hypoperfused for ninety minutes has refractory septic shock under one definition and not
under the other.**

**And the two panels agreed on different kinds of thing.** **The Spanish panel reached consensus on a
treatment — hydrocortisone — which the joint document does not address; the joint document reached consensus
on a diagnostic modality, critical care ultrasound, which the Spanish one lists among several.** **Agreement
rates were also very different: 23 of 28 statements against 13 of 29.** A panel that agrees on 82% of its
statements and a panel that agrees on 45% of its are not measuring the same uncertainty.

**This is the pattern the brief recorded this week from the opposite end**, where an analysis of 1.85 million
encounters found that antibiotic delay predicts death for two of seven sepsis time-zero criteria and not for
the other five. **Three independent documents, two consensus panels and one very large observational study,
all saying that sepsis is defined operationally rather than biologically — and all disagreeing about where
to put the thresholds.**

## 6. Fluid therapy terminology: a call for consensus that says it is not one

**[Revising Terminology and Phenotype Definitions for AI-Based Decision Support in Fluid Therapy: Call for
Consensus](https://doi.org/10.2196/103138)** · *Journal of Medical Internet Research* · **published
9 November 2026** · **open access** (PMC13747319)

> …its rational implementation is limited by inconsistent clinical terminology. As fluid therapy moves toward
> AI-empowered clinical decision support systems (AI-CDSS), there is a critical need for high-quality,
> verified training data. The reliability of these systems is currently threatened by misleading data
> resulting from ambiguous definitions and the interchangeable use of nonsynonymous terms, such as
> "dehydration" and "hypovolemia." … proposes solutions, including a shift toward adequacy-based
> phenotypes and categorizing blood and water volumes as "insufficient," "optimal," or "excessive"
> rather than relying on context-sensitive quantitative measures such as "low," "normal," or "high." …
> The proposed terminology and phenotype matrix are provisional: they represent the opinion of a
> multidisciplinary expert panel and have not undergone formal consensus, clinical validation, or testing
> within an AI system.

**The terms the panel singles out are "dehydration" and "hypovolemia", used interchangeably and not
synonymous; its proposal is adequacy-based phenotypes — "insufficient", "optimal" or "excessive" rather than
"low", "normal" or "high". And the disclaimer is the abstract's own last sentence: provisional, no formal
consensus, no clinical validation, never tested in an AI system.**

**Interpretation.** **The last sentence is why this is worth recording: the document disclaims its own
status in its abstract.** A reader who sees "expert panel" and "phenotype matrix" could easily file this as
guidance; the authors say plainly that it is an opinion piece, formally unvalidated and untested in any
system. **That is the honest version of what several documents in this archive have done less clearly, and
it earns the entry.**

**The substantive argument is narrow and good.** Calling a volume state "low" invites a question — low
relative to what? — that a machine learning label cannot carry, whereas "insufficient" names a relation to a
clinical objective. **Whether a panel can impose vocabulary on a literature is a different question, and
this one says it has not tried yet.** **It belongs on the watch rather than the brief because it proposes
how a field should define its terms, which is what a position paper does, and a definition document for a
condition managed in intensive care is in scope whatever specialty publishes it.**
