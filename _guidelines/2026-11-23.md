## 1. A tracked society that no index can see

**The index route produced nothing today.** Sixteen per-day collections over 16 to 23 November, all at
shortfall zero; 26 unique pubType records of which 1 survived the scope filter, 467 unique title-word
records, 28 in scope — **and the dedupe log built yesterday recognised all 28 as already reported.** Second
consecutive day of no new in-scope guidance from the index.

**The society route produced fifteen documents.** The ASA standards and practice parameters page carries
**fifteen statements dated 20 October 2026** — three new, eleven revised, one reaffirmed — and **none of the
fifteen is in this archive.**

### Why no sweep could have found them

**The ASA does not publish its statements to a journal.** They are documents on `asahq.org` with an
"Original Approval" date and a developing committee. **They have no DOI, no PMID and no journal of record,
so they are not in Europe PMC and never will be.** A search of Europe PMC for each of the three new
statements' distinctive title words returns unrelated papers and not the statements themselves.

**This archive runs two routes — a pubType sweep and a title-word sweep — and both operate on Europe PMC.**
**For a society whose output is web-published rather than journal-published, both routes have a yield of
exactly zero, permanently.** That is a different kind of blind spot from the ones recorded so far. The
17 and 18 November entries established that a document can be missed because the index does not *type* it as
guidance; the 21 November entry established that a whole journal series can deposit no abstract. **This is
stronger: there is nothing in the index to miss.**

### And the gap in this archive's own practice is narrower and more embarrassing than that

**The working ASA URL has been recorded here since 15 November.** The source notes read:
`https://www.asahq.org/standards-and-practice-parameters` works, where yesterday's constructed URL 404'd —
and
the outstanding list records the date it was checked. **The URL was found, recorded, and the page's items
were not read.**

**Recording where a page is and reading what it says are different actions, and only the first was done.**
The 20 October statements were already on that page on 15 November — the index footer says "Date of last
update: October 20, 2026" — so **five weeks of daily watches passed over fifteen documents that were sitting
at a URL this archive had written down.**

**NEW RULE: a society page is not checked until its ITEMS have been read and deduped. Recording that a URL
resolves is a note about the URL, not a check of the society.** The URL table started on 20 November now
needs a second column — the date the items were last read, not the date the URL was last confirmed.

**And a related finding about the page itself: it is the best standing index of the five tried today.** ASA
shows **per-item dates**, at day precision for the October batch and month precision for others, and labels
each item **New!**, **Revised** or **Reaffirmed**. **Of the five pages tried today it is the only one with
per-item dates.** EJCTS groups by year with no per-item date; SCCM lists only tentative year ranges for
upcoming documents; ESPEN returned an empty HTTP 202; IACTS returned HTTP 404. **The one society page that
can actually be diffed for new publications is the one that was recorded and not read.**

## 2. A scope-of-practice case that lists what its own candidates are not trained in

**[Statement on Cardiac Critical
Care](https://www.asahq.org/standards-and-practice-parameters/statement-on-cardiac-critical-care)** · ASA ·
**original approval 20 October 2026** · developed by the Ad Hoc Committee on Advancing
Anesthesiology-Based Cardiac Critical Care Medicine, reviewed by the Committee on Critical Care Medicine

> "...cardiothoracic anesthesiology and critical care medicine, are uniquely qualified to lead
> multidisciplinary cardiac ICU teams due to broad perioperative exposure, competencies, and procedural
> expertise."

**Interpretation — this is a professional-territory document and it is honest enough to be read as one.**
The claim is that anaesthesiologists trained in critical care, and especially those dual-trained in
cardiothoracic anaesthesiology and critical care, are **"uniquely qualified"** to lead cardiac ICU teams.
**There are no numbered recommendations, no thresholds and no evidence grading** — it is a position, and it
cites nine references including ACGME programme requirements and a 2025 AHA scientific statement.

**What makes it worth reading rather than discounting is that it argues against itself in the next
section.** Having claimed unique qualification, the document states that **"Impella insertion,
resternotomy for CALS, and fluoroscopy are not standard in anesthesiology training"**, and that
mechanical circulatory support, transoesophageal echocardiography and point-of-care ultrasound **"require
maintenance of skills"**. **A statement claiming a leadership role that then names the procedures its
candidates are not trained in has given the reader what they need to weigh the claim** — which is more than
most scope-of-practice documents do.

**The competency table is the usable part: nine domains** — neurological, cardiac, mechanical circulatory
support, pulmonary, renal, haematological, imaging, emergency, and leadership and systems — **split into
core competencies and advanced skills, with the advanced tier described as not yet mature in early
practice.** As a curriculum skeleton for a fellowship that does not formally exist, that is the document's
real contribution.

**And the obstacles section is unusually blunt for a society statement: "burnout, credentialing limitations,
reimbursement, as well as territory battles over patients, billing, and procedures."** Naming billing and
territory disputes in a clinical position statement is a recognition that the barrier to
anaesthesiologist-led cardiac critical care is not competency.

**Bound: there is nothing here to be right or wrong about empirically.** It is a claim about who should
lead, supported by a competency list and nine references. **The conclusion — that institutions using this
expertise "can expect" better continuity, efficiency, quality and safety — is an expectation, not a
finding, and no data are offered for it.**

## 3. A full dosing table, a 14% complication rate, and two weight bases

**[Statement on Remifentanil for Labor
Analgesia](https://www.asahq.org/standards-and-practice-parameters/statement-on-remifentanil-for-labor-analgesia)**
· ASA Committee on Obstetric Anesthesia · **original approval 20 October 2026, page last updated
17 November 2026**

> "...associated with maternal respiratory depression and oxygen desaturation, occuring in 14% of women; a
> three-fold increased risk compared to epidural... Dizziness occurs in 20% of patients receiving
> remifentanil compared to 1% with neuraxial analgesia."

**Interpretation — the document's purpose is to make a dangerous technique safer rather than to recommend
it, and it says so.** Remifentanil patient-controlled analgesia is framed as a high-risk option for
patients with a relative contraindication to epidural — bleeding disorder, anticoagulation, spinal hardware
— citing FDA guidance. **Severe obstructive sleep apnoea, baseline respiratory depression or sedation, and
concurrent sedating medication make a patient "not suitable".** Epidural analgesia is stated to be superior
to remifentanil PCA; remifentanil is superior to meperidine; versus fentanyl PCA the results are mixed.

**The 14% figure with a three-fold increase over epidural is the number that should travel with any
discussion of this technique**, and it is in a national-society document rather than buried in a
meta-analysis.

**The monitoring requirements amount to a statement that this cannot be done casually.** One-to-one nursing
with in-person respiratory observation; a dedicated intravenous line with a carrier infusion and the PCA on
the most proximal port; continuous monitoring for the first 30 minutes; respiratory rate, pain and sedation
scores every 30 minutes; blood pressure at least every 30 minutes; an anaesthesia practitioner available
24 hours a day; no additional opioids during the infusion; careful sedation assessment if opioids were
given in the preceding four hours. **Notification triggers are specific: sedation "not arousable to voice",
respiratory rate below 8 per minute, or SpO2 below 90% on room air.**

**And the document states the central contradiction of its own monitoring plan in consecutive sentences.**
Supplemental oxygen should be given to maintain SpO2 above 94% — **and then: "Supplemental oxygen may delay
recognition of apnea."** That is the real problem with pulse oximetry as the primary monitor for an
opioid-induced respiratory event, and **the statement names it rather than leaving the reader to discover
it**, which is why its hedge that continuous capnography "may increase the safety margin" reads as
under-stated rather than cautious.

**The dosing table is the operational content and it contains an inconsistency worth flagging.**
Preparation 20 mcg/mL (1 mg in 50 mL of 0.9% saline), pharmacy-prepared where possible; no basal rate and
no loading dose; **starting bolus 0.25 mcg/kg ideal body weight or 10 to 20 mcg; titration in increments of
0.05 to 0.1 mcg/kg; maximum bolus 0.5 mcg/kg or 50 mcg, and 70 mcg for patients with opioid use disorder;
lockout 2 to 3 minutes.**

**The starting bolus is specified per kilogram of IBW and the titration increment per kilogram of "IDW".**
That was verified against the page itself and not taken on report. **IDW is not a standard abbreviation, and
a dosing table that appears to switch weight basis between the starting dose and the increment is ambiguous
at exactly the point where it is used.** On a 90 kg patient with an ideal body weight of 60 kg, the two
readings of a 0.1 mcg/kg increment differ by 3 mcg on a maximum bolus of 50. **It is almost certainly a
typographical error for IBW, and almost certainly is not how a national-society dosing table should read.**

**One further detail with a dating consequence: the page's own "last update" is 17 November 2026, four weeks
after its original approval.** So this document was amended **six days ago**, inside this archive's coverage
window. **What changed is not shown, and the page offers no revision history.**

## 4. A replacement criterion, two block-height targets, and one sentence that matters most

**[Statement on the Management of Epidural Catheters for Cesarean
Delivery](https://www.asahq.org/standards-and-practice-parameters/statement-on-the-management-of-epidural-catheters-for-cesarean-delivery)**
· ASA Committee on Obstetric Anesthesia · **original approval 20 October 2026** · 23 references

**Interpretation — the document's value is that it commits to concrete criteria where most guidance on
failed epidural conversion does not.**

**The replacement criterion is explicit:** units should have protocols naming when to replace a poorly
functioning catheter, **"e.g., persistent inadequate analgesia after two physician-administered top-ups,
rather than prolonged troubleshooting without analgesic efficacy".** **Two top-ups is a number a labour
ward can act on**, and the phrase "rather than prolonged troubleshooting" is a direct criticism of common
practice.

**The block-testing passage gives two different targets from two different literatures and does not
reconcile them.** A stepwise approach: **straight leg raise for motor block, then sensory assessment with a
goal of T5 to light touch** — and then **"Other publications have recommended a T4 to pinprick may be
necessary"** for visceral manipulation. **Two modalities and two levels.** T5 to light touch and T4 to
pinprick are not the same test and not the same height, and a reader at the point of care has to choose.
**Naming both is more honest than picking one silently, and it leaves the decision where the evidence
leaves it.**

**The sentence that matters most is about general anaesthesia and it is unambiguous: "General anesthesia
should be offered, including prior to delivery of the fetus, whenever adequate surgical anesthesia cannot
be achieved or maintained."** **The clause "including prior to delivery of the fetus" is the whole point** —
the reluctance to convert before delivery is exactly where patients experience pain during caesarean
section, and a national society stating that the offer should be made anyway is the strongest line in the
document.

**The high-spinal warning is the practical counterweight to its own advice to replace a failed catheter in
theatre:** after a large-volume epidural bolus, **"subsequent full dose spinal carries a higher risk of high
spinal complication"**. So replacement is offered as an option and the specific hazard of the commonest
replacement is named in the same passage.

**Risk factors for failed conversion are listed and are conventional:** prolonged catheter use, higher body
mass index, difficult placement, multiple boluses, higher labour pain, urgency, chorioamnionitis.
**And the assessment advice contains a quiet criticism: perform frequent objective assessments and document
them, "since visual assessment may be inadequate."**

**Bound: no numbered recommendations, no evidence grading, no consensus method and no panel size.** **That
is the sixth consecutive guidance document in this archive to state none of the four**, and here it matters
less than usual because the document's claims are mostly procedural rather than comparative.

## 5. The other twelve, and the four pages that are not usable

**Twelve further ASA statements carry the 20 October 2026 date and are recorded here rather than summarised,
because they are revisions and reaffirmations whose changes the page does not show.** In this archive's
scope: **Statement on Neuraxial Analgesia or Anesthesia in Obstetrics** (revised), **Statement on Optimal
Goals for Anesthesia Care in Obstetrics** (revised), **Statement on Transesophageal Echocardiography**
(revised), and **Statement on Anesthetic Care During Interventional Pain Procedures for Adults**
(reaffirmed). Outside it: statements on burnout, well-being, fatigue, quality incentive programmes,
maternal peripartum racial and ethnic disparities, students in anaesthesia care, substance use disorder,
and third-party payor regulation.

**They are named, not read, and that is a deliberate limit: a "Revised" label with no revision history
cannot be reported as a change without reading the previous version, which the page does not provide.**
**Logged as an action rather than claimed as covered.**

**And four of the five pages tried today cannot be used as standing indexes, which is worth recording with
the reasons:**
