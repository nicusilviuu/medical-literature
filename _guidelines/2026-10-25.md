## The dated re-check failed, and the same day corrected this watch's method for handling that failure

**Today was the scheduled third check on the three abstract-free ISHLT documents** — the Baseline Lung
Allograft Dysfunction consensus, its companion perspective, and the frailty-assessment statement. **All three
still carry zero-length abstracts.** **And today, for the first time, every recovery route was tried and
exhausted:**

- **The index:** third check, still nothing.
- **A sibling record:** each has exactly one Europe PMC record, so there is no second deposit to read.
- **The PMC full-text route:** none has a PMCID and none is open access, so the route that rescued the SRLF
  atrial fibrillation guideline on 20 October does not exist for these.
- **The publisher:** the DOI resolves to Elsevier's linking hub, which returns a page whose entire content is
  the word "Redirecting" — the JavaScript wall already recorded for this publisher.

**So they are reported as unobtainable rather than rescheduled a fourth time.** This watch retired the ESPEN
"partially revised" diff on 18 October after seven weeks of the same pattern, and the same reasoning applies:
**three index checks and a publisher attempt with no route remaining is a finding, not a pending task.** They
move to a dormant list, to be revisited only if someone supplies a PDF.

**And then the same day produced the correction, which matters more than the failure.**

**Two AAST Critical Care Committee consensus guidelines on post-intensive care syndrome surfaced in today's
keyword sweep with zero-length abstracts in every record.** **Under this watch's standing practice they would
have gone straight onto the needs-a-PDF list — which now has nine entries and has been growing all month.**
**Both are open access with a PMC identifier, and both full texts came back on the first request: 156 and
165 kilobytes of XML, containing every question and every recommendation.**

**The lesson is specific and it changes how this watch should work.** **An abstract-free record is not an
unreadable document.** **The discriminator is not the abstract length — it is whether the record carries a
PMCID and an open-access flag.** **ISHLT failed today precisely because it has neither; these two succeeded
because they have both.** **That is a two-field check, and it should run on every abstract-free in-scope record
before it is written off.** **How many of the nine needs-a-PDF entries would survive it is now a question
worth answering, and it goes on the calendar.**

**One in-scope document today genuinely is unreadable, and the same check explains why.** **"Changes and
Modifications Included in the 2026 Acute Ischemic Stroke Guidelines Relevant to the Adult and Pediatric
Cardiac Surgery Populations", *Journal of Cardiothoracic and Vascular Anesthesia*, 27 September** — a
cardiothoracic-anaesthesia summary of what changed in a stroke guideline, which is both primary scope and the
delta-list format this watch praised yesterday. **One record, zero-length abstract, no PMCID, not open
access.** **It joins the needs-a-PDF list with its two-field check already done and failed.**

**Both sweeps ran.** The guideline-publication-type sweep returned **5 hits, none in scope** — hepatology,
two oncology documents, sleep medicine and ADHD. The title-keyword sweep returned **23 hits**, from which
today's items came. **Third consecutive day the publication-type sweep has found nothing in scope.**

## 1. AAST on post-intensive care syndrome, Part 2: mental health, caregivers, and money

**Society · Published:** **the AAST Critical Care Committee**, in *Trauma Surgery & Acute Care Open*,
**16 October 2026** — **nine days old**. **Six questions, six recommendations.** **Consensus defined a priori
as agreement among at least 80% of committee members, with any point failing to reach it recorded as "no
consensus" in the document.** **Reported from the full text, since no abstract exists.**

**The six questions are worth listing because they are not the ones a critical-care guideline usually asks:**
what patients and families are most concerned about after a prolonged ICU stay; which evaluations diagnose
mental health impairment; which patient and disease factors raise the risk; **what an intensivist can
actually do about it**; what the non-financial effects on families and caregivers are; and **what providers
can do about financial hardship.**

> **Patients with ICU stays >48 hours or those deemed high risk by the clinical team should be evaluated for
> psychological dysfunction associated with PICS: anxiety, depression, and trauma-related symptoms including
> PTSD. Standardized screening should start in the ICU and occur during care transitions** … **we recommend
> using validated tools such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) for anxiety and depression,
> and the Post-Traumatic Stress Syndrome 14-Question Inventory (PTSS-14) for trauma-related symptoms and PTSD
> during post-discharge recovery when PTSD-related symptoms can be more reliably assessed.**

**Interpretation.** **The screening recommendation is specific enough to implement tomorrow, which is rare in
this territory: a trigger (ICU stay over 48 hours), named instruments (HADS and PTSS-14), and a timing
rationale — PTSD symptoms assessed after discharge "when PTSD-related symptoms can be more reliably
assessed."** **That last clause is a measurement point and a good one: screening for post-traumatic stress
while the patient is still in the unit measures something other than post-traumatic stress.**

**The risk-factor recommendation contains two items that will surprise clinicians and are stated plainly.**
The biopsychosocial framework lists pre-ICU vulnerability including pre-existing psychiatric illness, baseline
cognitive impairment, **younger age, female sex**, prior trauma exposure and limited social support; and
in-ICU exposures including **delirium, deep or prolonged sedation, prolonged mechanical ventilation,
uncontrolled pain, ICU-related hallucinations or delusional memories, severe physiologic derangements such as
sepsis or shock, and sleep and environmental disruption.** **Younger age as a risk factor for psychological
sequelae of critical illness runs against the intuition that older patients fare worse** — and the in-ICU list
is almost entirely composed of things an intensivist controls.

**Which is what the fourth recommendation is about, and it is the most actionable thing in either part.**
**Reducing delirium and distressing ICU experiences while preserving orientation, mobility and continuity;
early mobilisation; systematic pain assessment and management consistent with the ICU Liberation ABCDEF
bundle.** **Nothing there is new as critical-care practice. What is new is the claim that doing it reduces
mental health morbidity months later**, which converts the ABCDEF bundle from a sedation-and-delirium
intervention into a psychiatric one.

**And the sixth recommendation is the one no other guideline this watch has reported would have made.**
**Patients at risk of financial hardship should be screened with structured multidomain assessments of cost
burden, employment impact, insurance status and access to resources — the AAST Health Economics Committee's
"Financial Five"** — with providers asked to mitigate preventable cost drivers through evidence-based ICU
practice and the ICU Liberation bundle, and hospitals asked to ensure access to financial counsellors.
**A consensus guideline that treats a patient's solvency as a clinical outcome, names a screening instrument
for it, and assigns responsibility to the hospital is doing something this archive's pathway thread has been
circling for a week.** **This is the sixth entry in that thread and the first where the cost falls on the
patient rather than the system.**

**The family recommendation completes it: anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, caregiver burden and
role strain, reduced quality of life, anticipatory and complicated grief, and disruption of family
dynamics** — with mitigation through a family-centred approach across transitions, routine screening for
distress, and **early integration of palliative care.**

**The limitation is in the Methods and the committee states it themselves, which is the most candid methods
statement this watch has recorded.** **"The methodology did not follow the iterative selection or appraisal
processes characteristic of a systematic review; instead, the search and selection strategies were determined
by individual authors."** **And: "each working group member appraised the quality and applicability of the
evidence at their discretion."** **So this is a targeted narrative review by subtopic, voted through at an 80%
threshold, and it says so in those words.** **Set against a month of documents that described GRADE and
withheld the distribution, a document that declines the systematic-review label outright is the more honest
failure mode.** **There are no evidence grades here at all — and given the methods, there should not be.**

## 2. AAST on post-intensive care syndrome, Part 1: a fourth domain, and the clinic

**Society · Published:** **the same committee**, same journal, **3 April 2026** — **six and a half months
old**, and found today only because the Part 2 full text cited it. **Six questions and recommendations, same
80% consensus threshold, same methods statement.** **Also abstract-free and also recovered in full.**

> **PICS is defined as a new physical, cognitive, or psychological impairment that develops and persists after
> ICU admission and hospital discharge. In addition to these domains, we recommend the explicit inclusion of
> persistent thromboinflammatory dysregulation and sustained organ dysfunction** due to recent evidence
> demonstrating persistent alterations in immunity associated with prolonged ICU stays and organ failure,
> **which are directly linked to post-discharge morbidity and mortality.**

**Interpretation.** **The definitional change is the substance: PICS has been a triad — physical, cognitive,
psychological — and this guideline adds a fourth, biological domain.** **Persistent thromboinflammatory
dysregulation and sustained organ dysfunction are not impairments the patient reports; they are measurable
derangements that persist after discharge.** **Adding them converts PICS from a syndrome defined by what the
patient cannot do into one that also has a laboratory signature** — which matters because it makes the
syndrome a target for mechanistic study rather than only for rehabilitation. **It also puts a surgical and
trauma committee's stamp on a construct largely developed in medical intensive care**, and the justification
given is specific: persistent immune alteration after prolonged ICU stay and organ failure, linked to
post-discharge mortality.

**The diagnostic recommendations are concrete and name the instruments, which is the practical value of the
document.** **For cognition: in-person Montreal Cognitive Assessment, with the Telephone Interview of
Cognitive Status as a validated remote alternative until an in-person MoCA is possible.** **For physical
impairment: evaluate changes in function and structure, activity limitations and participation restrictions —
and the committee states that no single test has been validated across all domains**, recommending an
individualised approach that accounts for the surgical cause of the ICU stay. **That is an honest admission in
place of a false standard, and it is the right one: the physical consequences of a trauma laparotomy and of
septic shock are not measured by the same instrument.**

**The clinic recommendations are the implementation half and they are unusually frank about feasibility.**
**A PICS clinic is defined as a specialised multidisciplinary outpatient programme covering four core domains —
physical, cognitive, mental health and social functioning — with a dedicated provider champion, ideally with
critical care and PICS expertise, plus nursing and physical therapy.** **And immediately: "we recognize that
implementation may not be feasible in all institutions because of resource and infrastructure
limitations."** **Referral: patients in critical care for more than 48 hours, or deemed high risk, evaluated
before discharge, with follow-up about two to four weeks after discharge.**

**The 48-hour threshold appears in both parts and is the operational core of the whole document.** **It is the
single number a unit would need to implement any of this, it is consistent across the two publications, and
it is a judgement rather than a derived cut-off** — nothing in either methods section suggests it came from a
risk model. **Worth knowing before building a service around it.**

**Taken together, the two parts split the syndrome across six months: Part 1 defines it, says how to diagnose
the physical and cognitive domains, and describes the clinic; Part 2 covers mental health, families and
money.** **Neither carries an abstract, so neither was findable by the normal route, and Part 1 would have
stayed invisible to this watch indefinitely if Part 2 had not cited it.**

## 3. ERS and ESRS on CPAP: one strong recommendation, and a category for genuine equipoise

**Society · Published:** **joint European Respiratory Society and European Sleep Research Society Task
Force**, in the *European Respiratory Journal*, **11 October 2026** — **fourteen days old**, carried from the
readable backlog. **Task Force of pulmonologists, cardiologists, respiratory sleep physicians, patient
representatives and methodologists.** **PICO questions, a systematic review, GRADE certainty for all
outcomes, and Evidence-to-Decision frameworks with discussion and voting.**

> The Task Force **generally suggests CPAP treatment for OSA because of its beneficial effects on sleepiness,
> other patient-reported outcomes and blood pressure (conditional recommendations). CPAP is recommended in
> patients with excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) (strong recommendation).** Additional specific conditional
> recommendations are given for patients with **moderate-to severe OSA, the elderly, and patients with
> arterial and treatment-resistant hypertension.** **The Task Force suggests either CPAP or no treatment in
> patients without EDS** (but taking into account other OSA-related symptoms), **in patients with atrial
> fibrillation with the aim of reducing its recurrence and in patients with impaired glucose metabolism to
> improve glycaemic control.**

**Interpretation.** **The structure is the finding, and it has three tiers where most guidelines have two.**
**One strong recommendation. A set of conditional ones. And a third category almost no guideline uses:
"either CPAP or no treatment" — explicit equipoise.** **That is a different statement from "insufficient
evidence." It says the evidence exists and does not favour either choice**, which is actionable in a way that
an evidence gap is not: a clinician can tell a patient that doing nothing is a defensible option and mean it.

**And the three places the Task Force puts equipoise are the three places enthusiasm ran furthest ahead of the
randomised evidence.** **Patients without excessive sleepiness; atrial fibrillation recurrence; glycaemic
control in impaired glucose metabolism.** **Those are precisely the prognostic indications that large
cardiovascular outcome trials failed to support** — and a guideline that declines to recommend CPAP for them
while strongly recommending it for sleepiness is the evidence catching up with the practice.

**Which produces the honest one-line summary of the document: CPAP is strongly recommended for how patients
feel, and conditionally at best for what happens to them.** The single strong recommendation is for excessive
daytime sleepiness — a symptom. **Blood pressure, hypertension subgroups, the elderly and moderate-to-severe
disease all get conditional recommendations; atrial fibrillation and glucose metabolism get equipoise.**
**A reader who takes away "CPAP works" has the right conclusion and the wrong reason.**

**Two structural credits.** **Patient representatives and methodologists are named as Task Force members** —
the second guideline in four days to disclose its composition in the abstract, after
[the Canadian spinal-procedures panel](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-22/) that seated provincial
funders. **And Evidence-to-Decision frameworks are named as the mechanism connecting evidence to
recommendation strength**, which is the step most GRADE-using documents leave implicit.

**It also agrees, independently, with the guideline this watch reported yesterday.**
**[The Indian Chest Society home non-invasive ventilation
guidelines](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-24/) recommended CPAP as the preferred initial treatment
for obesity hypoventilation syndrome with severe obstructive sleep apnoea.** **Two guidelines from different
continents and different specialties, four days apart in this archive, reaching the same position on CPAP's
primacy through separate consensus processes.** **Agreement reached independently is worth recording as
explicitly as disagreement.**

**The limitation is the one now familiar: GRADE certainty was assessed "for all outcomes" and the distribution
is not given.** **One strong recommendation among many conditional ones implies the certainty was mostly low
or moderate, but implication is not disclosure** — and a document that reports its framework, its voting
mechanism and its panel composition this carefully could have reported its grades too.

[Read the guideline](https://doi.org/10.1183/13993003.01186-2026) · *European Respiratory Journal*
