## 1. The check that could be defeated by duplicates

**The title-word sweep reported `hitCount=394 collected=395 SHORTFALL=-1`.** A negative shortfall is not a
thing the index can produce, and this is the first one this archive has recorded.

**The first explanation to reach for was wrong, and the test refuted it.** The obvious reading is that the
index grew during the run: the window's last day is today, the sweep took several minutes, and a record
first indexed mid-run would enter the result set after the hit count was read. **The hit count was re-read
immediately afterwards and is still 394.** The index did not grow.

**The real cause is in the helper, and the evidence was on the line above all along.** `epmc_all` prints two
lines:

```
collected 395 unique 394
epmc_all: hitCount=394 collected=395 SHORTFALL=-1
```

**395 records arrived across the pages, 394 were unique, and 394 is exactly the hit count.** One page was
fetched twice — the run took an HTTP 500 mid-paging and retried — the de-duplication removed the repeat
correctly, and **the data were never wrong at any point.**

**What is wrong is which number the check compares.** The shortfall is computed in the shell from the
running page-sum, while the de-duplicated count is computed in a separate Python block that prints it and
passes it nowhere. **The two numbers that have to be compared are printed one line apart and never meet.**

**The harmless direction is the one that fired today.** A duplicate inflates the page-sum, the shortfall
goes negative, and the anomaly is visible precisely because negative is impossible.

**The dangerous direction is the one that has never fired.** Suppose a sweep genuinely misses two records
and also fetches two duplicates. The page-sum reaches the hit count, **the shortfall reads zero, and the
unique count is quietly two short.** The check would report success on exactly the failure it exists to
detect — **the 5 November episode in which a sweep reported 107 records and returned 73, whose cause was
never identified and for which this check was the cause-agnostic answer.**

**Fixed today:** the unique count is written to a file and the shortfall computed from it, and the line now
reads `hitCount=N pages=N unique=N SHORTFALL=N` so that all three are visible together. **The tools README
said "always compare `collected` against `hitCount`" and the tool was comparing the wrong one of the two
things it printed.**

**The general lesson is not about this helper.** A check is a claim about a quantity, and the quantity it
actually reads can differ from the one it names — **and a check that reads the wrong quantity is worse than
no check, because it is trusted.** This one has been reporting shortfall on every sweep since 13 November.
**Every "shortfall zero" recorded in this archive before today should be read as "page-sum matched the hit
count", which is a weaker statement than it appeared to be.** In practice the unique counts were printed
alongside and did match on every run where the output survives, so no past sweep is known to be wrong —
**but the reassurance came from a line nobody was reading rather than from the check.**

**`10.1097/xcs.0000000000002293` — "Assessing the Evidence Base for the Association of Perioperative
Registered Nurses Guideline for Sterile Technique" — came out PRIMARY**, on the word `Guideline`. It is a
methodological review *of* a guideline and is as secondary as a document can be.

**That is the fifth instance in two days of the one fault**, after `standard` in "standard preoperative
fasting guidelines", `Guidelines` in "Impact of ... Guidelines", `guideline` in "guideline-defined", and
the bare-`statement` false positive recorded on 17 November. **It is not fixed today**, for the reason given
yesterday: the four deferred regex fixes are scored out-of-sample at the December month audit, and writing
a fifth fix against a fifth example found today would repeat the in-sample error that correction 15 is
about. **A fifth item is added to the December list: `ABOUT` needs "assessing the evidence base for".**

**What today does establish is the rate.** The bare-word fault has now produced a false positive on **two
consecutive days of fresh data**, which makes it the classifier's dominant failure mode rather than a
tail case — and that is an out-of-sample observation, unlike the 95.5%.

## 3. A guideline's citations measured against the outcome the guideline exists to prevent

**[Assessing the Evidence Base for the Association of Perioperative Registered Nurses Guideline for Sterile
Technique](https://doi.org/10.1097/xcs.0000000000002293)** · *Journal of the American College of Surgeons* ·
**published 17 November 2026** · methodological review of 268 references in Chapters 1–7 of the 2025 AORN
guideline

> "Among 268 cited references, 188 were primary studies. Only 38 (20.2%) evaluated postoperative SSI,
> whereas 106 (56.4%) assessed surrogate contamination and 57 (30.3%) evaluated other nonclinical outcomes;
> categories were not mutually exclusive. Among SSI-measuring studies, 23 (60.5%) reported at least one
> statistically significant infection-related difference. Only one randomized clinical trial demonstrated a
> statistically significant SSI reduction attributable to a sterile technique intervention. Potential
> conflicts of interest were disclosed in 30 primary studies (16.0%), including 17 with relationships
> plausibly relevant to the intervention or outcome evaluated."

**Interpretation.** This is a surrogate-endpoint audit applied to a guideline instead of to a trial, and it
is the same structure this archive recorded on 33 cardiac-surgery AKI trials over 22 years: **the short-term
surrogate produced the positive results and the patient-centred outcome produced the negatives.** Here
contamination is the creatinine and surgical site infection is the MAKE-90. **Fifty-six per cent of the
cited primary studies measure whether something got contaminated; twenty per cent measure whether a patient
got infected.**

**The arithmetic the abstract does not do is where the finding sharpens.** 23 of 38 is 60.5% as stated, but
**23 of the 188 primary studies is 12.2%** — and "at least one statistically significant infection-related
difference" is a far looser category than "an SSI reduction attributable to a sterile technique
intervention", **of which there is exactly one randomised trial.** The distance between 23 and 1 is the
paper, and both numbers are in the abstract without the comparison being drawn.

**The category overlap is declared and checks out:** 38 + 106 + 57 = 201 against 188 primary studies, which
is consistent with the stated non-exclusivity, and 38/188, 23/38 and 30/188 all reproduce their percentages.

**Two bounds, and the second is the one that keeps this from being an attack on the guideline.**

**First, this audits the citations, not the field.** A guideline can be sound and cite badly, or cite well
and recommend past its citations. The reference list is the right proxy for what the recommendations rest
on and it is a proxy.

**Second, and more important: sterile technique is a domain where the surrogate may be the only obtainable
evidence.** Patients cannot be randomised to non-sterile technique, so a shortage of SSI-endpoint trials is
partly a statement about what is ethically possible rather than about what the field neglected. **The
authors do not overreach on this. Their conclusion asks for guideline interpretation to "distinguish
contamination reduction from demonstrated SSI prevention" — a claim about how the evidence should be read,
not a call to withdraw the recommendations. That is the correct conclusion and a more disciplined one than
the title invites.**

**The conflict-of-interest finding is reported carefully and should be read carefully.** 30 of 188 studies
disclosed a potential conflict and **17 had relationships "plausibly relevant"** — the hedge is the
authors' own, the judgement is theirs, and 16.0% disclosure says as much about disclosure practice across
two decades of primary studies as about this guideline.

## 4. Non-adherence that looks protective, because the threshold is a severity marker

**[Real-world adherence to ERS/ATS recommendations for non-invasive ventilation in exacerbations of
COPD](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejim.2026.107269)** · *European Journal of Internal Medicine* · **published
17 November 2026** · prospective, nine Italian hospitals, 456 patients

> "Among 456 patients, 315 (69%) were guideline-adherent, 60 (13%) had NIV underuse, and 81 (18%) had NIV
> overuse. Underuse occurred predominantly in patients with mild respiratory acidosis receiving alternative
> respiratory support, mainly high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), whereas overuse occurred primarily in
> hypercapnic patients without respiratory acidosis. The composite clinical outcome occurred in 48 patients
> (11%) and did not differ across adherence groups. After adjustment, neither NIV underuse (odds ratio [OR]
> 0.85, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.33-2.20) nor overuse (OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.20-1.46) was associated with
> the composite clinical outcome, whereas higher Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II was
> independently associated with it (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.02-1.08)."

**Interpretation — both point estimates are below one, and that is the result to explain rather than the
null.** Underuse 0.85 and overuse 0.54 both point towards non-adherence being associated with *better*
outcomes. **The explanation is in the abstract's own description of the patterns, and the abstract stops one
step short of drawing it.** The ERS/ATS recommendation is keyed to pH. **Underuse happened in patients with
*mild* acidosis and overuse in patients *without* acidosis — so in both directions, "non-adherent" is
partly a label for "less acidotic", and the exposure is correlated with severity by the construction of the
threshold itself.** A guideline whose trigger is a severity marker cannot be audited for outcomes without
that confounding, and no amount of adjustment for SAPS II removes it, because pH is upstream of the
classification rather than a covariate alongside it.

**The intervals say the study cannot settle it either way.** Log widths of 1.90 for underuse and 1.99 for
overuse — roughly seven- and eight-fold spans — on **48 events divided across three groups.** The upper
bound for underuse is 2.20, so **a doubling of death or intubation from withholding NIV is entirely
compatible with this data set.** A null on 48 events is not evidence of no harm, and the conclusion's
wording ("not independently associated") is accurate while being easy to misread as reassurance.

**The same configuration as yesterday's FAERS paper, on consecutive days: the exposure of interest is null
and the severity covariate carries the only significant estimate.** SAPS II at OR 1.05 per point
(1.02–1.08) is the one thing this cohort establishes, and it establishes something everyone already knew.

**The genuinely useful finding is the shape of the underuse, and it is a finding about the recommendation
rather than about the clinicians.** Underuse was predominantly **high-flow nasal cannula given instead of
NIV in mild acidosis.** That is not a failure to follow guidance so much as **clinicians using a device the
pH thresholds were written before** — 13% of a prospective cohort treated with a modality the
recommendation does not describe. **An adherence audit that can name what the non-adherent arm did instead
has found a gap in the guideline, and this one can, because the authors classified deviations by direction
instead of as a binary.** Credit for that design choice: a binary adherent-or-not would have hidden both
patterns.

**Denominators check:** 315 + 60 + 81 = 456 exactly, and 48/456 = 10.5% against the stated 11%.
