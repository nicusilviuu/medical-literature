## 1. Inpatient CGM, insulin pumps and automated insulin delivery — and where it stops

**Published:** **simultaneously in *Diabetes Care* and *Diabetologia*, 22 September 2026** — **new
this week**
Olsen, Spanakis, Thabit, **Dhatariya**, Mustafa, Avari, Lumb, Dungan, Philis-Tsimikas, Kulasa,
O'Neal, Joshi, Liarakos and colleagues

> There is an **urgent and unmet need for a practical clinical guideline** addressing the use of
> continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), insulin pumps, and automated insulin delivery (AID) systems in
> hospital settings. The goal with this position statement is to facilitate effective and safe use of
> these devices… **in hospitals for non-critically ill hospitalized adults.**

Topics covered, quoted in full because the abstract is a topic list rather than a set of
recommendations:

> selection criteria for management with diabetes technologies, CGM-derived glycemic metrics and
> goals for adults with diabetes, insulin titration and administration guidelines, **perioperative
> care**, CGM alarm settings for hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, clinical staff training and
> workflows, integration of device data with the electronic health record, **imaging tests
> considerations**, and discharge planning.

**Interpretation.** A significant document for this project's scope, and the most interesting thing
about it is the boundary it draws.

**It is explicitly for non-critically ill hospitalised adults — so it stops at the ICU door.** That
matters this week in particular: a *Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology* study published on
**21 September** on the **accuracy of an interstitial CGM system in critically ill patients** was
picked up by this archive's sweep on 22 September and logged on the outstanding list — **seen but not
yet written up, so nothing is claimed here about its findings beyond its subject.** **The guidance covers the setting where
these devices work reasonably well and declines the setting where their accuracy is most in doubt** —
where vasopressors, oedema, hypoperfusion and hypothermia all attack the interstitial-to-blood
relationship the sensor depends on. That is a defensible scope decision, and it leaves intensivists
with a device their patients increasingly arrive wearing and no guidance on what to do with it.

**"Perioperative care" as a named topic makes this directly relevant here**, and it is the third
document in the seam this watch opened on 20 September. The archive now holds: the
[SPAQI SGLT2 consensus and the *Anaesthesia* multidisciplinary consensus on GLP-1, GIP and SGLT2
drugs](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-20/), and now inpatient glucose technology. **Kamlesh
Dhatariya is an author of both the *Anaesthesia* consensus and this one** — the same recurring
authorship pattern noted on [21 September](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/), where a small
group produces much of the guidance in this scope.

**Dual publication in *Diabetes Care* and *Diabetologia* on the same day** — the ADA and EASD
journals respectively — is how a document signals it intends to be the international standard rather
than a regional one. The two abstracts are identical but for British spelling.

*Access note: **the abstract contains no recommendations at all** — it is a statement of purpose and
a list of topics. This is now the routine finding for society documents in this archive, recorded for
ERC sections, AHA statements, ISHLT consensus documents, SECTCV/SEDAR, SPAQI and DAS 2025. **The
alarm settings, the perioperative section and the glycaemic targets are the document, and none of
them has been read here.***

## 2. Paraconduit herniation after oesophagectomy — the first expert consensus

**Published:** *Surgical Endoscopy*, **22 September 2026** — **new this week**
Gerdes, Schneider, Gero, Burri, Schoppmann, Popa, Gutschow and the **Paraconduit Hernia Collaborative
Group**

> The management of Paraconduit Herniation after Esophagectomy (PCH) is **controversial.** This survey
> aims to identify recommended strategies for prevention and patient handling… using the Delphi
> methodology.

Two rounds, 32 questions, web-based, on **non-revisional, elective PCH in asymptomatic patients**.
Items counted as *recommended* or *discouraged* at **>75% concordance**.

> **Eighty-two surgeons** with a median 17.5 years' experience from **19 countries across four
> continents** participated in both rounds (**response rate 43%**). The annual median individual and
> institutional caseload was **5 (IQR 5–5) and 5 (IQR 5–10) PCH surgeries**, respectively.

Where consensus was reached:

- **Prevention:** avoiding opening the left pleura during the index oesophagectomy
- **Indications for repair:** prioritising **biologically younger (<80 years) and tumour-free**
  patients, **complex multi-organ herniation**, and **radiographically enlarging PCH on
  surveillance** — while **explicitly discouraging repair for stable PCH or tumour recurrence**
- **Approach:** laparoscopic or robotic-assisted laparoscopy with **single-stitch suture hiatoplasty
  and gastropexy**
- **Mesh:** **biosynthetic absorbable mesh** for high-risk selective cases (recurrent PCH, weak crura,
  large hiatal area), **fixed with sutures**, with **tacks explicitly discouraged**

And where it was not:

> Many items… including **surgical measures to prevent PCH and most technical details of mesh
> augmentation (material, shape, and placement) did not reach positive or negative concordance.**

**Interpretation.** A careful document about a problem that is common after an operation few centres
do many of, and the caseload figure is the thing to notice.

**A median annual caseload of five per surgeon and five per institution is the most informative number
in the abstract.** The individual and institutional medians being identical implies that in the
typical participating centre, one surgeon does essentially all of them — and an IQR of 5 to 5 means
that value is tightly clustered. **This is a consensus assembled from 82 people who each manage this
problem about five times a year.** That is not a criticism of the method; it is the reason the method
was necessary. But it sets a ceiling on what the consensus can be: **pooled judgement from low-volume
experience, not distilled evidence.**

**The negative recommendations are the most useful part**, as they often are. **Discouraging repair
for stable PCH** — an asymptomatic radiological finding that is not growing — is a recommendation not
to operate, and those are rarer and harder-won than recommendations to operate. **Discouraging tacks**
for mesh fixation near the hiatus, adjacent to pericardium and aorta, is similarly concrete.

**The honesty about non-concordance is what makes the document credible.** That **prevention measures
and most mesh technical details reached no agreement in either direction** is stated plainly rather
than smoothed into weak recommendations — a contrast worth drawing with the
[videolaryngoscopy implementation guidelines](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-21/) covered on
Monday, which reached *strong consensus* while stating the evidence was of low quality. **Two ways of
being honest about a thin evidence base: report the disagreement, or report the agreement and warn
that it rests on opinion.**

**For this project's scope, the prevention item is the anaesthetic-facing one.** *Avoiding opening the
left pleura during the index oesophagectomy* is a decision made in theatre during the original
operation, with one-lung ventilation and a double-lumen tube in place — and it pairs with the
[STS oesophageal perforation consensus](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-09-18/) covered last week.

## 3. Healthcare Infection Society guidelines on operating theatre ventilation — which cannot be read

**Society · Published:** **Healthcare Infection Society**. *Journal of Hospital Infection*. The
**editorial announcing the guidelines** appeared **22 September 2026**

An editorial titled *"Healthcare Infection Society consensus guidelines on operating theatre
ventilation — a breath of fresh air"* appeared in the society's own journal this week. Operating
theatre ventilation is perioperative infrastructure — ultraclean air, laminar flow, air-change rates,
door discipline — and sits squarely in the space anaesthetists and surgeons share.

**The guidelines themselves cannot be retrieved.** A search of *Journal of Hospital Infection* for
ventilation papers since January 2025 returned eleven records, and a search on the society's name
returned three. **Every HIS document among them deposits no abstract:**

| Record | Date | Abstract |
|---|---|---|
| The editorial announcing the guidelines | 22 Sep 2026 | **none** |
| *"Microbiological factors in the design, validation and verification of operating theatre ventilation: Healthcare Infection…"* — apparently a component of the guidance | 17 Aug 2026 | **none** |
| *"Infection prevention and control in burns services: guidance from the Healthcare Infection Society"* | 8 Jul 2025 | **none** |

**Interpretation.** Recorded as a finding about reachability, not as a summary — because there is
nothing to summarise.

**A new publisher-level access pattern for the source notes: Healthcare Infection Society guidance
deposits no abstract in Europe PMC.** Three for three, across two different guidance documents and an
editorial, spanning fourteen months. This joins *Annals of Thoracic Surgery* invited commentaries and
*Anaesthesia* correspondence on the list of records not worth retrying — **but with an important
difference. Those are commentaries, where nothing much is lost. This is a consensus guideline, and
the entire document is invisible.**

**What this archive can say is therefore limited to this**: HIS has issued consensus guidelines on
operating theatre ventilation; they are new enough that their own journal is editorialising about
them this week; **and nothing about their content — not a single recommendation, not even a topic
list — is retrievable by the route this project uses.** The August paper on microbiological factors
in design, validation and verification is very likely a part of the same guidance, given its title,
**but that is an inference from the title and it deposits nothing either.**

**Logged as an open gap** rather than a closed one. It is the first document in this archive's history
that is known to exist, known to be in scope, and completely opaque. The route to it is the society's
own website rather than the literature index.
