## 1. ESOT on chronic lung allograft dysfunction — the late half of the question the ISHLT documents asked about the early half

**Society · Published:** **European Society for Organ Transplantation**, *Transplant International*,
**18 September 2026** — three weeks old. `Practice Guideline`. Multidisciplinary Task Force **including
patient and allied healthcare representation**, systematic reviews, **GRADE**, **eight PICO questions.**
Bos, Hellemons, Dave, Engel, Holm, Morlacchi, Müller, Pavanello, Picard, Rossi, Saez-Gimenez, Gottlieb, Vos

> **Chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) is the primary cause of late graft loss after lung
> transplantation, affecting up to half of lung transplant recipients within 5 years** and presenting with
> heterogeneous phenotypes, most commonly **bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) and restrictive allograft
> syndrome.** …
>
> **In terms of prevention, recommendations are in favor of using tacrolimus over cyclosporine, either
> mycophenolate or azathioprine, and azithromycin to reduce the risk of CLAD. Regarding treatment,
> montelukast could be considered in early-stage BOS. Current data are inconclusive in demonstrating benefits
> of anti-thymocyte globulin, alemtuzumab or extracorporeal photopheresis compared with standard of care, and
> do not support the use of antifibrotics in BOS.** The Task Force emphasizes the **urgent need for
> high-quality clinical trials and standardized care pathways.**

**Interpretation. Read this next to what this archive reported on [6 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-06/)
and the pairing is almost too neat.** Those were three ISHLT `Practice Guideline` records on **baseline lung
allograft dysfunction** — the failure of a transplanted lung to ever reach a normal functional baseline —
**and all three deposited zero characters of abstract**, so the entry could report their existence and
nothing else. **CLAD is the other end of the same timeline: the loss of a baseline once achieved.** Here the
guideline on the late problem **deposits its recommendations in full.** Same organ, same year, adjacent
questions, opposite deposit behaviour.

**The substance divides cleanly into what the evidence supports and what it does not, and the negative list
is longer.** In favour: **tacrolimus over cyclosporine**, **either** mycophenolate or azathioprine — a
recommendation that explicitly declines to choose — and **azithromycin**, which is the long-standing
observation that a macrolide reduces BOS risk. Against or unproven: **anti-thymocyte globulin, alemtuzumab,
extracorporeal photopheresis all inconclusive**, and **antifibrotics explicitly unsupported in BOS.**

**That last one is the most useful line in the document.** Antifibrotic drugs — pirfenidone and nintedanib —
are licensed for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and the inference that a fibrotic airway process after
transplantation should respond to them is mechanically plausible and widely acted on. **A GRADE-based task
force saying the data do not support it is a statement with direct prescribing consequences**, and it sits
alongside **montelukast "could be considered" in early BOS** — a weak recommendation for a cheap drug beside
no recommendation for expensive ones.

**Two structural notes.** **Eight PICO questions is a narrow frame for a condition affecting half of
recipients within five years**, and the Task Force says so in asking for trials and standardised pathways —
**the same closing sentence as every document in this archive's neurotrauma cluster.** And **patient and
allied healthcare representation on the panel** is worth recording: of the guideline documents catalogued
here since this watch began, the ones that name patient representation are a small minority, and the French
*conférence de consensus* jury with nurses and physiotherapists on it was singled out on 4 October for the
same reason.

**The lung-transplant cluster in this archive now has five pieces** — ISHLT on BLAD (consensus and
perspective, abstract-free), ISHLT on frailty in candidates (abstract-free), SPLF on indications and
contraindications (2022, queued), and **ESOT on CLAD (readable)** — plus the JHLT guidance on social support
in candidates still queued. **Candidate selection, early graft function, late graft function: the
documents exist for all three and only one of them can be read from its deposit.**

[Read the guideline](https://doi.org/10.3389/ti.2026.16959)

## 2. A disaster task force that converted 147 evidence statements into 22 suggestions

**Society · Published:** **Task Force for Mass Critical Care**, *Chest*, **15 September 2026** — three and a
half weeks old. `Practice Guideline`. **Three-round modified Delphi**, merging the **World Health
Organization** and **Guidelines International Network–McMaster** rapid-guideline frameworks, drawing on
peer-reviewed literature, **grey sources and firsthand experience.** From the backlog opened by yesterday's
re-sweep.
Persoff, Devereaux, Maves, Mehta, Burry, Chakravarti, Levy, Feldman, Baez, Felzer, Baum, Dries, Mukherjee,
Persson, Conrad, Hossain, Ghazipura, Anesi and colleagues

> **Background** … **Failures in these domains during COVID-19 contributed to disrupted care delivery,
> strained staff, resource shortages, and adverse patient outcomes.** …
>
> **Results** **A total of 147 evidence statements were evaluated, resulting in 22 suggestions** substantiated
> by other supporting literature and anecdotal experiences. The TFMCC identified **transparent, timely, and
> scientifically grounded public communication with proactive engagement through social media as
> indispensable** for information dissemination and **misinformation mitigation.** Planning and coordination
> across federal, state, regional, and hospital systems are critical… depends on **standardized crisis care
> frameworks, Medical Operations Coordination Centers, and interoperable health information systems.**
> Important findings include community engagement, equitable resource allocation, accessible communication
> strategies, and **processes to avoid restrictive visitation policies that can impede surrogate
> decision-making.** Finally, **integrating emergency department expertise into hospital incident command** and
> strengthening the **"last mile" of supply-chain distribution** are necessary…

**Interpretation. Start with the word the document chose: suggestions, not recommendations.** **147 evidence
statements were evaluated and 22 suggestions came out — fifteen per cent** — and the output is labelled with
the weaker word throughout. **That is a task force declining to call consensus-plus-anecdote a
recommendation**, and it is the right call for a field where the evidence base is one pandemic and a set of
after-action reviews. **Compare the Brain Trauma Foundation documents reported on
[7 October](/medical-literature/guidelines/2026-10-07/), which separated an evidence-based guideline from a
consensus algorithm companion into two papers.** Both solve the same problem — distinguishing what is known
from what is agreed — and this one solves it with vocabulary rather than structure.

**The visitation line is the sharpest thing in the document and it is a retrospective verdict.**
*"Processes to avoid restrictive visitation policies that can impede surrogate decision-making"* — a task
force reviewing the pandemic **recommending against one of that pandemic's most universal interventions**, on
the ground that it broke surrogate decision-making. **Restrictive visitation was implemented nearly
everywhere, for infection-control reasons that were real**, and the harm being named here is not infectious
but procedural: a patient without capacity needs a surrogate physically present to be a surrogate. **This
archive has been tracking code-status and surrogate decisions since 11 September, and this is the first
guideline encountered that treats visitation as a decision-making problem rather than an infection-control
one.**

**Two other items are operational and easy to skip over.** **"Integrating emergency department expertise into
hospital incident command"** — the people who run the front door were absent from the body that made the
decisions, which is a specific, auditable organisational failure. And **"the last mile of supply-chain
distribution"**, which is where personal protective equipment failures actually happened: not national
shortage, but the final step from loading dock to ward.

**Where it sits against the human-factors thread.** This archive's human-factors documents are all about
**team communication inside a resuscitation** — closed-loop communication, time-outs, debriefing. **This is
the system-level analogue: communication between hospitals, with the public, and with regulators**, and it
has the same evidentiary shape — **guidance built on mechanism and consensus, ahead of outcome evidence.** The
[6 October brief](/medical-literature/brief/2026-10-06/) found that four human-factors guidance documents
rested on one meta-analysis whose signal did not survive randomisation. **Here there is no randomised
evidence at all, and the document says so by calling its output suggestions.**

[Read the consensus report](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2026.08.049)

## 3. A society telling patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy to exercise

**Society · Published:** **Brazilian Society of Cardiology**, *Arquivos Brasileiros de Cardiologia*,
**21 September 2026** — two and a half weeks old. Indexed as **both `Systematic Review` and
`Practice Guideline`.** PubMed, Embase and Cochrane **through December 2024**; **RoB 2** and **ROBINS-I**;
**GRADE**. **The only new in-scope document from today's 21–30 September re-sweep.** Secondary scope —
cardiology.
Stein, Alô, Ritt, Fagundes, Silveira, Herdy, Colombo, Pisani, Braga, Nunes, Sacilotto, Silva, Milani,
Pimentel, Campos, Nunes, Nascimento and colleagues

> **Background** **Although historically discouraged due to the risk of arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death
> (SCD), physical exercise (PE) may be safe and beneficial** for individuals with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
> (HCM). …
>
> **Results** **Eight studies (four RCTs and four observational studies; 9,744 participants)** were included.
> **None reported an increased risk of mortality or severe arrhythmic events associated with PE, even at
> moderate or vigorous intensities. Syncope was rare.** Additionally, **a Korean observational study involving
> more than 7,000 participants** (mean age 59) demonstrated **reduced all-cause mortality with higher physical
> activity intensity (dose-response curve). In low-risk athletes, competitive sports were not associated with
> severe adverse events. The quality of evidence ranged from moderate to very low.**
>
> **Conclusion** **PE in HCM should be encouraged based on individualized assessment.** In patients without
> high-risk criteria for SCD, **benefits appear to outweigh risks.** For higher-risk subgroups,
> **recommendations are more cautious and based on expert consensus.**

**Interpretation. A guideline reversing a prohibition is a different object from a guideline adding a
treatment, and it needs reading more carefully, not less.** Exercise restriction in hypertrophic
cardiomyopathy has been near-absolute for decades, so **"should be encouraged" is a large move made on
evidence the panel itself grades from moderate to very low.** The panel is not hiding that: it stratifies,
recommending encouragement **only** for patients without high-risk criteria for sudden death and falling back
to expert consensus above that line.

**The arithmetic is where the caution belongs.** **9,744 participants across eight studies, of which more
than 7,000 are in a single Korean observational cohort** — so **the four randomised trials and three other
observational studies share fewer than 2,744 participants between them.** The headline participant count is
one cohort with a tail. And **that cohort is the source of the dose-response mortality finding**, which is
the most persuasive-sounding result in the abstract and the one most exposed to confounding: **people who
exercise more are different from people who exercise less in every way that matters, and a mean age of 59
in an HCM cohort selects for survivors.**

**"None reported an increased risk" is an absence-of-signal statement, and the denominators decide what it is
worth.** Sudden cardiac death in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is an uncommon annual event — I am not quoting a
rate I have not verified here — and **in a few thousand person-years of mostly low-risk patients, a real
excess risk of exercise could hide comfortably inside "none reported."** The honest reading is that **these studies exclude a large effect
in selected patients and say nothing about a small one, or about the patients who were excluded.**

**What makes the document defensible anyway is the structure of the recommendation rather than the strength
of the evidence.** It is **conditional on individualised risk assessment**, it **names the subgroup the
evidence covers** (no high-risk criteria for sudden death), and it **says plainly that the rest is expert
consensus.** **Set against the Chinese lung-biopsy consensus reported on 6 October — five strong
recommendations resting on level 4 evidence, without comment — this is how to carry weak evidence into a
guideline.**

**One methodological note worth recording.** It is indexed as **both a systematic review and a practice
guideline**, which means **the panel graded its own review and then wrote recommendations from it.** That is
efficient and transparent — the evidence and the recommendation are in one document, auditable together —
**but there is no independent appraisal step**, and the GRADE judgements and the recommendation strength were
made by the same people.

[Read the recommendations](https://doi.org/10.36660/abc.20260423)
